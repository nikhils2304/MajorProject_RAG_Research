General Idea

Proverbs are prevalent in almost all languages of the world. These proverbs mainly carry the mark of ancient oral traditions. Due to the ancient tradition, the specific origin of the proverbs cannot be identified, nor is the name of any particular individual associated with the collection of proverbs. It can be said that, like rhymes, proverbs are created by the community or the common people of ancient times. The size of a proverb is usually limited to one or two lines. Within this limited expression, such brilliance of life experience and life evaluation is revealed that it is accepted as a permanent asset of the language. In a way, a proverb is an excellent folk saying. In the proverbs prevalent in Bengali language, an extraordinary expression of wisdom is easily noticeable. Proverbs have achieved universality on the one hand, and on the other hand, they contain many details of the Bengali culture of many ages. Local environment, customs, life perspective, values, or সংস্কার-বিচার (a type of traditional court) are the main basis of proverbs. Even as times change, there is no change in these expressions. "If you don't know how to dance, the courtyard is crooked" can never be transformed into "If you don't know how to dance, the stage is crooked" in a changed time and place.

The Value of Proverbs

Proverbs are not the creation of a single individual, but the collective creation of community life. Many scholars believe that the main creator of the proverbs is the women of Bengal. The reason for this assumption is that the special experiences of the inner quarters or the detailed descriptions of everyday life that are found in the proverbs are closely related to the household of women's lives. Whether created by women or men, the philosophical discussions in simple language that are observed in proverbs must be duly praised. Proverbs mainly operate on two levels. The first is its literal meaning. In the second level, transcending that literal meaning, the proverb illuminates an eternal truth of life. According to researchers, in proverbs, folk sayings have entered the thoughts of the wise, and wise sayings have spread through individuals into collective life, into everyday language.

The proverbs are not only reliable reflections of reality but are also exceedingly memorable for their philosophical depth. In later times, many poets, writers, and musicians also found many wise sayings in their works which are accepted as proverbs. In this context, the names of Bharat Chandra, Bhola Moira, Bankim Chandra, Rabindranath Tagore, Mukundadas, Nazrul Islam, Atulprasad Sen, etc. are noteworthy.

Examples of Proverbs

1. Rub the palm, it smells sweet; rub the lemon, it becomes bitter. (Gaining results according to the field.)

2. An empty vessel makes much noise. (The boasting of a person without substance.)

3. The rice gruel runs out when trying to buy salt. (The unbearable state of poverty.)

4. Too many cooks spoil the broth. (Work is ruined by too many people.)

5. The job suits the one who is skilled, others get hit by a stick. (It is easier for the one who is accustomed to doing the job.)

6. The wealth belongs not to the owner, Nepo steals the yogurt. (An unworthy person appropriates the wealth of the rich.)

7. A local yogi receives no alms. (Close acquaintances do not value the qualities of a person.)

8. When kings fight, the Dolukhagra (a type of reed) dies. (In the conflict of the powerful, the life of the common man is destroyed.)

9. A saint when there is work, a rogue when the work is done. (Depriving one of wages after getting work done and defaming them.)

10. An empty barn is better than a wicked cow. (It is better to be without friends than to be friends with a wicked person.)

11. The ant grows wings to die. (Excessive activity before a fall.)

12. No rice at home, but a three-hand-long dhoti. (Criticism of external pomposity.)

13. Eating and sleeping in the middle of work. (A mockery of a lazy lifestyle.)

14. Playing the same tune. (Monotonous repetition)

15. Like chalk and cheese. (Completely opposite)

16. A burnt child dreads the fire. (To fear based on past experience.)

17. Can't hold a Hela snake, but goes to catch a Kēutē. (Trying to do work without understanding the limits of one's abilities.)

18. If I kill, I'll kill a rhinoceros; if I loot, I'll loot a storehouse. (Planning big events)

19. Fire in the market does not spare the saint's house. (Bad times do not discriminate between good and bad people.)

20. It takes two to tango. (A quarrel arises from the fault of both parties.)

* Use the above proverbs meaningfully in sentences.
