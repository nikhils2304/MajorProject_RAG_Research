It is said – ink, pen, and mind, three write. But where is the pen? The place I work is a writing office. Everyone here is a writer. But no one has a pen except me. Everyone has a glass screen or curtain in front of them, like a smooth mirror. And beneath it, like typewriters, is a keyboard with a letter printed on each button. The writers are constantly writing with it, occasionally stopping to look at the screen. Whatever has been written is displayed on the screen. They lovingly prepare my writing for printing as well. I am in trouble if I forget to bring a pen one day. --A pen! No one has a pen. If someone does find a dry, blunt-tipped one, I don't enjoy writing with it. I just get the work done somehow for the day. Yet our office is said to be a writing office, a writer's factory. There used to be a saying in Bengali, "No ink, no pen, yet I call myself a scribe!" By the grace of time, perhaps we are the same today.

50

I am a village boy. Those who are fifty or sixty years old might understand what our relationship with pens was like back then. We used to make pens by cutting thin bamboo strips. The difficult part was splitting the tip of the pen. The elders taught us that a pen shouldn't just be sharp; the tip should be carefully trimmed so that the ink doesn't drip all at once. Where would we write? Not on writing slates, but on banana leaves cut like paper. We would take bundles of them to school. The teachers would understand and discreetly tear off a piece for each student, and we would do our "homework" on it. Banana leaves had to be split. Only then would the ink flow slowly. That's why in our childhood we had banana leaves. After the lesson, we would throw them into the nearby pond on the way home. People call someone illiterate if they have no knowledge of letters, like "ox-meat to an ox." We used to make our own ink too.

"Sesame, myrobalan, silk-cotton bark/Mix with goat's milk/Rub in an iron pot/Then the ink is good." This was the prescription. Our mothers, aunts, and sisters also helped. Preparing ink involved so much effort. If it fell and a cow ate it, it was considered inauspicious. Throwing ink was also a sin.

The Chinese used to say – "The pen writes without tearing the paper, and the ink does not drip." Where will we find such a good scribe? Our simple method of making ink was different. After cooking at home on a wooden stove, a lot of soot would accumulate on the bottom of the pot.

51

We would scrape that off with a spatula and put it in water in a stone bowl. Expert calligraphers would rub haritaki (myrobalan) in that black water. Sometimes we had to have our mothers roast and grind sun-dried rice to mix in it. After mixing everything well, we would heat the sharp end of a ladle until it was red-hot and dip it into the water. The water would bubble a lot because it was so little, and that’s how our ink was made. When I think of those first writing attempts, it does pain me a bit! Then, after straining it through cloth, we would write on banana leaves with our homemade ink and pen. So many years later, when I think about losing that pen, I wonder, what if I had been born before Jesus Christ? What if instead of being born in India, I had been born in ancient Egypt? What if I were not Bengali but an ancient Sumerian or Phoenician? Then perhaps I would have broken off a reed from the banks of the Nile, blunted it, and written with it. Perhaps I would have sharpened it into a pen. Perhaps, as a Phoenician, I would have brought a bone from the edge of the forest – that would be my pen. Even if I were the emperor of Rome, even if I were Julius Caesar himself, my best artisans could only produce a bronze stylus, whose sharp point...

52

...was nothing more than a sharp bronze needle. Caesar, besides using this stylus to stab Casca, up until recently...

Whether it's a reed, a bird's feather, or bronze—call it a stylus or a skewer. The pen could even be a murder weapon that day. The Chinese, of course, have always written. The world’s stylus of knowledge was truly sharp. Even now, the students in school probably have fancy pens. Perhaps, even in rural areas, bamboo strips for pens are no longer found. Reed pens are only seen during Saraswati Puja. Milk instead of ink in glass inkwells. Reed pens instead of fountain pens or ballpoint pens. Feather pens are no longer seen either. Their English name is "quill." Lord Curzon used to tell Bengali journalists after seeing their hot English – "Babu quill drivers." Now, to see a feather pen, there's no other way but to look at old paintings or photographs. William Jones or Carey Sahib is depicted in front of their inkwells, with their quill-pen-wielding scribes. The British even invented a device for cutting these feathers into pens, similar to a pencil sharpener. It also had a sharp blade. Thus, the pen became obsolete.

53

Even the sight of an inkwell-pen is a distant dream. In some offices, ballpoint pens are arranged on the table. But those are merely for show, and they are dusty. Some say they are dot-pens. In Kolkata's Chowringhee, pen sellers are everywhere. Ten pens in one hand, pens in their pockets. Not just in pockets, though! Philosophers, I would say—those who search the world with a pen tucked behind their ears. In childhood, I had seen a doorkeeper who always kept a pen tucked in his socks. Nowadays, ultra-modern boys have pens arranged not in boots but in small pockets on their shoulders. Some even carry pens in their hair buns. The other day, as a female passenger was getting off a tram, someone shouted, "Hey, your pen is exploding!" Pen explosion. One time it was said, "Kayasthas are identified by their pens, Rajputs by their moustaches." Now, neither the pen nor the moustache belongs particularly to anyone. "Those who worry about how many letters are in the pen are illiterate. They read the Chandi at Kalighat." We only know that even if everyone in the country is not literate, the pen is now universal. The truth is, pens have become so cheap and so universally accessible that even pickpockets no longer use them for their tricks.

54

Scholars say that what truly revolutionized the world of pens was the fountain pen. At one time in Bengal, it was called a “reservoir pen.” For them, the nib pen is now untouchable. Waterman greatly improved it. The idea of the unfailing inkwell came from Rabindranath Tagore. Like many businessmen of that era, he used to go to work with his inkwell and pen. Once, he had gone to sign a contract with a businessman. Part of the document had been written when suddenly the inkwell overturned on the paper. He rushed off to find more ink. When he returned, he learned that another eager businessman had already completed the signing and finalized the contract. Distressed, Waterman made a vow in his mind—no more! He had to find a solution. The fountain pen was born.

I remember buying my first fountain pen. It was a few years after World War II. I went to a famous store in College Street to buy a fountain pen, they said. The shopkeeper asked, "What kind?" I was stumped. He rattled off, "Parker? Sheaffer? Waterman? Swan? Pilot?" He immediately understood my financial situation. "Well, I have a cheap Pilot. Japanese. Great!" As he said this, he removed the cap and threw the pen onto the table towards a board on one side of the bill, the gesture just like a circus performer throwing a knife at a living person. To my amazement, the pen stuck into the board.

55

He took the pen off the board and showed it to me, "See? The nib is fine." He wrote a line or two and gave it to me. That day, I went home with that magic Pilot. Later, I had many more famous and varied fountain pens. But I kept that Japanese Pilot alive for a long time.

One danger of the fountain pen is that it makes the writer addicted—if they are a wealthy writer, of course. The famous writer Shailajananda once showed me his collection of fountain pens – there must have been two dozen of them. There were several kinds of Parkers. Shailajananda said, "I got this addiction from Sharatchandra Chatterjee." He, too, had a passion for fountain pens. Waterman improved upon what Sharatchandra had. I took up the fountain pen after entering high school in the city. The practice of making ink also stopped then. I was a devotee of ink and pen. That is, I gave a holiday to the bamboo or reed pens of ink and nib.

We used to make ink in glass inkwells with ink tablets or pellets. Two types were available: red and blue. Of course, there were also ready-made inks available in inkwells and bottles. They had fancy names, like Kajal Kali, Sulekha, etc. Foreign inks were also available. However, the nibs and handles were different for fountain pens. I wanted a pointed nib. Yes, improved types of nibs were also available abroad at one time. These were made from cow horn or tortoise shell. Very durable. Diamonds were then fitted onto the tips of quill pens. The tips of fountain pens often became blunt, so this was the solution. Sometimes, they were covered with platinum, gold—making them even more expensive and durable. By doing all this, different types of cheap and expensive fountain pens were released on the market, gradually replacing inkwells and pens. When we were in college, almost every student had a fountain pen in their pocket. Reed pens, khag pens, feather pens—all vanished.

56

At the same time, from educated homes or offices and court tables, disappeared the pair of inkwells—those were just for decoration. The inkstand was like a small institution.

At one time, writing was dried with blotting-sand, then with blotting-paper. All in all, without seeing how many kinds of inkwells there could be, it is difficult to believe. Glass, cut-glass, porcelain, stone, jade, brass, bronze, sheep's horn, even gold! If someone in a village passed a couple of exams, the old people would bless them – "Live long, my child, may you have a golden inkwell." I learned that there really was a golden inkwell when I went to see Subho Tagore's famous inkwell collection.

57

Looking at all those inkwells, I was amazed to imagine that so many immortal works by Shakespeare, Dante, Milton, Kalidas, Bhababhuti, Kashiram Das, Krittibas—from Rabindranath, Bankimchandra, Sharatchandra—had all been written with ink from these inkwells. Alas, where have all those days gone?

In fountain pen advertisements, I was watching a stenographer's pride in his pen. Fountain pens are no less—I was seeing that their collection had seven hundred nibs! They have separate nibs for copywriters, those who practice singing, stenographers, those who write with their left hands—in short, separate nibs for every type of writer. The machine age is designed to fulfill everyone's demands. Yes, money!

The price of a diamond-studded, gold-bodied, diamond-hearted fountain pen has been set at two and a half thousand pounds (a pound equals eighty rupees!). After calculating, how much will the price of the pen be? Amazing! All this is just to please the millionaires.

58

It's as if the computer has vowed to send them to their museums. As a result, the pen, like handwriting, is on the verge of extinction. Is the day of the calligrapher over forever, then? Maybe.

Those who feel threatened by the pen being taken from the hands of people – what else is left? Bankimchandra wrote, "The stick is your companion, but the pen has a permanent place in history." The craftsmen who used to write beautiful letters with reed pens were called "calligraphists." In the Mughal court, how much respect and honor they received!

59

Not just the Mughals, but in all courts around the world. In our Bengal too, kings and landlords used to consider the skilled calligraphers as virtuous and made provisions for their maintenance. Ordinary householders would call in calligraphers to copy manuscripts. If you look at a manuscript, it is pleasing to the eye. “Samani sama shirshani ghanani viralani vichitrani, sankhal paribichhanna." All the lines are straight and clean. Each person has what you could call—pearl-like handwriting. Yet, how insignificant were their earnings!

One writer in the eighteenth century received thirty thousand takas, some cloth, and some sweets for copying four volumes of the Ramayana. A Sahib has written that in the nineteenth century, twelve annas or one rupee was worth so much! How much glory there was around it, and how much pride a calligrapher took in it! Many manuscripts have this warning—Beware! No one should try to steal this manuscript.

The pen is said to be mightier than the sword. The fountain pen also perhaps hints at the same. Because, in its accessories, there are words like "barrel," "cartridge." But occasionally the smell of gunpowder is heard.

60

In history, there are also stories of such “duels.”

So, when I see that even amidst so many changes, someone is clinging to a pen, it feels good. It also feels good to think that most writers of our time still write with a pen. Many of them did not ever type. Annadashankar Roy is the only one who has consistently written by typewriter. For the past few years, poet Subhash Mukhopadhyay has been using a typewriter. Almost everyone else writes with a pen. Of course, either a fountain pen or a ballpoint pen. Perhaps Satyajit Ray alone kept the honor of the nib pen alive until the end. One of his many healthy and beautiful passions was calligraphy. It is quite possible that his handwriting skills were related to his other artistic endeavors. Who doesn’t know that Rabindranath, who was honored worldwide as a painter in his later years, began by doodling in the margins of his handwritten manuscripts? While crossing out words, he would unconsciously create rhythmic black and white images. It is said that computers can also draw pictures. But how much of that picture is machine-made,

61

and how much is human?

I am a devotee of ink-guzzling pens, but that doesn't mean I have any quarrel with fountain pens or ballpoint pens. Because, I have already surrendered to ballpoint pens. In my mind, I have said, like that French poet – “You are strong, I am weak. You are brave, I am a coward. Still, if you want to kill me, then so be it. Consider me dead.”

Yes, the nib pen has been seen in the role of a murderer at least once. The extraordinary writer, the famous author of "Konkabati" and "Damarudhar," Trailokyanath Mukhopadhyay, died when his own pen accidentally pierced his chest. It is said that this wound led to his death.
