Gravity and Gravitation

Why does rain always fall from the sky towards the Earth? Why do fallen leaves from trees always fall to the ground? Why does the Earth revolve around the Sun? Why doesn't the moon fall onto the Earth like leaves from trees?

These events are all part of the game of gravity, or gravitation. In sixth grade, you learned that when a ball is dropped from above, it falls downwards due to the Earth's gravitational pull.

The Earth pulls all objects with gravitational force. That's why when you hold a book in your hand, you feel a downward pull. The more books you hold, the stronger this feeling becomes.

Spring balances are made to measure the weight of an object.

In the previous chapter, you saw how the Earth's gravitational pull is used to weigh objects.

In sixth grade, you also learned that it's not just the Earth that attracts all objects close to it; any two particles in this universe attract each other with equal force along the line connecting them.

This force is called gravitation. In reality, gravity is also a gravitational force. The gravitational force that acts between the Earth and any other object around it is called gravity.

Scientist Isaac Newton expressed the value of this gravitational force with a mathematical relationship.

That is, F = G*(m1*m2)/r^2

Here, F = gravitational force, m1 and m2 = the masses of the two particles, r = the distance between the two particles along a straight line.

G is called the "universal gravitational constant" because the value of G remains the same everywhere in this universe and doesn't depend on what medium is between the two particles.

In the SI system, the unit of G is N*m^2/kg^2

Through various experiments, the value of G has been calculated to be 6.67 x 10^-11 N*m^2/kg^2 in the SI system.
