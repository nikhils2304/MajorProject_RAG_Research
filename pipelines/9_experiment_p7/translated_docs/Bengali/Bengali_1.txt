The Discovery of Telenapota

Perhaps the conjunction of Saturday and Mars - it would be Mars, I suppose - can be achieved. Money can be found, and if someone comes and cajoles, the simplest fish, even those who never give up their aquatic existence, in that enterprise, once their hearts are pierced by the first hook, are eager to discover Telenapota.

++

If suddenly you have a two-day leave for the sake of discovering the most elusive place on earth, and you haven't had the good fortune of pulling anything out of the water other than a few small fish, then one day in the waning light of the afternoon you must board a bus to Telenapota, and amid the jostling of the road, along with the pushing of people, with a body sticky from sweat and dust in the Bhadra heat, you must get off at a crossroads two hours later.

Suddenly you will see a swamp-like place ahead; although the sun has not yet set, the surroundings are dark with dense forest. A rickety bus will pass over it with a grinding sound, heading off into the distance, disappearing around a bend in the road.

Looking anywhere, you won't see a soul. From the crossroads, a slightly larger road goes off to the side, winding like a curled-up water snake, disappearing into the dense bamboo groves and large thorny bushes. You will feel the damp, steamy atmosphere, saturated with the smell of rotting vegetation. You will feel as though a curse is slowly raising its invisible hood. It will seem as if someone has cut a canal of black water through it. Birds too seem to have withdrawn in fear.

For the discovery of Telenapota, it is essential that you have two friends and companions with you. As the bus departs, the darkness in front will seem to be rising. Perhaps they are not as eager as you are to fish, but who knows why they have come on this expedition.

After that, walking shoulder to shoulder, the three of you will try to break through the enveloping darkness, and after a short while, you will no longer see each other's faces, and you will look at each other with curious, questioning eyes. The buzzing of mosquitoes will become sharper and more concentrated. Occasionally, in the darkness, one of you will strike a clod of earth with a stick.

When you are wondering whether to go back to the main road and try to find a returning bus, suddenly from where that muddy canal disappears into the forest, you will hear a strange, uniquely astonishing sound – a cry that seems to be squeezed out with every breath.

It will feel as if someone, or something, is inhumanly forcing out a cry from within the forest. That sound will make you restless with anticipation. Your anticipation will not be in vain. In the dim darkness, you will first see a faint light swinging, and then a bullock cart will emerge from inside the forest, moving slowly and swaying from the canal.

Both the cart and the bullocks will seem to have emerged from a land of subterranean dwarfs.

Without wasting words, the three of you will somehow squeeze inside the canopy of the bullock cart and resolve the problem of how to fit the maximum amount of stuff into the minimum amount of space with three pairs of hands and feet and three heads.

The bullock cart will then begin to travel back the way it came, either along the road or through the canal. You will be amazed to see the dense dark forest slowly open up like a narrow tunnel in front, but still, the cart will move forward steadily at a slow pace.

For a while, you will try to adjust your limbs and heads. You will have occasional, unintentional collisions with your friends, and then slowly, the last outpost of consciousness will sink into the surrounding darkness. It will feel as if you have left the familiar world far behind.

There is a possibility that your senses will be disrupted, causing you a certain amount of discomfort. You will understand that only a timeless, misty world surrounds you.

Time stands still, so you won't be able to understand how long this enveloping feeling will last. Suddenly, you will wake up to a loud musical clamor and see stars through the canopy. The cart driver is enthusiastically playing a tin can from time to time.

When you ask curiously, the cart driver will inform you in a completely indifferent way, "Sir, it's to scare away the tiger."

Before you can ask in a trembling voice whether it is possible to scare away a tiger merely with the sound of a tin can, the cart driver will reassure you that by 'tiger' he only means a leopard, and that unless it is very hungry, this tin can is enough to keep it away.

While you are wondering how the existence of such a tiger-infested place is possible only thirty miles from the metropolis, the bullock cart will cross a huge meadow. By then, a waning moon will probably have risen in the sky. In its dim light, vague, vast ruins of ancient buildings will slowly pass by on both sides of the cart. Somewhere there is a silent pillar, somewhere the arch of a gateway, somewhere the ruins of a temple, all standing as witnesses to the passage of time, in vain hope.

You will sit up as much as possible in that state and feel a shiver run down your spine. You will have the impression of having fallen into some misty world of memories from the past, far beyond the living earth.

You don't know what time it is, but it will feel as if night never ends here. Everything is immersed in deep, primeval, endless silence, like the various animal bodies in a museum kept in alcohol.

After turning two or three times, the bullock cart will stop at a place. Somehow, you will gather your numb limbs and get out one by one like wooden dolls. A slightly pungent odor has been greeting you for a long time. You will realize it is the smell of decaying water hyacinth from the pond.

In the light of the newly risen moon, you will see a not-so-small pond right in front. Beside it, a fairly large dilapidated building, with broken roofs, collapsed walls, and eyeless windows like empty sockets, stands like a fortress against the newly risen moon.

You will have to arrange to stay in a relatively habitable room in one of these ruins.

The cart driver will bring a broken lantern into the room and place it there. Along with it, a pitcher of water. You will understand that you are the first representatives of humankind to set foot there after many eras. Perhaps someone has tried in vain to clean the cobwebs, tangled debris, and dust in the room before. You will get proof that the presiding spirit of the room is angry with an indistinct, stuffy smell. Dilapidated plaster will rain down from the ceiling and walls with the slightest movement. Two or three bats will argue with you all night over the possession of the dark room, like curses from that angry spirit.

For the discovery of Telenapota, one of your two friends needs to be a wine lover and the other a sleepy companion. As soon as you reach the room, one of them will stretch out on the floor over some kind of matting, immediately starting to snore, while the other will immerse himself in the drink.

As the night progresses, the glass chimney of the broken lantern will gradually become thickly blackened and slowly go blind. Having received news from some mysterious radio signal, all the able-bodied adult mosquitoes in the area will come to greet the newcomers and establish a blood relationship with them. If you are discerning, you will understand from their distinctive posture on the wall and on your body that they are the most aristocratic of the mosquitoes, the unique carriers of the malaria goddess Anopheles. Your two friends are then unconscious for two reasons. Slowly, you will leave the bed and stand up, then, in order to get some relief from the stifling heat, you will try to go up to the roof using the crumbling stairs, lantern in hand.

Every moment, the danger of falling bricks or tiles will try to deter you, yet you will not be able to avoid going up, drawn by some irresistible attraction.

When you go to the roof, you will see that in most places, the plaster has broken and turned to dust, and a forest of shrubs has driven its roots through the cracks and crevices, advancing the destruction of the building considerably from within; yet, in the faint light of the waning moon, everything will seem strangely enchanting. You will feel that if you stare for a while, the imprisoned princess of this deathly, slumbering, magical city will suddenly appear from some secret chamber, taking a golden or silver thread and passing it through the eye of a needle in the narrow ray of light that has fallen on the ruined heap across the way.

That light seems to have made her momentarily unconscious, you will realize. At that moment, in one of the windows of the house you had just seen, a faint shadow will appear.

Who is this woman at the window, why does she not sleep, you will try to think, but nothing will make sense to you. You will feel that it is all an illusion of your eyes. The bubble of a long-lost dream seems to have risen for a moment in the fathomless slumber of this ruined city, only to disappear again.

At some point, you will quietly come back down and lie down beside your two friends, and you will not know when.

When you wake up, you will be surprised to see that morning also comes to this land of eternal night, with the chirping of birds.

You will surely not forget your original purpose. With a joyful roar of the hunt, one of your friends will startle you as a fat, long snake slides down the broken steps and into the pond.

At the right time, after arranging a sixteen-course feast, you will lower your hook into the green waters of the pond, covered with duckweed, complete with the offerings.

The day will grow. From the bent bamboo tip hanging over the other bank, a colorful bird will flash sparks of color on the water of the pond from time to time, as if to mock you, and then return to the bamboo tip and taunt you in an incomprehensible language. A couple of dragonflies will compete, emerging from some crack and slowly moving their thin glass-like wings, trying to sit on your float, and then suddenly, the sound of water will break your reverie. Waves have risen in the still water; your float is swaying.

Turning your head, you will see a girl filling a shiny brass pitcher with water, pushing the duckweed aside with the waves of the pond. There is curiosity in the girl's eyes, but her movements are shy and hesitant.

You won't be able to guess her age. Looking at the serene, compassionate gravity of her face, it will not seem at all unnatural to you to see the long, relentless path of life she has crossed and her transition into youth. Looking at her thin, underdeveloped body, it will seem as if her adolescence has been suspended.

The girl will look straight at you, notice your float, then look away, and take the pitcher to her waist. The float is gone. A little awkwardly, she will start to leave, but looking back, the girl will suddenly say, "Why are you sitting? There's no fish in this pond."

Her voice is so calm, sweet, and deep that it is impossible for you to speak to a stranger like this on your own. Just as that unexpected shock is about to surface again, you will lift the fishing rod and see that the bait is gone from the hook.

Confused by the sudden impulse, you will forget to tug on the rod. As soon as you turn your head, you will have to look at her again. She, too, will turn her face away and leave with slow, hesitant steps, but it will seem as if a glimpse of a slight, radiant smile has played across her serene, compassionate face.

After that, the solitude of the pond's banks will no longer be broken. The kingfisher across the way, ashamed of its futile attempts to mock you, will have flown away long ago. Perhaps, with deep disdain for your strength and ability, the fish will not want to enter the competition a second time. The incident from a little while ago will seem unreal to you. You will be disappointed.

At one point, you will give up and pack up your fishing gear. You will hear your friends laughing at you, "Who was it, asking you about fishing?" Upset by their mockery, you will tell this story to anyone who will listen. Surprised by your eagerness, your friends will inquire, "How did Yamini herself arrange the afternoon meal in that deserted haunted place?"

Then, perhaps, you will learn that she is a distant relative of your wine-loving friend. At the same time, you will learn that the marriage arrangements were made there, with them.

You will find out that in the ruined heap where a shadowy figure was briefly causing your astonishment last night, Yamini lives.

In the harsh light of day, the unsightly reality will deeply trouble you. You never thought that the image of destruction could become so ugly.

You will be bewildered to learn that this is Yamini's house. Perhaps a room in this very house has been arranged for your meal. The unnecessary shame, shyness, or awkwardness that you had not noticed before will be more apparent to you now. It will seem as if the weariness of this abandoned, vast, desolate place has cast a shadow on her face. Even when she looks at everything, her gaze is lost in a deep, silent world. It seems as if one day she will slowly disappear into these ruins.

While serving you, you will see her become restless and anxious a couple of times, as if someone is calling from a room upstairs in a faint voice. Yamini will rush out worriedly. Each time she returns, it will seem as if the shadow of sadness on her face is growing deeper, along with a kind of helpless restlessness in her eyes.

When you have finished eating, you will hear the voice from above, desperately calling from the door, "Come and listen here for a moment, Monida."

Your wine-loving friend will go to the door. That conversation will be so subdued that you won't be able to hear it.

After hesitating for a while, Moni will say in a hesitant, frightened voice, "The householder is not understanding anything. She has become so restless since hearing about your arrival, I can't tell you! She keeps saying, 'He must have come. He is only too shy to see me, I know. Call him. Why are you hiding him from me?' I can't think what to do. Since she went blind, she has become so impatient lately that she doesn't understand anything you explain to her, and she gets so angry that she starts banging her head, and then it becomes difficult to save her life!"

"Hmm, this looks like a big problem. Even if she had eyes, I would have shown her that none of those who have come are Niranjan."

Now you will also be able to hear the weak but sharp, angry call from above. Yamini will now plead in a pitiful voice, "You come once, Monida, if you can calm her down with a little explanation."

"Okay, you go, I'm coming." Moni will now say to himself as he enters the room, "What a mess this has become, anyway. The old woman has lost her ability to move her limbs, she has no eyes, but she is determined that she will not die."

Perhaps you will now want to know what is going on. Moni will say in an uninterested tone, "What else! She had arranged Yamini's marriage with a distant nephew of hers named Niranjan when they were young. Even four years ago, that boy had come and told her that he would marry her daughter when he returned from his job abroad. Since then, the old woman has been counting the days in the hope, sitting inside this dragon-palace."

You will not be able to help asking yourself now, "Has Niranjan not yet returned from abroad?"

"Oh, when did he go abroad to come back! He just gave her this false hope because the old woman is so stubborn. He has no responsibility to save such a wretched girl. He has been married and is living a blissful family life for a long time now. But who will tell her that? If she is told, she will not believe it, and if she does believe it, she will collapse and die! Who will needlessly share the blame?"

"Does Yamini know about Niranjan?"

"Of course she knows! But there is no way to tell her mother. Well, I'm off to finish what I have to. Saying that, Moni will start to move towards the stairs.

At that moment, perhaps without your knowledge, you will have to stand up. Suddenly you may blurt out, "Let me come too."

"You will go!" Moni will turn around and look at you in astonishment.

"Yes. Is there any objection to going?"

"No, what objection is there!" Saying that, Moni will lead you along the way in a rather bewildered manner.

The room you reach through the narrow, dark, broken staircase will feel as if it is not upstairs, but in a tunnel under the ground. There is only one window, and it is closed; coming from the outside light, everything will seem blurry at first, then you will realize that a thin, skeletal figure, wrapped in tattered rags, is lying on an almost room-sized tattered bed. On one side of the bed, Yamini is standing like a stone statue.

Even within those skeletal limbs, hearing your footsteps will seem to cause a stir. "Who is it, Niranjan, have you come? The poor aunt has been remembered by you after so many days, my dear. All this time, I could not die in peace. The life that is stuck in my throat is because of you. Now, don't you disappear like that again!"

Moni will try to say something, but you will stop him, and without turning your face, you will suddenly say, "No, aunt, I will not run away again."

You will be able to feel the stunned astonishment on the face of a pure-hearted girl like Yamini. But you will not have the opportunity to look in any direction, because your whole being will then be breathlessly intent on the pair of eyes that are looking at you from within the empty sockets. It will seem as if two black flames of darkness are emerging from the empty sockets and licking and testing your entire body.

Then you will hear, "I have been counting the days guarding this ghost town. Yes, it is he! But, why were you so late in coming, my son?"

The old woman will gasp as she speaks. For a moment, in the midst of her lifelong struggle against death, the basis of deep despair, a faint hint of what seems like an unwavering vow can be discerned.

The old woman will say again, "You will be happy with Yamini, my dear. I am not saying this because she was born from my womb, but there is no other girl like her. The troubles and suffering have aged her, and she is not right in the head, she nags and torments the girl day and night so much that there is no end to it. This is a land of cremation grounds; even if you search ten houses, you will not find a man. Only old women like me, clinging to broken bricks, are struggling here and there; among them, that girl is doing everything, even though she is a woman!"

Even though you want to, you will not have the courage to look up even once. You will know that your true identity can no longer be kept secret.

The old woman will say with a short sigh, "Will you take Yamini, my son? Say your last words so that I can die in peace."

In a choked voice, you will only be able to say, "I promise you, aunt. My word will not change."

Then, in the afternoon, the bullock cart will come to a stop at the door again. The three of you will get into it one by one. Just as the cart is about to leave, Yamini, standing in front of the door, will only look at you with those sad eyes, raising the question with grateful hesitation, "Your fishing rod and tackle were left behind!"

You will smile and say, "What of it? Will the fish of Telenapota give you an opportunity to sneer at me again because I failed this time?"

Yamini will not turn her face away. From her eyes, not her lips, a sweet light seems to be floating across the horizon of your heart like the white clouds of autumn.

The cart will move on. Your friends may discuss how a terrible flood of malaria, one hundred or one hundred and fifty years ago, first washed away and left Telenapota on this forgotten, obliterated edge.

You will not hear those words very clearly. The crampedness of the cart and the jolting will trouble you, but one word will not sound harsh to you: Telenapota.

When you reach the crowded, brightly lit roads of the metropolis, a memory of Telenapota will still be shining like a star in your mind, a memory that is distant yet very intimate to you.

A few days will pass. You may not even notice whether a little fog has accumulated in the sky of your mind. Then, one day, when you are about to remove all obstacles and set out for Telenapota, you will suddenly have a headache and shivering chills: the doctor will come and feel your forehead.

The thermometer will show one hundred and five degrees. The doctor will say, "Malaria! Where did you catch it from?"

While listening to the diagnosis, you will become delirious in the fever.

After many days, when the bitter dream has passed, you will see that much of the dirt of the body and mind has been washed away in the meantime. As the setting star fades, the memory will seem like a blurry dream to you. You will feel that Telenapota is nowhere, there is nothing true.

That girl, whose face is grave and whose gaze is distant and compassionate, a shadow of the ruined city, who knows when, as a fleeting fantasy of some weak moment, Telenapota will be briefly discovered for you and then again be immersed in the fathomless depths of eternal night.

Premendra Mitra (1904-1988): One of the leading poets, novelists, and short story writers in Bengali literature.

Due to his father's railway job, he had the opportunity to travel to various parts of India, which was later reflected in his literary life. Sharp language and indirect style were the hallmarks of his writing. His characteristic style was satire and socio-political commentary through humor. Some of his famous collections of stories include: Benashi Bandar, Putul O Pratima, Mavika, Parna, Aforanta, Jalpayra Dhulidhusar, Mahanagar, Saptapadi, Nana Range Bona. Only Kerani, Moto Baro, Punam, Shrinkhal, Vikrita Kar Firde, etc. are his unforgettable short stories. His science-based writings include Pipre Puran, Mangalbaari, Prithibir Shatru, Karal Kit, Mayadanaber Dwip, etc. His first published story, "Shudhu Kerani," was in the "Prabashi" magazine in March 1924. He was associated with magazines such as "Kallol," "Kalikalam," and "Banglar Katha" "Bangabani". Ghanada, Mamababu, Bhoot-Shikari Mejokarta, and Goyenda-Kabi Parashar Barma, etc. are some of his creations.
