The Eye Opener

Ashapurna Devi

Tapan's eyes widened at the news! The new brother-in-law, the one Tapan's younger aunt had married just a few days ago with much fanfare, was a writer. And his books were published too. Many of his books had been printed.

That meant Tapan's new brother-in-law was a writer. A real, live writer. Tapan had never seen a real writer so closely, didn't even know it was possible. He had doubts that writers were like his father, his younger uncle, or his older uncle. But seeing his new brother-in-law, Tapan's eyes were opened.

Surprisingly, nothing was out of place, nothing was different, he was just an ordinary man! Just like them, he shaved, smoked cigarettes, and at dinner he would say, "Oh, enough, I can't possibly eat all this," and have half of it taken away. He took showers when it was time to shower, and slept when it was time to sleep. Besides—

Just like his younger uncles, he would argue vehemently about everything in the newspaper, and in the end, say, "Nothing will ever happen in this country," and go to the movies or go out for a walk, all dressed up.

It was summer vacation, so the brother-in-law was staying at his in-laws' house for a few days. Tapan had come to his uncle's house for the wedding, and had stayed because he had vacation. The brother-in-law also had a vacation.

So Tapan would have the opportunity to see a real, live writer all the time. And in that opportunity, Tapan could see that a "writer" wasn't some creature fallen from the sky, but a person just like Tapan and his family.

The brother-in-law was a college professor, and since he was on vacation, he was staying at his in-laws' house for a few days.

"Why shouldn't Tapan be a writer, too?"

Tapan, of course, protested loudly, "No, no!" but who listened to him?

Tapan was secretly thrilled by his aunt's excitement. Though he said "No, no" out loud.

And in that opportunity, he was taking a nice afternoon nap. The younger aunt rushed in.

By that time, the story had already passed into the hands of the younger brother-in-law. With a flurry of excitement, the younger aunt broke his sleep, saying, "He understands the true value of writing. A jeweler knows the value of a gem."

A little later, the younger brother-in-law called Tapan and, probably just to please his new bride's brother, said, "Tapan, your story is wonderful. It just needs a little 'correction' to be ready for publication."

At first, Tapan thought he was joking, but when he saw the look of pity on his brother-in-law's face, he was filled with happiness.

"Well then, you should get his story published," the aunt said. "It would be a worthy task for a brother-in-law."

The brother-in-law said in the same pitying tone, "Yes, I can do that! The editor of 'Sandhyatara' won't be able to say no to me. Okay, Tapan, I'll get your story published."

The topic came up at the tea table in the evening.

And everyone laughed at Tapan's story. But the brother-in-law said, "No, no, I'm telling you, Tapan has a knack for writing. And an eye for detail. Otherwise, a boy of this age would write about murders, accidents, or starving to death. Tapan hasn't gone in that direction, he's written about the experience and feelings of his first day of school, which is very good. He's got potential."

Tapan stared with bewildered eyes.

The brother-in-law left with the story. Tapan sat there, feeling grateful, counting the days.

That's what Tapan was thinking day and night. He had heard piles and piles of stories since he was a child, and now he knew what a story was.

He just didn't know that it could be written by such ordinary people. He learned that from seeing his new brother-in-law.

Then who could hold Tapan back?

At noon, when everyone was still, Tapan quietly took a notebook (his homework notebook, which his mother wouldn't leave behind even at the wedding!) and a pen, and went up to the third-floor stairs, and, whether you believe it or not, he wrote an entire story in one sitting.

When he read it after writing it, what a thing!

It really seemed exactly like a story! A chill went down Tapan's spine, the hairs on his head stood on end.

That meant Tapan had really written a story. That meant Tapan could now be called a "writer."

Suddenly, Tapan felt a terrible excitement, and he ran downstairs—and told his younger aunt first, "Auntie, I've written a story!"

His younger aunt was his lifelong friend, eight years older but the same age in spirit. So when he came to his uncle's house, everything was with his younger aunt. So he gave the terrible news of his joy to his younger aunt first.

But after the marriage, the younger aunt seemed to have become a bit more mature, so without even reading the whole story, she looked at it with a critical eye and said in a sweet voice, "Oh my, you've written this so well? You didn't copy it from anywhere, did you? Let me show it to your brother-in-law—"

Where was the tight, printed form of the story for which Tapan longed? Even after returning home from his uncle's house.

Meanwhile, at home, Tapan had become known as a poet, a literary figure, a storyteller. And everyone teased him, "You'll be something. Yes, you'll be something."

Yet, amid all this teasing, Tapan had written two or three more stories. The vacation was coming to an end, and the homework hadn't been done, but he was still writing. Writing in secret, as if he had a habit.

Then the vacation ended, and school started in earnest. He had completely lost hope about the first story, and he was sitting with a depressed mind when that event happened.

His younger aunt and brother-in-law came to visit one day, with a copy of "Sandhyatara" in their hands.

Tapan's heart leapt in his chest.

Could it be? Was this really the happiest day of Tapan's life?

But was that even possible? Tapan Kumar Roy's story, written in real print, would be passed around by thousands of boys?

Did such a miraculous event really happen in the world?

Yes, it had happened, it had really happened.

His name was even in the table of contents.

"First Day" by Tapan Kumar Roy.

The whole house was in an uproar. Tapan's story had been printed in a magazine. His writer brother-in-law had gotten it printed! The magazine was passed from hand to hand, everyone glanced at it and said, "Wow, he's written it so well."

The brother-in-law smiled mildly, saying, "I had to make a little 'correction,' of course. It was just a rough draft, after all."

Gradually, that word also spread.

The word about the corrections.

His father said, "That's why. How else would he write something so quickly and have it printed?"

His older uncle said, "It's not bad to have a writer brother-in-law like that. If we had one, we would have tried too."

The younger aunt sat with a satisfied expression, eating fried eggs and drinking tea, while the brother-in-law just drank coffee.

Today, there was no other topic of conversation, only Tapan's story and the greatness of Tapan's new brother-in-law. If he hadn't gone himself, would the editor of "Sandhyatara" have touched Tapan's story with his little finger?

Tapan seemed to disappear into all these words. The terrible joy that should have come from having a story printed, he couldn't find that joy.

After a long time, his mother said, "Why don't you read it out loud yourself, Tapan? I didn't know you had it in you!"

Tapan finally got the book in his own hands.

His mother said, "Go on, read it! Don't be shy! Read it, let everyone hear."

Tapan broke his shyness and started to read—

But what was this?

What was Tapan reading?

Every single line was new, unfamiliar to Tapan.

Where was Tapan in it?

That meant the brother-in-law had completely corrected Tapan's story. That is, he had rewritten it with his own experienced pen! Tapan couldn't read. He sat there like a mute. Then he got a scolding, "What's wrong with you? You seem to have gotten heavy. Everyone wants to hear it, and you can't read?"

Tapan read fluently. Tapan couldn't understand what he was reading. Still, "Wonderful, wonderful" was read out. And once again, the cry went up that Tapan's writer brother-in-law had gotten Tapan's story printed.

Tapan threw the book down and left, Tapan went up to the roof and wiped his eyes with the bottom of his shirt. Tapan felt that today was the saddest day of his life. Why? Tapan didn't know.

Only, at this moment of sadness, Tapan made a deep resolution, if he ever had his writing printed, he would give it away. His own rough draft. Whether it was printed or not.

Tapan should never have to hear "So-and-so got Tapan's writing printed."

And Tapan should never have to read someone else's lines while reading his own story.

There was nothing more sad than that, nothing more insulting!

Ashapurna Devi (1909-1995): One of the leading Bengali writers. She was born in Calcutta. She did not have the opportunity to study at school and college. Yet, through her extraordinary sharp vision, sensitivity, and experience of the familiar society, she depicted the Bengali middle-class life in her stories and novels with astonishing skill. During her long life, she wrote numerous novels, stories, and children's books. Among these, "Chhoto Thakurdar Kashi Yatra", "Pratham Pratishruti", "Subarnalata", "Bakul Katha", "Agnipariksha", "Sagar Shukhaye Jaay", "Shashi Babur Sansar", "Sonar Harin" etc. are notable. At least 63 of her books have been translated into various languages. She has been awarded the "Rabindra Puraskar", "Sahitya Akademi Award", "Jnanpith Award", several "D. Litt" from universities and various government honors.
