The word "kriya" means time. Everything that exists is subject to this time. From the beginningless past to the endless future - time is infinite. Nothing is beyond the grasp of time. In fact, time is also called "Kal" because it "cuts" or devours everything.

For convenience, we divide this continuous time into three parts. The event or action that has already happened, some of which is memorable and the rest of which has been lost in the abyss of oblivion - we call it the past. And the event or action that has not yet happened, but which will inevitably happen, or is likely to happen, we call it the future. Needless to say, both the past and the future are endless. And the moment that connects these two is called the present. Now, only this moment is the present. Just as the lamp of a moment ago is no longer the same in the next moment, because the oil has decreased, the shape of the flame has changed, and the wick has burned a little, yet we seem to see only one lamp at different moments, similarly, the present moment appears in this journey from the past to the future. Its proposal lies in the cause-and-effect relationship of the countless and complex flow of actions from the beginningless past, and its dissolution and transformation in the infinite possibilities of the future. That is, there are three main divisions of time: (a) past, (b) present, (c) future.

Present Tense

The tense of the action that is happening now, in the present, is called the present tense. As mentioned earlier, this present connects the past and the future. In terms of subtlety, it also has various

types. According to these types, verbs of different forms are formed within the scope of the present tense. For example:

“I have been missing you since that morning.”

"That's why he has come and stood in the middle of the road now."

Simple or Habitual Present, (2) Continuous Present, (3) Present Perfect, and (4) Present Imperative.

(1) Simple or Habitual Present:

Where the action happens naturally or happens all the time, its tense is called the simple present

or habitual present. In the simple or habitual present, no tense-denoting suffix is ​​added to the root verb. The person-denoting inflections "i," "a," and "e" are added in the first, second, and third persons, respectively.

(b) Use of the present tense in the sense of the past tense: Sometimes the present tense is used obliquely to mean the past tense. Like:

The tense of the verb that has not yet happened,

Basic Tense and Compound Tense

The tense of the verb can be divided into two parts according to "form" and meaning, namely __ 1. Basic Tense or Simple

Tense and 2. Compound Tense.

(1) Basic Tense or Simple Tense:

The tense in which the verb is formed by adding only the verb inflection to the verb without adding any other verb to the root

is called the basic tense or simple tense.

Simple/Habitual Present, Simple/Habitual Past, Habitual Past, Simple Future -- these

four are examples of basic or simple tenses.

(2) Mixed Tense or Compound Tense:

The tense of the verb that is formed by adding another verb and verb inflection to the root verb is called

mixed tense or compound tense. In Bengali, there are ten compound tense forms, respectively: Continuous

Present, Present Perfect, Continuous Past, Past Perfect, Continuous Future, Future Perfect

or Future Probable or Probable Past, Habitual Present, Habitual Continuous Present, Continuous Perfect Habitual

and Perfect Habitual or Probable Perfect Habitual.

Mood of the Verb

The way the action of the verb is completed in the sentence is called the mood or type of the verb. The mood or type of the verb

can be divided into three parts: (1) Indicative Mood, (2) Imperative Mood and (3) Relative Mood.

1. Indicative Mood: When any action is indicated by a verb through the occurrence or non-occurrence of an action, or even by a question or exclamation, then it is called the indicative or determinative or assertive

mood. For example:

"I have heard the deer-calling of Sindhumuni."

"Truth, Seleucus! How strange is this country!"

"Will all your work be in poetry?"

2. Imperative Mood: The special style of verb that is used when expressing an order, advice, request, permission, blessing, desire, or prayer is called the imperative mood. For example:

"Start the meeting, President." (Order)

"Forget it, brother, how much difference has been made with whom." (Advice)

"If you mistakenly write a wrong answer, still give me marks." (Prayer)

"See, don't forget us." (Request)

In terms of "tense," the imperative is seen in two parts: the imperative of the present tense and the imperative of the future tense.

(a) Imperative of the Present Tense:

In the present tense, the imperative of the present tense is used to denote prayer, advice, request, permission, etc.

It is only used in the case of the verbs of the middle person and the third person. In the present

tense of the imperative, the suffixes "un," "is," "uk" __ are usually added to the root verb to form the verb. In the imperative of the "kor" verb in the present tense, "kor" + o - koro in the middle person,

"kor + un - korun to denote respect, and "kor + is - koris is formed to denote insignificance/closeness. And in the third

person, kor + uk - koruk and kor + un - korun is formed to denote respect. For example:

"If I don't find it, I will drown in a Chinese sentence."

"Fill my life with happiness, take away my thirst, and give me life first."

"You leave quickly."

(b) Imperative of the Future Tense:

The imperative that expresses the time of action of prayer, order, advice, request, etc., in the future is called the future

imperative. Here, the suffix "ib," which denotes the future tense, and the verb inflections "e" or "io" of the middle and third

persons are added to the root verb. There is no imperative in the first person. In the future tense of the "kor"

verb, kor + ib+ e = koribe or kor+

io - korio in the middle person. The verb inflections "is" and "haben" are added to denote insignificance and respect, respectively. For example, "kor + is - koris and "kor + haben - koriben. For example:

"Don't go, night, with your stars."

"Never tell a lie."

"Respect your parents."

It should be remembered that Acharya Suniti Kumar Chattopadhyay has reminded us, "The imperative verb is not a separate tense form, but a distinct mood of the verb."

If the meaning of one sentence in a mixed or complex sentence depends on another sentence, then the

verb mood of the sentence on which the meaning depends is called the relative mood or conditional mood. For example:

"If no one comes

when they hear your call, then walk alone."

"If people

love people honestly, then it does not go in vain."

"If you cannot change, then you will have to die standing still."
