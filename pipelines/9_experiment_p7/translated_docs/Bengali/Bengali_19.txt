Chapter 4

Suniti Kumar Chatterjee

Like any other five subjects, India is a country of diverse variety even in food and clothing - although there is a unity of an underlying link even within this diversity. Due to the different natural environments, climates, and food compositions in different regions of India, the food of Punjab, North India, Bengal, Maharashtra, South India, Nepal, etc., are of different types. In Europe, the entire continent has been divided into two main parts based on the natural environment - the relatively cold Northern Europe and the warmer Southern Europe. In Northern Europe, people raise more cows. They usually eat more butter in that region, and things fried with butter (and pork fat when lacking) are more common. Besides, beer made from barley is more commonly consumed as a drink in the northern countries. Southern Europe is the land of goats, and olive trees thrive in this region. Olive oil is commonly used in cooking; grapes also grow abundantly, so everyone drinks wine made from grape juice. Looking at these food differences, Northern Europe is said to be a Land of Butter, and Southern Europe is a Land of Oil and Wine. India can be broadly divided in this way - Punjab, North India, Maharashtra are dry regions, and the coastal regions are rainy; and considering the general food habits, the northern region can be called Land of Bread and Lentils and Ghee, and Bengal, Odisha, Assam, the coastal regions of Madras, Bombay, and Kerala can be called Land of Rice, Fish, and Oil. That is, the land of rice, fish, and oil.

Oil is not the same everywhere. Mustard oil is used in Bengal, Assam, and Odisha, sesame oil in Andhra, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu, and coconut oil in Kerala - ghee eaters do not like any of these (thanks to today's "vegetable ghee," India has become unified in this matter). This has brought about a kind of revolution in food in different parts of India, yet in some ways, there is an all-India commonality in this food. The first thing about Indian food - it is dominated by vegetarianism. The general food is lentil-roti (this roti is sometimes made from wheat flour, sometimes from barley, and sometimes from gram flour). Lentils must have some spice and oil or ghee. India's favorite food is rice and vegetables, be it vegetarian lentils or vegetable stew, dalna, or shukto, or fish and meat curry. Two things from India have been accepted in the international food list, and almost every country's restaurant serves and eats them. These are Curry i.e. spicy vegetable (meat, egg or fish) and rice, and Chutney i.e. spicy-sour-sweet chutney. Besides, another Indian dish is sometimes served, and people eagerly eat it, and that is Curry-Rice - this is also an English-Indian creation.

However, outside of India, curry-rice is not as widespread as chutney. Another Indian food is Rasam - a sour-spicy lentil soup - which is very ancient and prevalent in South India, but has not gained popularity throughout India from the ancient times. In Tamil, it is called "Mulgutanni" i.e. chili water, from which the name Mulligatawny Soup has originated in the mouths of the English. There are a few other things as well, but they have not become as widespread as the pickle or chutney. For example, Khichuri - it is a popular food all over India, but since lentils are not so popular outside, it is difficult for outsiders to like Khichuri. Payesh is another divine Indian food, but there is not much tradition of this payesh in Greece, another milk-rich country.

India has created some good sweets, but they have not been able to establish their own place in the world's confectionery world. Most of India's culinary achievements are with vegetarian food, and India has not been able to compete with other countries in vegetarian food. The fish cuisine of some regions of India has its own characteristics and specialties, such as in Bengal. In meat cooking, India has mostly imitated Iran. Iranian cuisine has its own distinct place in the culinary world. A little excess of Iranian spices has created Indian Mughlai cuisine - but in the main Indian cuisine, Mughlai cuisine has not been able to establish its distinct place.

India is mainly a country of vegetarians. Eating meat is very less common in this country compared to other countries. Being a hot country, vegetarian food is more suitable for health. Those who eat fish and meat are not few in number. But they do not eat it every day, or do not get it. Therefore, there is more prevalence of vegetable, lentil, milk, etc. The availability of vegetarian food and the widespread ideal of non-violence are the main reasons for attracting the general public of the country towards vegetarian food. Many meat-loving communities have gradually become vegetarian after coming to India - the Aryans were meat-loving before, but later became predominantly vegetarian. This is also true of the Aryans of India.

In prehistoric times, humans used to collect food like animals and birds - hunting, catching fish, or gathering and increasing, or digging the soil and picking up animal or bird meat, fish, turtles, crabs, snails, mussels, tubers, insects, whatever they found, they ate everything. Later, when humans learned animal husbandry, learned to cultivate barley, rice, wheat, millet, etc., then the milk and meat of domestic animals, and the fruits and crops of agriculture, especially came into their enjoyment, man came to the advanced stage of preparing and storing food from the primitive state of collecting food. During this time, according to the natural environment of the country, food habits developed among people in different regions - the people of pastoral semi-nomadic tribes, like the original Aryans, became slaves to milk and meat, and among the various farming communities, such as the Nishads or Austrics, the non-Aryans of India, rice, lentils, vegetables, and (where available, fish, and meat of domestic ducks, chickens, pigeons, etc.) became more prevalent. Cows, goats, sheep, horses - these animals were not so common among the non-Aryans of India - people did not use bullocks-horses for plowing even for cultivation at that time, they used to do "Jhum" cultivation or cultivate by hoeing the soil with their hands. However, they raised pigs, and ate pork.

As a result of such diverse environments, generally before the Aryans came to India, rice, lentils, vegetables, some fish and meat, were the food of the pre-Aryan people. The Aryans came as a pastoral, milk-loving, meat-eating people. They used to offer their favorite food to their worshiped deities through the fire with Homam rituals. This food was meat and fat of goats, sheep, cows, horses, suffocated or otherwise cruelly "Alambhan" killed, barley bread "Purodasha", milk, ghee, and intoxicating Soma-rasa. Without these things, without offering these to the deities by throwing them into the fire, their worship would not be done. The worship rituals of the non-Aryans were different. There was no part of fire in it. Various types of raw crops, fruits, and cooked delicacies were offered in front of the deity's idol or symbol, and people ate it with respect as the deity's Prasad. Some amount of fish, meat, and eggs were also given in this delicacy (eggs are no longer used in Hindu society in India where Aryan or Brahmin attitude is strong, but the use of eggs is seen in Nepal, the eastern border of India, and outside India). Animals were sacrificed, the animal's head was cut off in one stroke, and blood was taken in a pot and placed in front of the deity, or somewhere that blood was smeared on the deity's idol. However, vegetarian food was considered the most suitable for the Thakur's offering. The meat-loving Aryan people who performed Homa rituals settled in India. They came under the influence of the non-Aryans. A mixture of language and culture began between the two communities, as a result of Anuloma and Pratiloma marriages. The mixed Hindu community was created. Then the different ideals of non-vegetarian and vegetarian food came before people's eyes. Arguments and propaganda for and against these two continued. In the Anushasan Parva of the Mahabharata, the mention of this non-vegetarian-versus-vegetarian issue is seen. Meat is the most delicious, nutritious, and popular food, this opinion has been acknowledged in the Mahabharata - this is the statement on behalf of the meat-loving community; but at the same time, it has also been said as the final word that it is appropriate to give up meat, keeping in mind the ideal of non-violence. That is, the idea that vegetarian food is of a higher level than non-vegetarian food in philosophical judgment is also seen in history. On one hand there is the meat-eating of the Vedic Aryans, and on the other hand there is the abstinence from meat among the general public of the later Aryan Hindus. In Kautilya's Arthashastra, it has been said that a serving of rice, a quarter of its amount of ghee or oil, and soup cooked with spices - probably lentils - this is "Aryabhakta," that is, the food of the Aryan or upper-class Hindu. In the food of the lower class, the proportion of lentils and ghee or oil is somewhat less.

This lentil-rice (or lentil-roti later) became the most notable food of India.

Turkish Muslims conquered India and became the kings of North India in the eleventh century AD. They used Persian language in royal affairs and literature. Whether they were Turks, Pathans or Afghans, or Iranians - in their homeland they ate wheat bread, mutton curry - kebab-shulpak meat. Coming to this country, they were surprised to see that Hindus did not touch meat. In Persian, they commented, "Hindu aan dalla-ba dalla mi-khourdand, o az mi-gashtand dil-bhat?" - "Hindus eat grain with grain and turn away from rice with lentils?"

This lentil-rice (or what is called 'dal-roti' or dal-bhat) - This lentil-bhat tradition is still going on as the main food tradition throughout India.

At the end of the seventeenth century, the Dutch observer François Pelsaert stated that the staple food in Jahangir's India was boiled rice or bhat, with dal, achaar, and then a little ghee on top.

The method of cooking meat that existed in Hindu-era India, a special description of which is found in Kautilya's Arthashastra, cannot be found anymore. There are many types of rice mentioned in the Sanskrit books on prevalent cooking methods during the reign of King Nala - therefore, it is known as 'Nala-Pak'.

From the time of Chaitanyadeva, many kinds of descriptions about vegetables-meat, various kinds of eggplant have been written in Bengali Vaishnava literature. Similarly, descriptions of vegetarian and non-vegetarian feasts - dal, bhat, shak, tarkari- and sweets, peda, etc. are found in Hindi and North Indian literature.

The Sanskrit names of foods "Kacchi" i.e. raw and "Pakki" i.e. food cooked in ghee, describe the high-class feasts from North to South. Laddu Peda, this sweetmeat originated in the pre-Muslim era from the fifteenth century, during the reign of the Mughal Empire. Mohanbhog is an ancient name--this name is found in Sanskrit books, it is also prevalent somewhere in India. But Imarti, Jalebi, Barfi, Kalakand--these very popular sweets were of Persian origin. Many orthodox Hindus would not want to eat the word "Gaja," i.e. Goujihba, the original name of the pastry. The Persian “Gao-Zaban”

"Gao-jiha, Gaoji, Gaja" - this name has now changed to such an extent that in order to describe its original form, we explain it by saying - "Jivhegaja".

Our Sandesh Peda, Barfi, Kalakand, Gulab Jamun, Rabri; and Chanar Murki, Chamcham - these are very famous. However, the tradition of making sweets by curdling and congealing milk, and then turning them into cheese is not prevalent anywhere else in India other than Bengal's Rosogolla - Rosogolla from Bengal has now become very famous throughout India as "Bangal-Mithai" due to its own virtues.

The second type of sweet is made with flour, chatu, or lentils of barley, wheat flour, etc., mixed with sugar or sugar juice. There must be some kind of nuts mixed in.

Indian sweets do not use eggs--but in a very limited number of sweets (for example, various types of bora and vadi) fried in ghee or oil, salt is added. South Indian Upma, Luchi, and various types of fuluri fall into this category.

Indian cooking is predominantly vegetarian - but there are regional specialties. The way "baghar" or tempering is added to lentils, fries, vegetables, etc. in North India is different from the tempering or seasoning in our country.

Again, the taste of vegetables is changed by boiling them in rice water somewhere. Yogurt, tamarind, amchur, chilies, black pepper, garam masala, or fried masala, coconut, these are some of the main ingredients of Indian cuisine.

(Talking about these two) heavenly Mughlai, i.e. Iranian-Turani cuisine, is not much used in Arab countries. Of all the regional or village cooking in India, Chinese or European cuisine is one of the most important ingredients. In that case, a book can be written about the specific Bengali cuisine and the foreign cuisines that have found a place in the Bengali kitchen. Fortunately, the famous book "Pak Pranali" by Bipradas Mukhopadhyay is worth mentioning with pride. Bengal has adopted many things from the Portuguese, French, and English - especially meat cooking. The Dutch "Pospas" has become our "Paspas" - it's not boiled rice with meat. The English "chop cutlet" names we have borrowed, but the method we have learned from the Portuguese.-English chop cutlet does not cook meat minced, it has bones and rib meat is used in it. Indo-Portuguese cuisine is prevalent in Goa and Chittagong, and Anglo-Indian cuisine, which is cooked by Englishmen and firings all over India - ghee-mixed masala, saffron-flavored pulao has taken place in European hotel restaurants.

Similarly, South Indian Babbuchi cuisine, which is available in a dilapidated state, can also be considered as a specimen of superior vegetarian cuisine of Odisha.

In some places in India, ancient cooking methods have been going on for seven-eight hundred, perhaps a thousand and a half years - the offerings given at the Jagannath Temple in Puri - since the twelfth century AD, have been going on unchanged for these few centuries. The same can be said about the offerings of many temples in India. The history of culinary art of India lies within these special discussions. Abul Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari mentions thirty kinds of food ingredients in Akbar Badshah's kitchen - ten kinds of pure vegetarian, ten kinds of mixed and non-vegetarian, ten seers of ghee, ten seers of misri, ten seers of semolina, and other halwa ingredients are proof of this Badshah's wishes.

In short, what the people of India eat, its discussion is amusing and instructive in many ways - there are many differences. But still there is a general Indianness. It must be admitted that the common characteristics of the Indian way of eating are maintained through the Indian restaurants in foreign countries - London, Paris, New York. In Indian restaurants, these things will be there - rice, lentils, vegetarian and non-vegetarian curry, pulao, halwa, fried vegetables, pakora, chutney, yogurt, "rasgulla", "gulab jamun", etc. Some people consider papad to be an all-Indian thing. The word is found in Sanskrit as "pappata" - it is an onomatopoeic word. From "parpata" to "pappad-panpar" then papad, panpar, papadum, popadum, etc. are its modern forms. But it seems that this popular Indian food made from lentil powder is originally from the Dravidian community, and in Tamil, the word "pap" means lentils.

There is no doubt that a large part of Indian food is based on the food items and cooking methods of the pre-Aryan era.

Suniti Kumar Chatterjee (1890-1977): Linguist, polyglot, and National Professor. Born in Shibpur, Howrah. He is remembered for writing the book "The Origin and Development of the Bengali Language." Other books written by him include "Dwipmoy Bharat o Shyamdesh," "Europe Bhraman," "Sanskritiki," etc. His autobiography "Jiban Katha" is a very enjoyable book. "Path-Chalti" is the story of his world tour.
