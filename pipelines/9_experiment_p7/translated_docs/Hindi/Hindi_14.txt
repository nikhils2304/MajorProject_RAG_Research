The India-UAE Strategic Path This article is based on the article titled "India, ' deepen ties with mega defense plan, LNG deal" published in The Hindu on 20/01/2026. This article analyzes the gradual development of India-UAE relations, highlighting the journey from cultural and trade relations to a comprehensive strategic partnership. It sheds light on the achievements, challenges and future plans in the changing global landscape. India-UAE relations have developed as a major strategic partnership. Under the India-UAE CEPA, bilateral trade has exceeded $100 billion in FY 2024-25 and a new target has been set to reach $200 billion by 2032. The UAE has emerged as India's third largest trading partner and a major source of Foreign Direct Investment (FDI). The partnership's cooperation has expanded beyond the oil sector to new areas such as the 10-year LNG agreement, Fintech (UPI–AANI & RuPay–JAYWAN), and the India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC). Recent talks on strategic defense partnership, including defense industrial cooperation and interoperability, have further strengthened it. Based on a large Indian diaspora of over 3.5 million, this partnership is transforming economic interdependence into permanent geopolitical convergence. How have India-UAE relations changed over time? § Phase I: Commercial and Cultural Era (Pre-1971): o This phase was shaped by geography-based relations rather than formal diplomacy, in which the Arabian Sea enabled a borderless maritime economy. o India was the economic presenter of these relations and the Indian Rupee continued to be used as the de facto currency in the Trucial States until 1966. o Cultural relations were established through traditional dhow (an ancient maritime network connecting the Arabian Peninsula, East Africa and South Asia for over 2,000 years) based trade, where Indian merchants were an integral part of Gulf society, not immigrants. o This phase shows that India–UAE relations are cultural and predate the oil boom, making today's partnership a reiteration of historical continuity. • The devaluation of the Indian Rupee in 1966 established a major economic separation for the first time, pushing the Gulf countries towards independent currencies and sovereignty. § Phase II: 'Transactional' Era (1971-2000): o After the formation of the UAE, relations became entirely transactional, focusing on oil exports and Indian migrant labor. o Geopolitically, Cold War-era alliances created distance, as the UAE leaned towards the US-Saudi bloc and was viewed from Pakistan's perspective due to India's Soviet proximity. o Security relations reached their lowest point as Dubai emerged as a hub for smuggling and anti-India elements, further increasing distrust in New Delhi. o Indian workers boosted the UAE's development during the 'Gulf Dream', but were seen only as an economic resource, not a source of soft power. o This phase illustrates strategic short-sightedness, exemplified by the UAE's support for Pakistan on the Kashmir issue in forums such as the Organization of Islamic Cooperation. § Phase III: Economic Awakening and 'Benign Neglect' (2000-2014): o This period saw growth in trade but political engagement between India and the UAE remained stagnant. o Politically, the paradox of 'high trade, low politics' existed, where there were strong commercial ties but minimal high-level diplomatic access. o Energy relations remained transactional, limited only to buyer-seller, lacking strategic depth such as joint reserves or downstream investment. o The deportation of Aftab Ansari in 2002 signaled a quiet shift, with the UAE distancing itself from being a safe haven for terrorism and initiating covert security cooperation with India. § Phase IV: Strategic Reorganization and 'Golden Age' (2015-Present): o This phase marks a decisive shift from transactional relations to a broad strategic partnership, driven by shared realistic interests in the post-America Middle East. o The Prime Minister's visit to the UAE in 2015 ended a 34-year hiatus, condemned state-sponsored terrorism and re-established the UAE as India's global connectivity hub through IMEC and I2U2. o Geopolitically, the UAE has separated India from Pakistan, evidenced by inviting India as a special guest to the Organization of Islamic Cooperation in 2019 despite Pakistan's boycott. o The Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) was signed in record time (88 days of talks) in 2022. o In January 2026, the two countries signed a Letter of Intent for a formal strategic defense partnership. What are the areas of current development in India–UAE relations? § Advanced Economic Partnership: Bilateral economic relations are no longer limited to commodity exchange, but are moving towards deeper institutional integration, aimed at protecting both economies from global instability. This is credited to the Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA). § In January 2026, the two countries set a target of increasing annual trade to $200 billion by 2032, building on the $100 billion achievement in FY 2024-25. o By doubling this trade target, both countries are expressing their commitment to establishing an economic corridor in the Global South that bypasses traditional Western trade dependencies. § Initiatives such as 'India Mart' and 'India-Africa Bridge' in Jebel Ali Free Zone (JAFZA) are being rapidly integrated to integrate Indian Small and Medium Enterprises into the UAE's global re-export network. § Strategic Defense Axis: India and the UAE are moving towards a formal security architecture, reflecting shared strategic autonomy and a collective approach against cross-border terrorism. This shift indicates that the UAE now considers India as a primary partner for maritime security and defense industrial innovation in the Indian Ocean, rather than traditional regional allies. o For example, a Letter of Intent (LOI) was signed in January 2026 for a strategic defense partnership, including defense industrial cooperation and interoperability. o Joint exercises such as Desert Cyclone and Desert Flag now involve multi-domain operations, real-time interoperability, and complex warfare scenarios involving high-intensity air-land coordination, marking a shift away from symbolic exercises towards real war-fighting preparedness. § From Crude Oil to Clean Energy Partnership: Energy relations have now moved beyond the 'transactional crude oil' phase to embrace long-term energy security and the 'SHANTI' (Sustainable Exploitation and Development of Nuclear Energy) framework. o By venturing into the field of civil nuclear cooperation and Small Modular Reactors (SMR), the UAE is strengthening its position as a major financier and partner in India's green energy transition. • For example, HPCL entered into a 10-year LNG supply agreement with ADNOC in 2026, under which 0.5 million metric tons of LNG will be supplied annually from 2028. • At the same time, both countries are collaborating on Small Modular Reactors (SMR) to leverage India's new nuclear policies and the UAE's Barakah nuclear expertise. o Complementing this shift, India and the UAE are aligning their climate strategies to co-develop a green hydrogen value chain, combining India's manufacturing prowess with the UAE's capital to create a sustainable 'green corridor' for the Global South. § Fintech and Digital Alliance: Financial and digital connectivity has now become the 'new oil' and both nations aim to create a sovereign digital payments corridor that challenges the dominance of Western financial systems. o The establishment of 'digital embassies' and supercomputing clusters highlights a transition towards higher level technological interdependence. o The integration of India's RuPay card with the UAE's Jaywan card system has revolutionized remittances for over 3.5 million Indian expatriates. o In addition, the two sides have now agreed in principle to establish a supercomputing cluster in India through cooperation between C-DAC (India) and G42 (UAE). • Integrated with the AI India Mission, this facility will support public and private research, application development and commercial innovation. § Infrastructure Cooperation- 'Greenfield' Asset Transfer: Investment strategy has now evolved from passive portfolio holdings to active 'Greenfield asset creation'. o The aim of this change is to physically integrate Indian manufacturing zones (such as Gujarat) with the Jebel Ali transshipment hub, thereby operating the logistics chain from start to finish by bypassing global blockades, effectively operating the IMEC corridor. • For example, the UAE has pledged to develop the Dholera Special Investment Region (Gujarat), which includes a new international airport and Greenfield port. At the same time, DP World has pledged to provide an additional $5 billion to upgrade India's port logistics network. § Space Cooperation- From 'Launch Client' to 'Co-Developer': Space cooperation has now moved beyond a purely transactional 'buyer-seller' model (UAE pays for launch) to transform into a 'co-development' partnership, aimed at capturing the global commercial payload market. o The two nations are building a shared industrial base by combining ISRO's cost-effective engineering and UAE's capital, moving beyond state-level science missions to focus on private sector integration and human space flight training. • For example, in January 2026, IN-SPACe and the UAE Space Agency jointly signed a historic Letter of Intent (LoI) to jointly develop commercial launch facilities. § 'Knowledge Corridor' and Skill Mobility: This relationship has shifted from a 'labor-export' model to a high-level 'knowledge partnership', aimed at creating a seamless ecosystem for academic excellence and professional certification. o This transformation ensures that the Indian diaspora in the UAE is no longer just a workforce, but the primary driver of the UAE's transition to a post-oil, innovation-based economy. o For example, after the opening of IIT Delhi-Abu Dhabi in 2024, the student strength in this campus will be increased to 400 by the end of 2026 and specialized Masters programs in AI will also be started. o This is supported by the 2025 Memorandum of Understanding on Mutual Recognition of Qualifications, which enables the mobility of professionals in the 3.5 million+ Indian community. § Cultural and Sports Diplomacy: This relationship has moved beyond transactional diplomacy to become a cultural partnership, in which the UAE has emerged as an extension of India's cultural expansion. This approach follows a dual strategy: o Ensuring long-term affiliation of the Indian diaspora through unprecedented religious and cultural recognition and moving beyond the perception of a 'temporary workforce'. o Providing a neutral and reliable platform for managing sensitive regional relations between India and Pakistan. o For example, the BAPS Hindu temple, built on 27 acres of land donated by the President of the UAE, was inaugurated in February 2024. The UAE has also strengthened its position as a stable neutral venue for sensitive sporting events between India and Pakistan. What are the main areas of differences in India-UAE relations? § Structural Trade Imbalance and CEPA Misapplication: Despite implementing zero-duty access by the Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA), India still faces the challenge of export diversification beyond low value-added sectors such as gems and jewelry in order to balance the heavy energy import bill. o This structural inequality forces India to constantly negotiate non-tariff barriers and rules of origin in order to prevent "pass-through" of third-party goods, while the UAE's trade surplus creates complexities in fully adopting the local currency settlement mechanism. • For example, in FY 2025, while bilateral trade exceeded $100 billion, India's trade deficit remained at approximately $26 billion, mainly due to oil imports. o In addition, India fears that the UAE is becoming a conduit for third-party goods (such as gold/silver), which can avoid duties, thus forcing India to further tighten its scrutiny, which is hindering trade. § Geopolitical Sensitivity of the IMEC Corridor: Disruption in the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC) highlights the sensitivity of India's regional connectivity strategy to West Asian instability, particularly the Israel-Gaza conflict. o This has hampered the physical integration of the UAE-Israel phase and India's 'Gateway to Europe' has become a hostage of regional insecurity. As a result, New Delhi has had to rely on long and expensive sea routes, while also maintaining diplomatic balance with Iran and the Arab world. § 'Hydrocarbon-Renewable' Energy Paradox: India faces the dual pressure of securing immediate supplies of fossil fuels to power its growing economy, while also partnering with the UAE on green hydrogen, creating a policy collision where long-term decarbonization goals compete with short-term energy security needs. o This relationship is trapped in a transitional phase where India will have to deepen its dependence on the UAE's oil and LNG infrastructure, while also trying to compete with the UAE as a global green hydrogen export hub. • For example, Indian entities have entered into a 10-year LNG supply agreement (1.2 million metric tons per year) with ADNOC, further strengthening dependence on fossil fuels, while both countries are pursuing mutually conflicting net zero 2070/2050 targets. § Financial Interoperability and Risks of Rupee Accumulation: The Local Currency Settlement System (LCSS) faces a liquidity constraint, where UAE exporters accumulate Indian Rupees (INR) from their trade surplus, but do not have sufficient means to reinvest extensively in India's bond or equity markets. o This 'stuck liquidity' creates reluctance among UAE banks to completely eliminate the dollar peg, thus limiting the mechanism to symbolic transactions rather than becoming the main trade settlement standard. § Labor Market Nationalization vs. Immigrant Dependence: The UAE's Emiratization campaign poses a structural risk to the 3.5 million Indian workforce, especially in white-collar sectors, which sustain India's billions of dollars in annual remittance flows. o Large companies were required to achieve 8% Emiratization by the end of 2025, non-compliance of which now attracts annual financial penalties, indirectly harming the Indian workforce as it limits recruitment flexibility and prioritizes national employment. o Although blue-collar employment remains relatively safe, a decline in professional opportunities signals long-term pressure on India's remittance-based foreign exchange reserves. § 'Paperwork to Project Outcome' Investment Delays: A recurring hurdle is the performance gap between the UAE's vast sovereign commitments (ADIA/Mubadala) and the slow pace of actual capital allocation. This gap is often exacerbated by obstacles such as fears of retroactive taxation in India and lengthy dispute-resolution processes, which discourage long-term infrastructure investment compared to fast-paced Western markets. o Out of the stipulated $75 billion investment target, actual Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) is only about $22 billion (as of March 2025) and recent talks on reviewing the bilateral investment treaty have focused on bypassing the 'termination of local measures' clause, specifically to accelerate the remaining capital flow. o For example, despite ADNOC investing capital, the Ratnagiri Refinery project has been stalled due to land disputes. § Defense Strategic Divergence—Military vs. Diplomatic Stance: A strategic inconsistency remains despite increased maritime cooperation. Unlike India's direct Naval assertiveness in the Red Sea, the UAE prioritizes cautious, non-partisan diplomacy to address regional proxy powers. This divergence limits the scope of joint kinetic operations despite shared threats. o For example, defense-related outcomes in recent talks have been limited to defense industrial manufacturing (drones/AI) rather than joint patrols. • India's deployment of 40 warships in the Red Sea continues to operate independently of the UAE's naval assets, highlighting the reality of 'coordination without integration'. § China's Influence and Strategic Defensiveness: The UAE's engagement with China in technological and military ties (Huawei 5G, port infrastructure) poses a serious security dilemma for India. This complicates the international, American and international (I2U2) framework and sensitive technology transfer. o For example, several Chinese companies have also invested in manufacturing and trading units within the Economic Zone Abu Dhabi Group. What measures can be taken to strengthen India-UAE relations? § Institutionalizing a 'Sovereign Fast-Track' Mediation Mechanism: To resolve the 'investment delays' issue, India should establish a special dispute resolution corridor within GIFT City specifically for UAE sovereign wealth funds (ADIA, Mubadala). o This will enable the spirit of the bilateral investment treaty to be implemented, bypassing the long delays in India's lower courts, thereby transforming reluctant capital into active, long-term 'Greenfield' infrastructure assets. § 'Rupee-Dirham Corporate Bond' Market Creation: To address the LCSS liquidity-related constraint, policymakers should facilitate the listing of high-yield Indian 'Masala Bonds' or green infrastructure bonds in the Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM). o This financial innovation will help UAE banks reinvest their accumulated surplus rupees in yield-generating Indian assets rather than converting them into dollars, thereby transforming the local currency settlement system from a symbolic trade instrument to a robust, cyclical investment instrument. § Implementing a 'Strategic Food Security Treaty' including Sanctions Waivers: India should go beyond general assurances and sign a binding treaty that grants the UAE specific, quota-based exemptions from ad hoc agricultural export restrictions (rice/wheat/sugar). o In exchange for this 'supply chain immunity', the UAE would be contractually obligated to fully fund the stalled I2U2 food parks, creating a closed 'farm-to-port' corridor that guarantees price stability for Indian farmers and food sovereignty for the Emirates. § Accelerating the transition to 'co-production' of the defense industrial base: The defense partnership needs to shift from a 'buyer-seller' dynamic to a 'joint intellectual property' model, especially targeting drone warfare, AI surveillance and desert warfare electronics. The defense partnership should move beyond the ‘buyer-seller’ framework towards a ‘joint intellectual property’ model, especially in the areas of drone warfare, AI-based surveillance and Desert Warfare electronics. o By combining India's frugal engineering and software efficiency with the UAE's advanced component procurement network, the two countries can build a 'shared strategic autonomy' that reduces dependence on Western or Chinese OEMs for critical security needs in the Indian Ocean region. § Establishing an 'International Skill Harmonization' Protocol: To combat the risks of Emiratization, India should co-create a 'skill passport' system where Indian vocational certifications are pre-validated by the UAE National Qualifications Authority. o This measure will actively upgrade the Indian blue-collar workforce into 'grey-collar' technicians (automation, HVAC, clean energy), ensuring they remain indispensable to the UAE's post-oil 'knowledge economy', while securing high-value remittances for the Indian economy. Conclusion: India–UAE relations have moved beyond transactional pragmatism to emerge as a mature strategic partnership based on trust, technology, and shared geopolitical realism. Navigating the volatile West Asian landscape remains challenging for both countries; their growing consensus on connectivity, clean energy, and digital public infrastructure signals a shift towards co-construction from dependency. The real challenge lies in how visionary frameworks can be transformed into actionable projects that can ensure mutual strategic autonomy. If this partnership continues to be persistent and robust, it can redefine the integration of South-West Asia and emerge as an ideal model of adaptive cooperation in a multipolar world.
