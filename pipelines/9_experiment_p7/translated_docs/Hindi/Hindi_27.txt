Why Manufacturing Has Lagged in India: Introduction: Despite starting the 20th century with similar income levels as China and South Korea, India's manufacturing sector has stagnated, limiting job creation, productivity growth, and broad income expansion. The Current State of Manufacturing in India (with Data): • Low and Stagnant GDP Share: Manufacturing contributes ~15% of India's GDP (World Bank, 2023), while it was ~27% in China and ~25% in South Korea during their peak industrialization phases. • Weak Employment Absorption: According to the Periodic Labour Force Survey (2022–23), manufacturing employs only ~11.6–12% of India's workforce, significantly lower than East Asian countries at their development stage. • Service-Led Growth: India's service sector contributes over 55% of GDP but employs a much smaller share of workers, leading to jobless or low-quality job growth. • Rising Inequality: Oxfam (2023) reported that India's top 10% hold over 77% of national wealth, indicating growth without widespread job or wage benefits. Reasons for Manufacturing Underperformance: • Public Sector Wages and the 'Dutch Disease' Effect: Economist Arvind Subramanian argues that relatively high government salaries increased wages and prices across the economy. Low-productivity manufacturing firms couldn't match these wages, making Indian manufacturing less competitive. • Real Exchange Rate Pressure: Higher domestic prices increased imports and reduced export price competitiveness, even if the rupee's nominal exchange rate didn't appreciate sharply. • The Cheap Labor Trap: Abundant labor in India reduced the incentive for firms to invest in automation and productivity-enhancing technology. • Evidence from ASI: The Annual Survey of Industries (2022–23) shows that fixed capital increased by 10.6%, while employment increased by only 7.4%, increasing capital per worker to ₹23.6 lakh, indicating capital deepening without large-scale job creation. Why Didn't High Wages Inspire Innovation? • Missed 'Induced Innovation' Process: In countries like Britain, Germany, and South Korea, high wages motivated firms to innovate. In India, the manufacturing sector failed to respond similarly. • Stagnant Private Sector Wages: Entry-level salaries in IT and manufacturing-related services have shown minimal real growth since the early 2000s (ILO and NITI Aayog study), even with rapid firm-level expansion. • Platform Economy without Productivity Gains: Many Indian unicorns (food delivery, ride-hailing) rely on labor abundance rather than technological upgrades, reinforcing a low-wage equilibrium. The Way Forward: • Technology-Led Industrialization: Promote the adoption of Industry 4.0 technologies – automation, robotics, AI, and advanced manufacturing – through targeted incentives and R&D support. • Human Capital and Skill Intensification: Align skill missions with industrial needs, focusing on technical education, apprenticeships, and continuous reskilling. • Labor Market Reforms with Security: Balance flexibility and social security to encourage formal employment and productivity-linked wage growth. • Strengthening the Industrial Ecosystem: Develop integrated manufacturing clusters with plug-and-play infrastructure, logistics connectivity, and supplier networks, beyond existing coastal hubs. • MSME Upgradation and Scaling: Support MSMEs in technology adoption, access to credit, and integration into global value chains. • Stable and Predictable Policy Regime: Ensure continuity in industrial, trade, and tax policies to reduce uncertainty and encourage long-term investment. • Export Competitiveness with Value Addition: Focus on higher-value manufacturing from low-cost exports through standards, quality upgrades, and innovation. • Balanced Wage Policy: Promote productivity-aligned wage growth to incentivize innovation, rather than suppressing wages through labor abundance. • Public-Private Collaboration: Leverage partnerships between government, industry, and academia to accelerate innovation, technology diffusion, and skill development. Conclusion: India's manufacturing lag stems not just from policy choices like high public sector wages, but from a deeper failure to induce technological upgrades. Without productivity-led manufacturing growth, India risks continued jobless growth, rising inequality, and incomplete structural transformation. What is the Bureau of Port Security (BoPS)? • Statutory Basis: The BOPS is constituted under Section 13 of the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025, as a dedicated regulatory authority for port and ship security. • Administrative Control: It operates under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping, and Waterways, modeled on the Bureau of Civil Aviation Security. • Core Mandate: The BOPS provides regulatory oversight, coordination, and standard-setting for the security of ships, ports, and port facilities in major and non-major ports. • International Compliance: It is empowered to enforce global norms such as the International Ship and Port Facility Security Code, ensuring India’s ports meet international maritime security standards. Why was the BOPS Needed? (Challenges in Coastal Security): • Fragmented Security Architecture: Currently, responsibility for coastal and port security is divided among multiple agencies – the Indian Coast Guard, Central Industrial Security Force, State Marine Police, and Navy – leading to coordination gaps and response delays. • Expanding Threat Spectrum: India faces rising risks of maritime terrorism, arms and narcotics smuggling, human trafficking, illegal immigration, sea piracy, and illegal fishing. The increasing digitalization of ports has also exposed vulnerabilities to cyber-attacks on port IT systems. • Rapid Maritime Growth: According to official data, cargo handling in India increased from 974 MMT in 2014 to 1,594 MMT in 2025; inland waterways cargo grew eightfold to 145.5 MMT. Higher traffic intensifies security risks if governance doesn't keep pace. • Lack of a Single Regulator: Previously, no single statutory body existed specifically for port security regulation, audit, and compliance monitoring. How the BOPS Addresses These Challenges: • Single-Point Regulatory Authority: The BOPS acts as the nodal body for security oversight, reducing inter-agency overlaps and closing coordination gaps. • Standardization of Security Protocols: Under the BOPS, the CISF has been designated as a Recognized Security Organisation to prepare a uniform security plan, conduct risk assessments, and train personnel across all ports. • Tiered Security Framework: Security measures will be implemented based on threat perception, ensuring flexibility without compromising vigilance. • Focus on Cyber Security: The BOPS is expected to host a dedicated cyber security division to safeguard port IT and logistics systems in coordination with national cyber agencies. • Information Sharing and Intelligence Coordination: The BOPS will facilitate the collection and exchange of maritime security intelligence, strengthening preventive and deterrent capabilities. Linkages with India’s Maritime Vision and Legal Reforms: • Maritime India Vision 2030: The BOPS aligns with India’s goal of developing “world-class port infrastructure,” where security is integral to efficiency and investor confidence. • Modern Port Laws: The formation of the BOPS complements the enactment of the Indian Ports Act, 2025 (replacing the Indian Ports Act, 1908), along with the Coastal Shipping Act, 2025, aimed at enhancing ease of doing business, safety, and stability. • Global Reputation: Nine Indian ports are now featured in the World Bank’s Container Port Performance Index, making robust security administration essential to maintain credibility. Concerns and Criticisms: • Maritime Federalism: Coastal states have raised concerns that the new port laws expand the Centre's control over non-major ports, potentially undermining state autonomy. • Inspection Powers: Critics argue that the broad inspection and entry powers under the new laws lack clear judicial safeguards, raising civil liberties concerns. • Implementation Capacity: The BOPS’ effectiveness will depend on staffing, technical expertise, and seamless coordination with existing maritime forces. The Way Forward: • Clear Centre-State coordination protocols to ensure uniform security standards while addressing federal concerns. • Capacity building through specialized training in maritime and cyber security. • Robust accountability and audit mechanisms to balance security powers with procedural safeguards. • Technology integration using AI, surveillance systems, and real-time data sharing for proactive threat detection. Conclusion: The Bureau of Port Security represents a significant institutional reform to match India’s growing maritime footprint with a coherent security framework. Its success will depend on cooperative federalism, technological capabilities, and transparent governance. Source: The Hindu
