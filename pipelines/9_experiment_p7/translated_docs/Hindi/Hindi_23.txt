Making India's Energy Security Future-Oriented This article is based on the article titled "From energy scarcity to abundance" published in The Financial Express on 02/01/2026. It underlines how the world is moving towards an era of energy surplus, inspired by technological innovation, which provides India with an important opportunity to reduce its dependence on imported oil. India's energy security is a central determinant of its economic stability, strategic autonomy and long-term development path. Despite being the world's third largest energy consumer, India relies on imports for about 85% of its crude oil, more than 50% of its natural gas and about 25% of its coal to meet its energy needs, making the economy vulnerable to global price volatility and geopolitical risks. With energy demand projected to double by 2040 due to urbanization, industrialization and rising incomes, the main challenge is to ensure that energy supply remains accessible, reliable and sustainable. In this context, India's commitment to the targets of 500 GW of non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030 and net-zero emissions by 2070 makes it clear that contemporary energy security is now inseparable from clean energy transition, diversification of supply sources and technological self-reliance. What is the current status of India's energy structure? § Energy Demand: India is currently the third largest energy consumer globally and contributes about 6-7% to global primary energy demand. Rapid urbanization, industrial expansion, rising incomes and electrification of transport and domestic sectors are continuously increasing energy demand. § India's total energy demand is expected to almost double by 2040, making energy availability a central developmental issue. § Primary Energy Mix: India's total primary energy consumption includes various fuel sources, reflecting traditional and modern energy needs in the industrial, transport, residential and commercial sectors, which according to the latest estimates of the International Energy Agency are as follows - o Coal: Approximately 46% of total energy supply (2023) o Oil (Petroleum and other liquid fuels): Approximately 25% of total energy supply (2023) o Natural Gas: Approximately 5% of total energy supply (2023) o Renewable Energy: 22% share in energy production (2022) What are the major improvements in India's energy sector? § Universal Energy Access: One of the most transformative achievements of India's energy sector has been the assurance of near-universal electricity access. o According to the latest data from the Ministry of Power, all villages and homes now have electricity access, a historic achievement in rural development. • As a result, per capita electricity consumption has increased in India (18,410 MW joules/person in financial year 2023-24). This indicates better access and improvement in living standards, although it is still lower than the global average, it still indicates steady progress towards energy equality. o Rural electrification has enabled irrigation, cold storage, digital connectivity, education and health services, directly improving productivity and quality of life. § Rapid Pace in Clean Energy Transition: India has recorded its highest ever increase of 44.5 GW in renewable energy capacity till November 2025, which is almost double the increase of the previous year. As a result, non-fossil sources now account for more than 51% of installed power capacity, enabling India to achieve Paris Agreement targets ahead of schedule. o Key initiatives driving India's clean energy transition include PM-Kusum, Green Energy Corridor, PLI schemes for Solar PV and PM e-Drive scheme. • In addition, the Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation (SATAT) initiative is transforming agricultural waste into a strategic energy resource, reducing LNG imports along with air pollution. § Significant Progress in Energy Storage: Taking into account the intermittency of green grids, India has taken steps towards developing large-scale energy storage capacity. o This transformation ensures that renewable energy can provide continuous, stable supply during industrial and high demand times. o For example, India's battery energy storage capacity is expected to increase from 507 megawatt hours in 2025 to approximately 5 gigawatt hours (GWh) in 2026, representing an almost tenfold increase. § Improved Grid Strength and System Reliability: The "One Nation, One Grid, One Frequency" framework has evolved as a high capacity highway for green energy, greatly reducing regional power shortages and price volatility. o Green Energy Corridors and digital grid management systems have improved system adaptability and reduced power outages. o Meeting more than half of the daily demand of renewable energy on high-peak days demonstrates the maturity of the system. • The Central Electricity Authority (CEA) estimates that regional interconnections could increase by 50 percent by 2030, helping India export surplus renewable energy. § Development of Decentralized and Distributed Energy Systems: Decentralized energy solutions have expanded rapidly, especially in rural and agricultural areas. Rooftop solar panels, solar pumps and microgrids are reducing transmission losses and empowering communities with local energy production. o For example, under the Pradhan Mantri Surya Ghar: Free Electricity Scheme, solar panels have been installed on the roofs of 26,76,432 houses, generating a cumulative installed capacity of 7,879.07 MW. o These systems increase energy adaptability, support agricultural income and promote sustainable rural livelihoods. § Progress in Biofuel and Ethanol Blending: India is making relentless efforts towards fuel self-reliance by converting its agricultural surplus into high-octane fuel, effectively transforming the agricultural sector into an energy center. o Rapid implementation of the 20% ethanol blending (E20) target is a dual-purpose strategy of foreign exchange conservation and rural wealth creation. o For example, more than 1,000 crore liters of ethanol have been blended during Ethanol Supply Year (ESY) 2024-25, while the blending rate was 19.97% in October 2025. § Nuclear Energy and SMR Adoption: To strengthen energy transition in India, the Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Act, 2025 has historically opened the nuclear sector for private investment. o India's approach is focused on small modular reactor (SMR) technology to provide reliable, carbon-free, base-load energy for heavy industries where reducing pollution is challenging. o Rs 20,000 crore has been allocated for the nuclear energy mission in the Union Budget 2025-26, with the aim of achieving 100 GW capacity by 2047. § Promoting Green Hydrogen: India is working towards becoming a competitive global exporter of green molecules by leveraging its ultra-low cost renewable energy, with the aim of fully decarbonizing fertilizers and steel. o The National Green Hydrogen Mission is no longer limited to policy frameworks, but is moving towards physical implementation of dedicated coastal 'hydrogen hubs'. • Three major ports - Deendayal Port Authority (Gujarat), V.O. Chidambaranar Port Authority (Tamil Nadu) and Paradip Port Authority (Odisha) were recognized as Green Hydrogen Hubs in October 2025. What are the major issues related to India's energy sector? § Continued Dependence on Fossil Fuels: Despite the growth of renewable energy, petroleum still accounts for about 27% of primary energy consumption and India depends on imports for about 85% of its crude oil needs. § This makes the Indian economy vulnerable to global price volatility and external shocks, affecting inflation, trade balance and fiscal stability. Despite diversification in energy sources, India still has high dependence on imports. o At the same time, coal remains India's largest single energy source, with an installed capacity of 219 GW. o This dual dependence on imported fuel and domestic coal highlights the challenges of ensuring energy security, managing environmental impacts and accelerating the transition to cleaner and more sustainable energy sources. § Gap between Renewable Energy Production vs. Capacity Utilization: Although non-fossil fuel capacity has exceeded 50% of total installed power capacity, actual renewable power generation is low due to uncertainty and storage limitations. o Currently, renewable energy contributes approximately 20–25% (approximately 22% in financial year 2024–25) to total electricity generation, highlighting the gap between installed capacity and reliable electricity generation. • Seasonal variability, limited energy storage and grid balancing challenges impede this use. § Grid Integration and Infrastructure Limitations: Rapid increase in renewable energy capacity puts pressure on transmission and distribution infrastructure. o Integrating variable resources such as solar and wind requires better grid adaptation, accurate forecasting and expanded transmission networks in the states. • For example, more than 50 GW of installed renewable energy capacity is underutilized due to transmission constraints, where production assets are technically ready, but energy cannot be efficiently transferred because transmission lines, substations and evacuation routes have not been expanded in line with capacity expansion. o Although investments in Green Energy Corridors and smart grid technologies are ongoing, delays in implementation and inter-state coordination issues can impede the smooth integration of renewable energy. § Land Acquisition and Right of Way (RoW) Disputes: Securing large, contiguous land parcels for "giga-scale" solar parks is causing community opposition and legal disputes over fertile agricultural land and grazing rights. o These conflicts delay project progress and lead to costly litigation for developers, affecting the low tariff benefits of renewable energy. • For example, in June 2025, thousands of farmers protested against the government's plan to acquire 8,300 acres of fertile land for a solar project near Ramayapatnam port in Andhra Pradesh. § Financial Pressure in Electricity Distribution Companies: Many state distribution companies (DISCOM) are under financial pressure due to high aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) losses, cross-subsidies and delayed payments. o The aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) losses of DISCOMs increased from 15.4% in financial year 2023 to 17.6% in financial year 2024. o This affects their ability to invest in infrastructure, procure energy efficiently and implement reforms. o Although recent policies promote tariff rationalization and smart metering, the viability of DISCOMs for regional efficiency remains challenging. § Undeveloped Natural Gas Market: Natural gas currently contributes only 6.8% to primary energy consumption, well below the government's target of 15% by 2030. o Infrastructure constraints, limited pipeline networks and historically high LNG prices have slowed the growth in gas demand. • A robust natural gas ecosystem is essential for cleaner industrial processes, electricity generation and carbon emissions reduction, but its progress has been slower than expected. § Environmental and Climate-related Pressures: India's energy sector is a major contributor to air pollution and carbon emissions. o Although India's power sector CO₂ emissions have declined year-on-year by 1% in the first half of 2025, the sector remains a major contributor to air pollution and carbon emissions. o Balancing economic development and environmental objectives remains a complex policy challenge. o In addition, the continued delay in installing flue gas desulfurization (FGD) technology in coal-based power plants continues to pose a threat to national air quality and public health. § Supply Chain and Critical Minerals Related Risks: India's heavy dependence on imported critical minerals for electric vehicle (EV) batteries and high efficiency solar cells makes the energy transition vulnerable to severe geopolitical shocks and "resource nationalism". o The recent export restrictions imposed by major global suppliers (such as China) on rare-earth magnets from 2025 have forced Indian automobile manufacturers to cut production targets, highlighting a single point of failure. • For example, in mid-2025, Maruti Suzuki cut its short-term production targets for its first electric vehicle, the e-Vitara, by two-thirds due to a shortage of rare metals. What measures can India adopt to strengthen its energy security? § Diversification in Energy Structure: A key pillar of energy security is diversification. Currently, India imports about 85% of its crude oil and more than 50% of its natural gas, making it vulnerable to geopolitical risks and global price shocks. o Increasing the share of domestic coal along with renewable energy, nuclear energy and clean technologies can reduce dependence on imported fuels. o India's achievement of more than 50% non-fossil capacity demonstrates that diversification is already underway and needs to be accelerated. § Strengthening Grid Infrastructure and Energy Storage: Energy security is not limited to production, but its supply is also important. Investing in inter-state transmission networks, green energy corridors and smart grids strengthens system adaptability and stability. o Energy storage through pumped hydro, battery storage and emerging technologies is essential to manage renewable energy uncertainty and meet peak demand. § Increasing Domestic Production and Strategic Reserves: Increasing domestic production of coal, oil and gas is important for near and medium term security. o Also, expanding Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR) helps balance short-term supply disruptions. o India's existing SPR capacity, which meets several days of imports, is being expanded (such as Padur in Karnataka and Chandikhol in Odisha) to increase adaptability to global supply shocks. § Improving Energy Efficiency and Demand-Management: Energy security is strengthened not only by increasing supply, but also by reducing waste. India's energy intensity has decreased significantly in the last decade due to efficiency measures. o Programs such as LED lighting, energy-efficient appliances, industrial energy audits and time-of-use pricing are helping to control demand growth. o Lower energy intensity reduces import dependence and infrastructure pressure and supports sustainable development. § Electrification of Transport and Alternative Fuels: Transport is India's largest oil consuming sector. Promoting electric mobility supported by renewable energy-powered charging infrastructure can significantly reduce oil imports. o In parallel, biofuels, ethanol blending, compressed biogas and green hydrogen can diversify fuel options for transport and industry. o India's ethanol blending program has already exceeded 20% blending, reducing crude oil imports and increasing fuel security. § Strengthening Distribution Sector: Economically viable and efficient DISCOMs are essential for energy security. o Ongoing reforms such as smart metering, tariff normalization, reducing AT&C losses and market-based energy trading increase reliability and attract investment. o A robust distribution sector ensures that produced energy is efficiently delivered to consumers without any systemic pressure. § Domestic Manufacturing and Technological Self-reliance: Energy security is closely linked to technological self-reliance. o Expanding domestic production of solar modules, batteries, electrolyzers and electrical equipment under Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes reduces import dependence and strengthens supply chains. o This also creates employment opportunities and establishes India as a global clean energy hub. § Integration of Energy Security and Climate Goals: India's long-term energy security is in line with its commitments to net zero emissions by 2070. Clean energy, electrification and efficiency improvements balance growing demand with environmental sustainability. o Energy diplomacy, diverse sources of imports and regional energy cooperation further strengthen India's strategic position. Conclusion: India's energy transition is strongly promoting Sustainable Development Goal 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) by achieving universal electrification and rapidly increasing fossil fuel-free energy capacity to more than 50%. By reducing import dependence, improving grid reliability and promoting efficiency and clean technologies, energy is now becoming a driver of inclusive development rather than a barrier. The coming decade will determine how effectively India transforms this momentum into long-term security, affordable and sustainable energy.
