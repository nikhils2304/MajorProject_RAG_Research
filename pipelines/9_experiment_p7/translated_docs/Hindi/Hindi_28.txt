India–New Zealand Free Trade Agreement: A New Template for India’s Trade Diplomacy

Context (Introduction)

The recently concluded Free Trade Agreement (FTA) with New Zealand signals a deliberate shift in India’s trade strategy, prioritizing regional security, mobility, services, and investment over tariff liberalization.

Features

•	Fast-Tracked yet Cautious FTA: Negotiations began in March 2025 and concluded by December 2025, making it one of India’s fastest FTAs. Total bilateral trade was approximately US$2 billion (fiscal year 2024-25), but both sides aim to double it within five years.

•	Comprehensive Market Access: New Zealand agreed to zero-duty access on 100% (8,284 tariff lines) of Indian exports upon implementation. The average applied tariff of ~2.2% on Indian goods will be eliminated, benefiting textiles, leather, engineering goods, pharmaceuticals, plastics, and processed food products.

•	Strategic Tariff Exclusions: India has protected ~30% of tariff lines, notably dairy, sensitive agricultural products, sugar, and select metals – addressing concerns that led India to exit RCEP in 2019.

•	Focus on Investment and Services: New Zealand has committed to US$20 billion in investment over 15 years, moving the FTA beyond goods trade. It offers commitments in 118 service sectors, including Most Favored Nation (MFN) status in 139 sectors.

•	Breakthrough in Mobility and Education: A standout feature is a mobility pathway (three-year visas) for 5,000 skilled Indian professionals at any time, who can work in IT, healthcare, education, construction, and prestige professions (Ayush doctors, yoga instructors, chefs). New Zealand also allows unlimited entry of Indian students, 20-hour weekly work rights, and extended post-study visas – far exceeding the limited Working-Holiday quotas of past agreements like the Australia-India ECTA.

Why This FTA Matters: Key Benefits

•	People-Centric Trade Architecture: By linking mobility, education, and services with trade, this FTA builds a deeper socio-economic bridge, leveraging the 300,000-strong Indian diaspora (~5% of New Zealand’s population).

•	Agriculture with Protection: Action plans for apples, kiwifruit, and honey combine technology transfer, Centers of Excellence, and capacity building with tariff-rate quotas – balancing farmer protection and productivity gains.

•	Boost to MSMEs and Employment: Zero-duty access for labor-intensive sectors (textiles, leather, footwear, gems and jewelry) strengthens India’s export competitiveness and job creation.

•	Rules-Based Facilitation: Commitments on customs clearance (48 hours; 24 hours for perishables), electronic documentation, robust rules of origin, and expedited pharma regulatory pathways reduce non-tariff barriers.

Issues and Challenges

•	Non-Tariff Barriers (NTBs): Recognition of Indian qualifications, quality standards, and conformity assessments will determine real benefits – often an FTA weakness.

•	Awareness and Utilization: Past FTAs show low uptake due to lack of awareness among MSMEs; proactive outreach is essential.

•	Implementation Capacity: Delivering on agricultural action plans, TRQ monitoring, and services mobility requires sustained inter-ministerial coordination.

•	Modest Trade Base: With low initial trade volumes, core gains may be limited unless firms actively integrate into Oceania supply chains.

The Way Forward

•	Make it a Template, Not an Exception: Replicate the New Zealand model – selective tariff liberalization, strong service and mobility chapters, investment commitments, and regional security safeguards – in future FTAs.

•	Aggressive Push for NTB Resolution: Establish joint working groups for standards, mutual recognition, and rules-of-origin compliance.

•	Sector-Focused Promotion: Target MSMEs, education providers, healthcare firms, and IT services to exploit full preferences.

•	Leverage for Pacific Outreach: Use New Zealand as a gateway to Oceania and Pacific Island markets, aligning with India’s Indo-Pacific strategy.

Conclusion

The India-New Zealand FTA signals a mature, interest-driven trade diplomacy – balancing openness with protection, and goods with people-centric mobility. Its success will depend on implementation and NTB removal, determining whether it becomes a sustainable template for India’s future FTAs.

Women, Time Poverty and the AI Upskilling Gap in India

Context (Introduction)

Recent time-use survey data reveals that Indian women face acute time poverty due to unpaid care work, limiting their upskilling potential – a disparity that risks deepening women’s exclusion from quality employment in the AI-driven economy.

Current Situation: Women’s Labor Force Participation and Time Use

•	Low and Unequal Workforce Participation: India’s female labor force participation rate (FLFPR) is around 40% (Time Use Survey, 2024; PLFS trends) – well below the global average. Critically, much of this increase is driven by unpaid family work and low-wage self-employment, not secure wage employment.

•	Double Burden of Work: According to the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation’s Time Use Survey (2024), working women spend ~9.6 hours daily on a combination of paid and unpaid work, compared to ~8.6 hours for men.
o	Women’s total work soars above 70 hours per week during prime working ages (25-39 years).
o	Men’s work hours stay within 54-60 hours per week, with over 80% dedicated to paid work.

•	Unpaid Care as a Key Constraint: Women spend significantly more time on childcare, eldercare, cooking, cleaning, and household management, while men’s unpaid work remains lower and flatter across life stages.

•	Acute Time Shortage for Skills Development: Women spend 10 hours less per week than men on self-development, including education, skills upgrading, and wellness. This gap widens to 11-12 hours during peak career years – precisely when upskilling is most critical.

Why Women’s Low LFPR Persists: Structural Causes

•	Household Responsibilities as Primary Barrier: The Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) consistently shows that ~40% of women outside the labor force cite domestic duties as the main reason for non-participation.

•	Lack of Care Infrastructure: Limited access to affordable childcare, eldercare, piped water, clean cooking fuels, and safe transport increases the burden of unpaid work.

•	Informality and Job Quality: Women are over-represented in informal, low-productivity, and precarious jobs, offering limited returns on skills investment.

•	Social Norms and Gender Roles: Care work remains feminized regardless of income, education, or urbanization, reinforcing unequal time allocation.

How AI and Automation Exacerbate This Situation

•	High Automation Risk: Women are disproportionately employed in routine, low-skill jobs – clerical work, basic services, and informal activities – that are most vulnerable to AI-led automation.

•	Upskilling Requires Time – Women Lack It: The AI transition demands continuous learning. However, women’s time poverty directly restricts access to reskilling, trapping them in low-value work.

•	Algorithmic Bias Against Caregivers: AI-driven productivity metrics often disregard unpaid care responsibilities, potentially penalizing women for career breaks, flexible hours, or reduced availability.

•	Underestimation of Women’s Work in GDP: Women contribute only ~17% to India’s GDP, not due to lack of effort, but because unpaid labor – which is central to social reproduction – is excluded from national accounts.

Policy Tools and Government Initiatives

•	Gender Budgeting: India’s gender budget remains below 1% of GDP, limiting its transformative potential. Time-use data is not systematically incorporated into budget design.

•	Skills and AI Missions: Initiatives like the India AI mission and AI Career for Women aim to increase women’s participation in emerging technologies, but scale and reach remain limited.

•	Care Economy Programs: Schemes like Anganwadi services exist, but scope, quality, and urban applicability remain inadequate for working women.

The Way Forward

•	Recognize Time Poverty as an Economic Constraint: Explicitly integrate time-use survey data into labor, skills, and industrial policies.

•	Invest in Time-Saving Infrastructure: Expand affordable childcare, eldercare, piped water, clean energy, and safe public transport – shown globally to boost women’s labor participation.

•	Flexible and Lifelong Skilling Models: Design modular, localized, digital, and flexible training programs aligned to women’s time and mobility constraints.

•	Outcome-Based Work Cultures: Encourage employers to shift from hours-based to outcome-based evaluations, supported by right-to-disconnect and flexible work norms.

•	Revalue Care Work: Progressively incorporate unpaid care into economic planning and explore compensation, social security, or service replacement models.

Conclusion

India’s low female labor force participation is not a supply problem but a time poverty problem. In the AI age, where skills determine survival, women’s unpaid labor and time scarcity risk becoming the biggest structural barrier to inclusive growth. Unless women’s time is freed, valued, and mainstreamed into policy, India’s Viksit Bharat 2047 vision will remain fundamentally constrained.

Export Concentration and the Limits of Export-Led Growth in India

Recent export data shows robust national performance, but a disaggregated analysis reveals rising regional concentration and weak employment outcomes, questioning the long-held assumption that export growth automatically drives broad-based industrialization and labor absorption.

Current Situation: Exports Concentrated in Few States

•	High Regional Concentration: Data from the Reserve Bank of India’s Statistical Handbook on Indian States (2024-25) shows that five states – Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Uttar Pradesh – account for nearly 70% of India’s total exports, up from about 65% five years ago.

•	Core–Periphery Export Geography: India’s export engine shows increasing concentration in the western and southern coastal belt, while large parts of northern and eastern India remain weakly integrated into global trade.

•	Rising Concentration Index: The rising Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) of export geography indicates clustering rather than diffusion of export capacity.

•	Misleading National Averages: Aggregate export growth conceals deepening inter-state divergence, creating an illusion of broad-based success while lagging regions are decoupled from trade-led development.

Reasons for Export Concentration Across States

•	Agglomeration and Supply-Chain Clustering: Firms gravitate towards states with established ports, logistics, supplier networks, and skilled labor pools, reinforcing first-mover advantages.

•	Financial Asymmetries: RBI credit-deposit (CD) ratios are above 90% in export hubs like Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh, indicating strong recycling of local savings into local industry. In contrast, states like Bihar and Eastern Uttar Pradesh remain below 50%, indicating capital flight from the hinterland to coastal hubs.

•	Human Capital and State Capacity Gaps: Low-exporting states consistently face deficits in education, health, skills, logistics, and industrial governance, limiting their entry into high-value export ecosystems.

•	Global Capital Preferences: In today’s trade environment, capital seeks economic complexity and ecosystem depth, not just cheap labor – these advantages are already concentrated in industrialized states.

Why the Export-Led Model Is Losing Its Transformative Power

•	Slowdown in Global Trade Volumes: Global merchandise trade growth has structurally slowed to 0.5-3%, according to the World Trade Organization, limiting space for late industrializers.

•	Global Export Concentration: The top 10 exporters control nearly 55% of global merchandise trade, according to a UN Trade and Development (2023) report, increasing barriers to entry.

•	Capital-Intensive Export Growth: Data from the Annual Survey of Industries (2022-23) shows that fixed capital (10.6%) is growing faster than employment (7.4%), with capital per employee rising to ₹23.6 lakhs, indicating automation and capital deepening.

•	Stagnant Manufacturing Employment: The Periodic Labour Force Survey shows that manufacturing employment is stuck at ~11.6-12% of the total workforce, despite record export values.

•	Collapsing Employment Elasticity: Exports are increasingly generating value without large-scale job creation, bypassing the labor-intensive industrialization phase that underpinned East Asian development.

•	Exports as Outcome, Not Driver: States export because they already have industrial depth, not because exports create development – reversing the classical development sequence.

The Way Forward: Rethinking Export and Industrial Strategy

•	Go Beyond Export Value Metrics: Track employment elasticity, wage share, regional diffusion, and economic complexity, not just export volumes.

•	Target Lagging State Capacity: Invest in logistics, industrial infrastructure, skills, urban clusters, and governance capabilities in low-exporting states.

•	Labor-Absorptive Industrial Policy: Prioritize sectors with high employment multipliers alongside capital-intensive champions.

•	Complexity-Building Approach: Enable gradual entry of lagging regions into dense “product spaces” through supplier development, technology diffusion, and MSME upgrading.

Conclusion

India’s export growth increasingly reflects accumulated industrial advantage rather than serving as a pathway for inclusive development. Without reorienting export and industrial policy towards job creation and regional convergence, exports risk deepening inequality rather than delivering structural transformation.
