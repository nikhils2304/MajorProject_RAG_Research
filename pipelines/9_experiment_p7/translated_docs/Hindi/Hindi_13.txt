The Rise of Natural Farming in Indian Agriculture: A Structural Transition

This editorial is based on the article titled "Designing the transition to natural farming" published in The Hindu Business Line on 20/01/2026. This editorial examines India's transition to natural farming against the backdrop of rising input costs, soil erosion, and climate pressures. It also analyzes the policy initiatives, structural challenges, and corrective pathways needed to make natural farming economically viable and widespread.

Against the backdrop of rising agricultural input costs, climate instability, and agricultural distress, a silent but significant transformation is unfolding in India's fields. From Andhra Pradesh to Uttar Pradesh, farmers experimenting with natural farming are challenging the chemical-based development model that propelled the Green Revolution. This transformation is not the result of some backward-looking amnesia, but rather reflects a practical search for ecological adaptation, soil sovereignty, and income stability. The debate is no longer limited to whether a shift in agriculture is necessary, but rather focuses on "how urgently and on what scale this transition should be undertaken."

What is Natural Farming?

§ Introduction: Natural Farming (NF) is a chemical-free, minimal intervention farming system that works in harmony with nature's natural processes, developing a self-regulating and ecological agricultural system through the use of biodiversity, soil health, and farm-based resources (such as bio-inputs made from cow dung and urine) instead of artificial inputs, tillage, and chemical weeding (weed control).

o Natural farming supports the interdependence of natural ecosystems between soil, water, microbiome, vegetation, flora, fauna, climate, and human needs.

o Philosophical Basis: The most famous proponent of natural farming is Japanese farmer Masanobu Fukuoka, who propounded its 4 core principles in his work 'The One-Straw Revolution'—

• No Tillage: In this process, the soil is neither tilled nor turned, which preserves the soil structure and maintains the activity of microorganisms.

• No Chemical Fertilizers: Nutrients are provided by nature (by microorganisms, earthworms, and organic matter), not by artificial chemicals.

• No Weeding by Tillage/Herbicides: Weeds are controlled naturally by mulching instead of eradicating them from the root, as they play a role in the balance of the soil.

• No Chemical Pesticides: Pests are controlled by ecological balance and natural predators, not by pesticides or poisons.

§ Natural Agriculture in India - 'Zero Budget Natural Farming' (ZBNF): In India, natural agriculture is prevalent in the form of Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF), promoted by Subhash Palekar. It now often receives support under government initiatives such as the National Mission on Natural Farming (NMNF).

o The term 'zero budget' means almost zero cost of production, as farmers do not need to buy expensive inputs like fertilizers or pesticides.

§ Components of Natural Farming

o Seed Treatment (Bijamrita): Using locally adapted indigenous seeds and enhancing the vitality of seeds by treating them in the field with natural formulations like Bijamrita and providing protection from soil-borne diseases.

o Soil Health Management (Ghan Jeevamrita): Use of agricultural products like Ghan Jeevamrita to improve microbial activity, nutrient availability, and long-term soil fertility.

o Year-Round Soil Cover (Mulching): Continuously covering the soil with living crops or crop residues, which helps conserve moisture, balance temperature, control weeds, and prevent soil erosion.

o Soil Micro-Climate Management (Wafasa – Soil Aeration): Minimizing soil disturbances to enhance natural porosity, ensuring an optimal air-water balance for better root growth, moisture retention, and increased crop tolerance.

o Plant Health Management: Using organic inputs and needs-based natural formulations to strengthen plants' disease resistance, reduce pest outbreaks, and reduce dependence on external chemical inputs.

o Resource Efficiency and Income Enhancement: Holistic adoption of natural farming principles to reduce input costs, improve soil health, and increase net agricultural income from sustainable agricultural practices.

What factors are driving India to move towards natural farming?

§ Economic Stability and Farmers' Income Security: Indian agriculture is currently trapped in a vicious cycle of high external input-based costs and low returns, with excessive reliance on chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and proprietary seeds.

o For example, on average, farmers spend ₹5,961 per acre on chemical inputs for rice cultivation, while farmers practicing fully Zero Budget Natural Farming spend ₹846 per acre on natural inputs.

o Most farmers in India (more than 85%) are small and marginal farmers, who are most affected by rising costs and climate change. Natural farming reduces barriers to entry into farming for farmers by reducing dependence on market-purchased inputs and credit.

§ Soil Health and Long-Term Productivity: A study by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR), based on the analysis of 2.54 lakh soil samples taken from 620 districts in 29 states, indicates that unscientific fertilizer use and climate change are reducing the level of organic carbon in India's arable soil.

o Natural farming restores the biology of the soil through organic carbon enrichment and microbial activation, which improves nutrient cycling and soil structure.

§ Water Conservation and Climate Adaptation: Traditional agriculture consumes excessive water and is sensitive to irregular monsoons and rising temperatures.

o At the same time, about 600 million people in India are already facing high to extreme water stress.

o Natural agricultural practices such as mulching and better aeration in the soil reduce evaporation losses and increase moisture retention capacity.

o This reduces the demand for irrigation, as well as improves the tolerance of crops to drought and global warming stress.

• As climate risks increase, such agro-ecological resilience is becoming a necessity rather than an option.

§ Environmental Conservation and Biodiversity Restoration: Chemical runoff contaminates groundwater, damages ecosystems, and disrupts beneficial insect populations.

o Natural farming minimizes the use of artificial chemicals, which makes possible the revival of natural predators, pollinators, and soil organisms.

o This restores ecological balance and reduces long-term pest pressure. From an environmental perspective, this shift helps internalize the ecological costs that were previously borne externally under chemical agriculture.

§ Human Health and Food Safety: Farmers are directly exposed to hazardous agricultural chemicals, while consumers face risks from pesticide residues in food and water.

o Natural farming reduces chemical risk in the food chain, reducing occupational health risks and public health burden.

o Clean food systems are also in line with growing consumer demand for safe and nutritious products (especially in the European Union market).

§ Institutional and Fiscal Prudence: Fertilizer and pesticide subsidies in India put a heavy burden on the exchequer and encourage inefficient input use. Fertilizer subsidy is the second largest subsidy after food subsidy.

o Promoting natural farming can gradually rationalize subsidy expenditure by focusing on knowledge, training and ecosystem services rather than chemicals.

o This improves the efficiency of public expenditure without affecting food security. Over time, this aligns agricultural policy with ecological and financial stability.

§ Alignment with Sustainable Development and Future Food Systems: In a climate change-affected future, India needs to balance food security and environmental limits.

o Natural farming contributes to sustainable agriculture, tackling climate change and land restoration goals without requiring high capital investment.

o It represents a transition towards regenerative food systems rather than extractive production models. Strategically, it plays a central role in building a future-ready agricultural economy.

What measures have been taken by India to promote natural farming?

§ Institutional Mission Mode: National Mission on Natural Farming (NMNF): This 'Mission Mode' effectively institutionalizes the transition from chemical-intensive agriculture to chemical-free agriculture by creating a dedicated management structure that standardizes protocols and ensures direct financial flow to districts.

o Approved in the second half of 2024, this mission has a provision of ₹2,481 crore. It aims to cover 1 crore farmers in 7.5 lakh hectares area and provide an incentive of ₹4,000 per acre/year to reduce transitional costs.

§ Fiscal Federalism and Subsidy Reform - PM-PRANAM Scheme: This scheme encourages states to reduce the use of chemical fertilizers, under which they are allowed to retain 50% of the subsidy savings as central grants, while 70% is earmarked for the construction of organic and bio-fertilizer infrastructure.

o By converting subsidy savings into fiscal awards, it incorporates ecological outcomes within a competitive federal structure, giving states an incentive to promote natural farming and balanced nutrient use.

§ Supply Chain Infrastructure – Bio-Input Resource Centers (BRC): To overcome the labor and time constraints in preparing bio-inputs like Jeevamrita, the government is promoting BRCs as village-level micro-enterprises that supply standardized, ready-to-use natural inputs.

o This reduces the hard work of farmers and creates entrepreneurship opportunities for self-help groups and rural youth. The roadmap for session 2024-25 aims to establish 10,000 Bio-Resource Centers (BRC) to serve 15,000 clusters, ensuring reliable availability of bio-equipment to more than 1 crore farmers.

§ Environmental-Geographic Corridor– Arth Ganga Initiative: Under the Arth Ganga Project, a chemical-free natural agriculture corridor is being developed along the banks of the Ganges River to reduce the runoff of agricultural chemicals and link river rejuvenation with sustainable agriculture.

o By promoting Ganga-branded organic belt, this initiative leverages cultural values to secure high-quality markets for farmers. This initiative prioritizes natural farming in a 10 kilometer strip along the river bank, integrating water conservation with livelihoods.

§ Certification and Market Confidence: Digital Portal and Branding: To address the challenges related to credibility and traceability, the government has simplified certification processes through the Jaivik Kheti Portal, Participatory Guarantee System (PGS) and Traditional Agriculture Development Scheme (PKVY), which allows small farmers to certify natural products without expensive third-party audits.

o This digital, cluster-based model aggregates farmers into an integrated market, helping them reach better buyers by circumventing intermediaries. Currently, more than 6 lakh farmers are registered on the portal, while the proposed 'Single National Brand' under NMNF aims to standardize quality in 15,000 clusters.

§ Educational Integration and Expansion of Gender-Based Services: ICAR has integrated natural farming into undergraduate and postgraduate courses, transforming it from traditional practices into scientifically validated teaching methodology, for which extension capacity has been strengthened through research and field trials in KVK.

o Complementary to this, the Krishi Sakhi Convergence Program (KSCP) deploys trained women self-help group members as semi-extension workers, who leverage peer networks for practical change.

o Linked to the Lakhpati Didi initiative, more than 70,000 Krishi Sakhis (as of August 2025) now ensure last-mile guidance, community ownership, and livelihood creation.

What are the main issues associated with India's transition to natural agriculture?

§ ‘Yield Penalty’ or Transitional Decline: In the initial years of transition to natural farming, there is a sharp decline in crop yields due to the rebuilding of the soil's biological system, although its level depends on the region-specific.

o This 'transitional decline' creates a serious liquidity crisis for small farmers dependent on limited resources. Unable to bear even a single season of low yields, many farmers abandon the practice before the soil fertility stabilizes.

§ Labor Intensity and Input-Generated Labor Burden: Natural farming practices, including preparing organic inputs like Jeevamrita and manually controlling weeds, are more labor-intensive than traditional input-based systems.

o Such practices often require greater reliance on physical labor, especially where mechanization is limited, which can be a challenge for farmers in areas facing labor shortages and rising wage rates.

o These create obstacles in widespread adoption, especially in areas where agricultural families depend on non-agricultural income or are struggling with lack of time.

§ Crisis related to availability of 'Indigenous Cattle': The basic framework of natural farming is based on dung and urine of indigenous cows, but the mechanization of agriculture has led to a sharp decline in the number of male animals. The availability of fresh bio-inputs becomes a practical challenge for non-dairy farmers.

o For example, according to the 20th Livestock Census, a decline of about 6% has been recorded in the total number of indigenous animals.

o This creates a paradoxical situation, where a farming practice considered self-reliant forces animal-less farmers to buy bio-inputs, thereby restarting a cost structure that they were trying to avoid.

§ Asymmetric Fiscal Incentives: At the policy level, a clear contradiction is visible, where the government promotes natural farming with meager funds, while simultaneously supporting chemical farming with huge subsidies, effectively paying farmers to maintain chemical dependence.

o In the year 2024–25, chemical fertilizer subsidy has reached around ₹1.64 lakh crore. This unbalanced financial structure makes cheap urea extremely accessible and makes natural farming economically less attractive despite its long-term ecological benefits.

§ Lack of Certification and Traceability: The current market lacks a robust, comprehensive verification system for natural products, due to which farmers are forced to sell high-quality chemical-free crops at normal market prices, as they cannot prove their 'natural' status to remote urban consumers.

o Reliance on Participatory Guarantee Systems (PGS) is socially inclusive, but commercially weak, as it lacks the brand trust of third-party certification required by major retail chains and exporters.

§ Lack of Scientific Validation: There remains an underlying conflict between traditional knowledge and formal agricultural science, as the lack of rigorous, long-term peer-reviewed studies in various agro-climatic zones creates skepticism among extension officers and scientists.

o In the absence of 'Package of Practices' validated by universities for each crop-area combination, state agriculture departments hesitate to aggressively promote natural farming as a mainstream strategy.

§ Market Absorption and Price Risk Uncertainty: Despite the reduction in input costs from natural farming, farmers remain subject to markets where price determination is based on traditional production standards.

o In the absence of assured procurement or differentiated price premiums, even equal or slightly lower yields translate into income risk.

o This weak market signal discourages farmers from transitioning, especially in grains and perishable crops where MSP or contract farming controls price determination.

§ Food Insecurity and Nutritional Vulnerability: Although natural farming promises long-term sustainability, cost reduction and restoration of soil health, its transition phase poses short-term risks that are severely linked to India's existing food and nutritional challenges.

o According to The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World-2025, about 12% of India's population (about 172 million people) are undernourished and about 42.9% cannot afford a healthy diet due to rising food costs, which shows deep structural challenges in food access, affordability and quality.

o This persistent food insecurity increases vulnerability for small farmers turning to natural farming, as even slight declines in yields or market uncertainties can further impair families' ability to obtain adequate nutrition and income.

What measures are needed to facilitate the transition to natural agriculture in India?

§ Institutionalizing 'Transition Insurance' for yield gaps: To deal with the immediate decline in productivity during the conversion phase, the state should formulate a 'Yield Loss Insurance' mechanism, which compensates farmers not only for crop failure, but also for specific volumetric differences between traditional and natural production during the initial three years.

o This financial risk-minimization instrument should be linked to regional benchmark yields rather than market prices, thereby preventing instability in farmers' incomes during the period of soil's biological system rejuvenation. Thus, the transition period can be seen as a state-supported initial phase for long-term ecological asset building.

§ Decentralizing Bio-Equipment Supply Chains ​​through Micro Enterprises: Instead of relying solely on farmer-level production, the ecosystem needs a network of professional 'Bio-Input Resource Centers' (BRC), which operate as village-level micro-enterprises run by women's groups or rural youth.

o This commercialization ensures standardization of quality (pH and microbial density) for inputs such as Jeevamrita and Beejamrita, which reduces the labor burden on individual farmers and creates a reliable, just-in-time supply chain that mimics the convenience of chemical fertilizer shops but with localized, circular economy principles.

§ Creating 'Bio-Village Clusters' to Leverage Wider Benefits in the Market: To solve the problem of fragmentation, the transition strategy should focus on notifying contiguous 'Natural Agriculture Zones' or Bio-Villages rather than isolated individual adoption.

o Such spatial clusters will develop economies of scale for sowing coordination, prevention of chemical pollution from neighboring fields, and bulk supply. This will give Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) the ability to negotiate with large retailers as a certified chemical-free regional brand.

§ Digitizing Trust with 'Blockchain-Enabled Traceability': To circumvent expensive and slow third-party certification systems, the sector needs a 'Participatory Guarantee System (PGS) 2.0' based on distributed ledger technology.

o By allowing consumers to scan QR codes and view the immutable history of agricultural inputs peer-to-peer verified by local farmer groups, this system replaces institutional administration with 'digital trust', which significantly reduces compliance costs for small farmers, while providing premium market assurance to urban consumers wary of 'greenwashing'.

§ Making 'Green Procurement' Mandatory in the Public Distribution System (PDS): The state can act as a 'first market maker' by mandating that a certain percentage of produce for mid-day meals and Anganwadis be procured exclusively from local natural agriculture groups.

o This creates a guaranteed, non-volatile demand flow system that protects farmers from open market fluctuations during their sensitive transitional years, while simultaneously improving nutritional security for public beneficiaries, thereby effectively bridging the gap between public health goals and agricultural sustainability.

§ Restructuring Agricultural Extension Services in the 'Farmer-Scientist Cycle': The current top-down technology transfer model should be replaced by 'co-creation laboratories', where agricultural universities validate traditional knowledge rather than merely imposing laboratory techniques.

o By formally recognizing 'Champion Farmers' as adjunct faculty and codifying their experiential knowledge into the official curriculum, this system can bridge the 'lab-to-land' credibility gap, ensuring that scientific research is responsive to the hyper-local realities of agro-climatic zones and pest dynamics.

Conclusion:

Natural farming represents a strategic shift away from input-intensive agriculture towards a regenerative, climate-resilient, and financially prudent food system. However, its success depends on mitigating the risks associated with the transitional phase, balancing asymmetric subsidy structures, and building credible markets and scientific validation. Through mission-mode implementation, outcome-based incentives, and institutional trust-building mechanisms, natural farming can transform soil health into a productive economic asset. If implemented effectively, it can strike a balance between farmer income security, ecological sustainability, and long-term food sovereignty—all three.
