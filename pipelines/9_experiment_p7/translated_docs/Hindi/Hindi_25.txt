Child Marriage in India: A Persistent Social Scourge Undermining Human Development

Context (Introduction) Despite consistent decline over the past two decades, child marriage remains a deeply entrenched social practice in India, undermining progress in health, education, poverty alleviation, and gender equality and threatening India’s efforts to eliminate it by 2030.

Current Situation: Progress with Deep Regional and Social Disparities

*   Declining national trend, uneven reality: National surveys indicate that child marriage among women has fallen from nearly half in the mid-2000s to about a quarter by 2019–21, reflecting policy attention and social change.

*   Large absolute numbers persist: Given India's population size, even reduced percentages translate into millions of girls married before 18, keeping India among the countries with the highest absolute burden.

*   Variations across states: High prevalence continues in eastern and central states like West Bengal, Bihar, Tripura, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan, indicating uneven social development.

*   Rural and marginalized communities most affected: Child marriage is concentrated in rural areas, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and minority communities, reinforcing cycles of deprivation.

*   Slow progress among young adults: High prevalence among women aged 18–29 indicates recent cohorts are affected despite legal restrictions.

*   Threat to SDG commitments: Experts believe failure to curb child marriage undermines progress across multiple development goals related to health, education, poverty, and gender equality.

Key Drivers of Child Marriage

*   Poverty and economic insecurity: Girls from the poorest families are many times more likely to marry early, as marriage is seen as a strategy to reduce economic burdens.

*   Low educational attainment: Girls with little or no schooling face significantly higher risk of early marriage, as education delays marriage and improves bargaining power.

*   Gender norms and patriarchy: Deeply ingrained beliefs about female purity, honor, and caregiving roles continue to prioritize early marriage over girls' autonomy.

*   Inadequate school infrastructure: Lack of secondary schools, safe transportation, toilets, and hostels pushes adolescent girls out of education, increasing their vulnerability.

*   Weak law enforcement: Low reporting, poor registration of marriages, and limited convictions under child marriage laws weaken deterrent effect.

*   Unintended legal consequences: Use of strict child protection laws has sometimes pushed minor girls toward unsafe, informal solutions, increasing health risks.

Socio-Economic Impacts

*   Reduced maternal and child health outcomes: Early pregnancies are linked to higher maternal mortality rates, anemia, malnutrition, and low birth weight.

*   Entrapment in intergenerational poverty: Girls married young are more likely to drop out of school, have lower skills, and live in low-income households.

*   Educational barriers: Marriage almost always marks the end of girls' formal education, limiting lifetime earnings.

*   Reduced female workforce participation: Early marriage and childbirth restrict women's entry into paid work, undermining demographic dividend gains.

*   Higher risk of domestic violence: Evidence suggests child brides face greater risk of spousal violence and limited decision-making power.

*   Strain on public services: Poor health and nutritional outcomes increase long-term pressures on healthcare and welfare systems.

Government Efforts and Policy Responses

*   Legal framework: The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 provides for annulment of marriages, penalties, and protection, forming the backbone of legal action.

*   Awareness campaigns: Initiatives like the Child Marriage Free India Campaign aim to mobilize communities through sustained social messaging.

*   Girl child education and incentives: Conditional cash transfers and scholarship schemes attempt to retain girls in school and delay marriage.

*   Gender-focused campaigns: Programs like Beti Bachao Beti Padhao combine advocacy with investments in education and welfare.

*   Convergence with health and nutrition schemes: Adolescent health programs address anemia, nutrition, and reproductive health risks.

*   Participation in local governance: Panchayats, frontline workers, and women's self-help groups are being increasingly involved in prevention efforts.

Necessary Improvements

*   Targeting the poorest and most vulnerable: Social protection and livelihood support for vulnerable families can reduce economic incentives for early marriage.

*   Strengthening secondary education access: Expanding schools, hostels, toilets, and safe transportation for adolescent girls is crucial.

*   Sensitively improving law enforcement: Focusing on prevention, counseling, and community solutions rather than only punitive action.

*   Addressing gender norms at the community level: Long-term engagement with parents, religious leaders, and boys is necessary to shift attitudes.

*   Enhancing data and monitoring: District-level tracking and early warning systems can help identify high-risk areas.

*   Integrating health, education, and legal responses: A coordinated approach across different sectors is necessary to break the cycle of early marriage.

Conclusion

Child marriage in India is not just a legal issue but a multi-dimensional development challenge. Unless poverty, educational gaps, health risks, and gender inequality are addressed together, India’s goal of eliminating child marriage by 2030 will remain an aspiration rather than an achievement.

How Lower-Income States Are Catching Up: India’s Quiet Growth Shift

Context (Introduction) India’s recent growth story reveals a significant shift: many historically poorer states are now growing faster than richer states, primarily driven by sustained public investment in infrastructure and supportive center-state fiscal coordination.

What has changed in India’s growth pattern?

*   First divergence, now convergence: Before the pandemic, richer states consistently outpaced poorer states; after FY19, lower-income states started growing faster, reversing prior trends.

*   Key converging states: Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Bihar have shown marked improvement in relative growth performance.

*   Why it matters nationally: As India’s GDP is the sum of states’ GSDP, faster growth in lagging, more populous states significantly boosts overall national growth.

*   Unexpected post-pandemic outcome: Contrary to fears, poorer states did not suffer lasting damage from COVID and instead improved growth momentum.

*   Early but broad-based trend: Convergence is recent but visible across many states, indicating structural change rather than one-off rebound.

*   Sign of inclusive growth: Reducing regional growth gaps strengthens the foundation for long-term, inclusive development.

The Main Engine: State Capital Expenditure

*   Public investment drives growth: Higher state spending on roads, urban infrastructure, and logistics emerged as the strongest factor explaining faster growth.

*   Infrastructure equalization: Emerging states rapidly increased infrastructure investment, improving connectivity and lowering costs for businesses.

*   Crowding in private investment: Public capex boosted investor confidence, encouraging private firms to co-invest alongside the state.

*   Stronger growth multiplier: Capital expenditure generates more output and employment than routine revenue expenditure.

*   Signaling governance: Sustained capex signals policy stability and intent to reform, shaping long-term growth expectations.

*   Complementing central projects: State investment filled critical gaps around national highways, railways, and logistics corridors.

How Center-State Finances Made it Possible

*   Post-pandemic revenue support: Higher transfers after COVID improved state finances and enabled investment.

*   Capex loan program for states: Low-cost, ring-fenced loans ensured funds were used only for capital projects.

*   Rapid expansion: This program significantly expanded in six years, providing substantial funding to states.

*   Preserving capex: States opted to increase deficits rather than cut infrastructure spending.

*   Fiscal coordination success: Cooperation between center and states minimized pro-cyclical investment cuts.

*   Stability despite GST changes: Capex loans provided protection to states even after GST compensation ended.

Risks to Continued Convergence

*   Slowing central tax growth: Recent slowdown in nominal GDP growth and repeated direct and indirect tax cuts have slowed central gross tax revenue. As roughly 41% of the divisible tax pool is shared with states, any slowdown directly constricts state revenue, as seen in a decline in aggregate state receipts in FY25 after years of steady growth.

*   Rising state fiscal deficits: To protect capital expenditure, many states allowed deficits to rise after the pandemic. Consequently, many states now operate near or above 3% of GSDP, limiting their capacity to absorb future revenue shocks without cutting spending.

*   Expansion of welfare and cash transfers: In advance of recent state elections, governments in states like Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, and Telangana expanded cash transfer and subsidy schemes. Though socially valuable, these schemes increase committed revenue expenditure and reduce fiscal space for infrastructure.

*   Risk of capital expenditure contraction: State budgets indicate that when revenue pressures intensify, capex is often the first adjustment variable. With revenue already softening in FY25, prolonged stress could force states to curtail infrastructure projects.

*   Uneven fiscal capacity across states: Richer states have strong own-tax bases and borrowing capacity, while poorer states depend more on central transfers. This makes emerging states like Bihar and Assam more vulnerable to revenue instability.

*   Fragility of policy continuity: Infrastructure investment requires multi-year commitment, but past experience shows that changes in political priorities or fiscal stress can abruptly disrupt capex cycles, reducing long-term growth benefits.

What Needs to Be Done to Sustain Convergence?

*   Expand the public lending program: A larger and more predictable multi-year financing could stabilize state investment planning.

*   Protect infrastructure spending: Capex should be prioritized over short-term revenue spending.

*   Fully utilize deregulation: States must implement labor and trade reforms to translate infrastructure into employment.

*   Attract labor-intensive manufacturing: Emerging states can capitalize on wage advantages in textiles, footwear, and furniture.

*   Link with private investment: Public projects should be designed to attract private capital.

*   Leverage global supply chain shifts: States can integrate into mid-tech manufacturing as companies diversify production locations.

Conclusion

India’s future growth depends on state-led convergence. While state capital expenditure is now above 4% of GDP and the center targets a developed India by 2047, emerging states are poised to accelerate growth. If infrastructure investment, fiscal discipline, and reforms continue, convergence could deepen. However, revenue stress and policy slippage could derail progress, making long-term center-state coordination essential.
