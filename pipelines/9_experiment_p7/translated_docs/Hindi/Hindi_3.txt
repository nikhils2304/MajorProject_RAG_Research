Re-imagining and Development-Oriented Transformation of India's Agricultural Sector

This article is based on the article titled "Budget implications for the agriculture sector" published in The Hindustan Times on 02/02/2026. This article analyzes India's transition from a subsidy-based agricultural system to infrastructure, technology and value-chain-oriented development, and highlights both the opportunities and structural challenges inherent in building an income-stable and resilient agricultural sector. India's agricultural system is gradually transitioning from a subsidy-based and production-centric model to a framework driven by infrastructure, logistics, and digital governance. Targeted incentives provided in Budget 2026-27 for ports, warehousing, AI-based advisory services, and export facilitation reflect a qualitative shift in agricultural value-creation and value-capture processes. This policy shift is oriented towards market strengthening from agricultural assistance, which has a wide-ranging impact on income stability, diversification and global competitiveness. However, relatively limited investment in irrigation and agricultural research and development raises concerns about the long-term resilience of agriculture in the current scenario of climate instability and resource pressures.

What are the main factors affecting India's agricultural sector?

§ Transition to High-Value Plantation Crops: Moving beyond crops essential for food security, Budget 2026-27 promotes high-value 'cash crops' to boost farmers' income and reduce import dependence. o This policy is specifically focused on coastal and hilly economies, aiming to promote the cultivation of nuts and horticultural crops, to increase the share of high-quality products in global markets and to develop import-substitution for raw materials such as cocoa and cashew. • For example, the budget provides for a dedicated scheme for coconut promotion. o To revive old and low-yielding orchards and expand high-density cultivation of walnuts, almonds and pine fruits, the budget proposes to support a dedicated programme to ensure value addition through increased farmers' income and youth participation. o In addition, special attention is given to key fibers such as silk, wool and jute under the National Fiber Plan, ensuring direct benefits to farmers operating in these areas.

§ AI-Integrated Digital Extension: The launch of Bharat-Vistar marks an important shift from static data collection to active, AI-powered advisory services. By integrating 'AgriStack' (Farmer Registry) with ICAR's scientific data, this multilingual platform aims to democratize effective agriculture and provide real-time, location-specific advice to bridge the vast information gap from lab to field. o Budget 2026 has allocated ₹150 crore specifically for Bharat-Vistar to implement this AI framework.

§ Structural Transition to Allied Sectors: The Economic Survey 2025-26 highlights a decisive structural shift where animal husbandry and fisheries are becoming the primary engines of agricultural development, leaving behind the traditional crop sector. o This 'organic diversification' acts as an important insurance against monsoon instability, keeping rural incomes stable even when traditional crop production declines. • For example, animal husbandry recorded a CAGR of 12.77% at current prices between FY15 and 2024, with an increase of approximately 195% in gross value. • In addition, fish production increased from 95.79 lakh tonnes in FY14 to 197.75 lakh tonnes in FY25, promoting rural income and employment diversification.

§ Integration of Blue Economy and Expansion of Value Chain: The government is industrializing the fisheries sector by integrating inland water bodies into formal value chains to promote 'Blue Growth'. o The focus is no longer just on the quantity of fish caught, but on processing and export competitiveness, and fishing vessels are being effectively considered as 'mobile export units' to maximize foreign exchange earnings. • For example, Budget 2026 proposes to integrate 500 reservoirs and Amrit Sarovars into the fisheries value chain. It has also declared fish caught in the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) by Indian vessels duty-free, with the aim of promoting marine exports.

§ Increase in Exports Amidst Protectionism: Despite global trade barriers and tariff wars, Indian agricultural exports have shown remarkable growth, shifting towards non-traditional markets and processed goods. o This strategy involves shifting away from volatile bulk commodities (such as wheat) and focusing on higher-value, tariff-resistant products such as coffee and processed fruits, ensuring the continuity of the trade surplus. • For example, during FY25, India's coffee exports reached $1.80 billion, representing a 40.2% increase compared to $1.28 billion achieved in FY24. o In addition, India is the largest producer of millet in the world, accounting for 38.4% of global production (FAO, 2023).

§ Institutionalizing Natural Agriculture: Efforts to promote natural agriculture have now moved beyond a pilot project and transformed into a mass participation movement, aiming to sustainably reduce the burden of fertilizer subsidies and restore soil health. o This approach is now cluster-based and market-based, aiming to create a unique value chain for chemical-free products, rather than just a resource reduction-based strategy. o The National Natural Agriculture Mission has been allocated ₹750 crore in Budget 2026-27 to support bio-equipment resource centers. The aim is to involve 1 crore farmers to meet the growing demand of the organic export market.

§ Women's Role in Effective Agricultural Revolution: The Namo-Didi Yojana is successfully creating a new class of rural technical workforce among women, which is meeting both the labor shortage and the need for effective application of nano-fertilizers. o This reflects a social shift where women are not only agricultural laborers but also property owners and technology providers, shifting the balance of power in rural agricultural economies. o Under this scheme, with an expenditure of ₹1,261 crore, 15,000 drones are being made available to women self-help groups. These drones cover 1 acre in 7–8 minutes, enabling self-help groups to earn income from rent and reduce fertilizer consumption through effective spraying. • In addition, the central budget has proposed 'SHE-Mart' to promote self-help entrepreneurs, under which community-owned retail outlets will be set up in each district for marketing products manufactured by women and self-help groups, which will help women in agriculture, animal husbandry and allied sectors to move from subsistence work to entrepreneurship.

§ Collectivization through FPOs: Fragmentation of small farmers is being rapidly reduced through the '10,000 FPO' scheme, which is transforming farmers from 'price takers' to 'price setters' through collective bargaining. o This institutional innovation is now moving towards maturity, moving beyond mere registration to genuine commercial integration, empowering direct market contacts and the potential for value addition at the agricultural level. o As of December 2025, ₹430.77 crore has been distributed as matching equity grants to 6,557 FPOs and credit guarantee cover worth ₹662.71 crore has been provided to 2,671 Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs).

What are the major issues related to India's agricultural sector?

§ Disparity in Climate Insurance: A serious policy paradox is that the frequency of extreme weather events far exceeds the fiscal capacity of state compensation. o While the 'climate crisis' has become a permanent feature disrupting crop cycles, budgetary allocations for insurance (PMFBY) are actually declining, increasing reliance on 'adaptation' rather than 'compensation', which makes small farmers vulnerable to immediate liquidity crises. o For example, in 2025, India faced extremely adverse weather events for 331 out of 334 days, affecting 1,74,00,000 hectares of crop area. • Yet Budget 2026-27 has allocated ₹12,200 crore for PMFBY, which is less than the revised estimates.

§ Disguised Unemployment and Labor Stabilization: Despite structural transformation of the broader economy, the agricultural sector still absorbs a disproportionate share of the workforce, indicating 'disguised unemployment'. o The failure of the manufacturing sector to attract workers from farms has created a situation where increased agricultural production does not translate into increased per capita agricultural income, as a large share of the revenue is distributed among many dependents. o According to the Periodic Labor Force Survey (PLFS) 2025, 57.7% of the rural workforce is still engaged in agriculture, which is higher than in previous years and effectively suppresses rural wage growth.

§ Imbalance in NPK Ratio and Soil Inefficiency: Excessive subsidies on urea have created distortions in nutrient ratios, leading to severe toxicity and stabilization of yield growth ('soil inefficiency') in the soil. o Farmers over-use cheap nitrogen and neglect phosphorus and potassium, creating a 'yield barrier' where applying more fertilizer produces a disproportionate crop output, endangering long-term food security for short-term gains. • For example, the Economic Survey 2025-26 identified the NPK consumption ratio as an uneven ratio of 10.9:4.1:1 (while the ideal ratio is 4:2:1).

§ Imbalance in MSP-Procurement: The demand for a legal guarantee for Minimum Support Price (MSP) arises from market failure, where open market prices often fall below the cost of production at the time of harvest. o The current procurement structure is mainly focused on wheat and rice and is limited to Punjab and Haryana, leaving farmers of pulses, oilseeds and coarse grains in other states outside the safety net despite the 'paper MSP'. • For example, while the government announced an increase in Minimum Support Price (MSP) for 22 crops in FY26, actual procurement for crops such as oilseeds remained negligible.

§ Fragmentation and Inefficiency of Land: Continuous sub-division of land over generations has reduced the average operational holding size to such an extent that mechanization has become economically unviable. o This hinders economies of scale of land fragmentation, making modern resources (such as irrigation and harvesting machines) expensive for small farmers, and they get caught in a low-resource-low-production balance. • According to estimates for 2025, the average land size has decreased to 1.08 hectares, with 86% being small and marginal farmers. Limited use of agricultural machinery on these small plots is increasing the cost of production.

§ Deficiencies in Post-Harvest First-Mile: Despite improvements in cold chain capacity, critical 'first-mile' connectivity (from farm gate to aggregation point) is still unsatisfactory. o Most post-harvest losses occur immediately after harvesting, as there is a lack of packaging houses and pre-cooling units near the fields. As a result, farmers are forced to make 'distress sales' to local traders rather than getting a better price in the organized market. • For example, India incurs an annual economic loss of approximately ₹92,651 crore due to post-harvest losses (CIPHET).

§ Dependence on Imports of Edible Oils and Pulses: India's continued dependence on global markets for essential proteins and fats is a major strategic weakness. o Despite 'mission mode' interventions, domestic production of oilseeds and pulses fluctuates wildly with the monsoon, forcing the government to rely on zero-duty imports to control inflation, which paradoxically reduces domestic prices and discourages farmers from increasing production. • For example, India imported 16 million tonnes of edible oil worth approximately ₹1.61 lakh crore during the 2024-25 marketing year ending in October to meet domestic demand, exposing sensitivity to global price volatility and foreign exchange outflows. o Production of tur (arhar) in Maharashtra for the 2025-26 season is estimated to decline by as much as 40% due to severe crop damage due to excessive rainfall, floods and waterlogging, which could lead to increased imports to meet supply shortages.

§ Groundwater Depletion and Energy-Link: Free electricity policy for tubewells in major agricultural states has created a distorted incentive for groundwater exploitation, creating an ecological crisis. o This 'energy-water nexus' promotes the cultivation of water-intensive crops such as paddy in semi-arid areas, which is depleting water reserves at an irreversible rate and effectively exporting water from arid states to the rest of the world. • For example, according to the Dynamic Groundwater Resource Assessment 2025, 10.8% of the units (730 out of 6,762) are over-exploited, while the total stressed units (over-exploited, critical and semi-critical) together account for approximately 25%. o In addition, approximately one-fourth of India's total electricity consumption is used in the agricultural sector, while 70% of the country's electricity is carbon-based (Center for Policy Research).

What measures are needed to strengthen India's agricultural sector?

§ Universalizing Digital Public Infrastructure: The primary measure for modernization is to expand AgriStack nationwide to create a seamless digital ecosystem for every farmer. o By integrating land records, crop surveys and unique farmer identification, the government can enable 'plug-and-play' access to formal credit, crop insurance and tailored advisory services. o This digital framework eliminates the role of intermediaries, reduces loan processing time from weeks to minutes, and allows for precise targeted benefit transfers, preventing leakages and ensuring that assistance reaches the real farmer who tills the land directly.

§ Transitioning to Effective Agriculture through Bharat-Vistar: A significant shift from general agricultural extension is required, for which AI-powered precision advice is required through platforms such as the newly proposed Bharat-Vistar. o By leveraging real-time satellite imagery and multilingual AI, farmers can receive farm-specific recommendations regarding soil-health, irrigation schedules and local pest alerts. o This 'Intelligence-as-a-Service' model helps small farmers to optimize input utilization, reducing over-use of urea and water, thereby reducing production costs, as well as increasing yield quality and environmental sustainability.

§ Promoting Market-Based Crop Diversification: To break the ecologically harmful wheat-rice monoculture cycle, India should implement productivity-linked incentives (PLI) for high-value and climate-resilient crops such as oilseeds, pulses and plantation crops (coconut, cashew, cocoa). o Instead of just announcing MSP, the state should develop region-specific crop boards that provide comprehensive support from high-quality germplasm to guaranteed purchase for processing industries. o This shifts the focus from 'quantity-centric' farming to 'value-centric' entrepreneurship, aligning domestic production with global demand and reducing heavy imports of edible oils.

§ Decentralized Post-Harvest Infrastructure and 'First-Mile' Connectivity: To strengthen the agricultural sector, it is essential to bring value-addition processes closer to the farm, for which village-level processing centers should be established financed by the Agricultural Infrastructure Fund (AIF). o Measures should prioritize 'first-mile' logistics, such as solar-powered micro-cold rooms, pack-houses and grading units managed by Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) or self-help groups. o By reducing post-harvest wastage in horticulture, farmers can avoid forced sales during peak harvest times and gain the necessary 'holding power' to negotiate better prices in national and global markets.

§ Mainstreaming Climate-Adapted and Natural Agriculture Practices: As climate instability becomes a permanent risk, India should institutionalize Sustainable Agriculture Practices (SAP) such as Zero Budget Natural Agriculture (ZBNF) and high-density horticulture. o This involves establishing a decentralized network of bio-input resource centers at the panchayat level to provide organic fertilizers and pesticides to reduce the financial and ecological burden of chemical inputs. o Such measures not only replenish organic carbon and groundwater levels in the soil, but also help Indian products meet the increasingly stringent 'green' standards of international export markets.

§ Commercialization of Allied Sector: As the allied sector is now growing faster than crop farming, it needs to be institutionalized on an industrial scale through dedicated value chains and credit-linked subsidies. o These measures should include the formation of 'Livestock FPOs' and the linking of inland water bodies (such as Amrit Sarovars) to the formal 'Blue Economy' for intensive fisheries. o Ensuring universal accessibility to high-quality semen banks, mobile veterinary clinics and digital traceability for milk and meat products will enable these 'monsoon-resistant' sectors to become the primary drivers of rural income stability and export growth.

§ Improving Agricultural Credit through Risk-Guarantee Frameworks: To promote private investment, the financial structure should evolve from simple interest subsidies to a credit-risk guarantee fund. o By providing partial guarantees to banks, the state can incentivize lending in 'high-risk' areas such as agricultural startups, climate technology and small-scale mechanization (drones/robotics). o Reducing risk in this area attracts venture capital and encourages young 'agri-entrepreneurs' to modernize the value chain, transforming agriculture from a subsistence activity to a technically sophisticated and profitable commercial venture.

Conclusion:

The foundation of India's agricultural future lies not only in subsidy expansion, but in strengthening value chains, digitizing decision-making and diversifying crops beyond water-intensive staple crops. By integrating precise AI-based advice, post-harvest infrastructure and industrialization of allied sectors, farmers' income stability can be ensured against climate and price crises. Achieving the goals of self-reliance in oilseeds, export-competitive agricultural processing and groundwater-neutral farming will require a transition from price support to productivity enhancement and market empowerment. Thus, climate-adapted, market-linked and technology-based agriculture is of central importance to India's development, food security and rural adaptive capacity.
