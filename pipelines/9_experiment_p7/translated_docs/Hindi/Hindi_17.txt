The Revitalization of India's Space Program: This editorial is based on the article titled "A call to reenergise the Indian space ecosystem" published in The Hindustan Times on 15/01/2026. The article analyzes how recent launch failures have exposed structural, strategic, and institutional weaknesses in India's space ecosystem. It argues that strengthening space governance is essential for India's national security in the coming decade. India's space ecosystem, developed under the leadership of ISRO, has evolved from a developmental program to a strategic and commercial domain. India is among the six countries possessing end-to-end capabilities in the space sector. However, despite achieving remarkable missions and holding a 35% share in the global small satellite launch market in 2017, India's share has sharply declined in recent years due to launch, manufacturing, and policy hurdles. The failure of the PSLV-C62 mission in January 2026 has further highlighted these challenges. At a time when the space sector has become the backbone of navigation, communication, disaster management, and military operations, the strength of India's space ecosystem has emerged as a fundamental national development requirement. What developments are currently underway in India's space sector? § Human Spaceflight - Gaganyaan Mission: ISRO is preparing for the unmanned orbital test flight, G1 mission. o This crucial test flight will carry the humanoid robotic payload Vyommitra into space, simulating human actions and validating life-support systems. o The first manned flight, involving 4 Indian astronauts (Gaganyaanauts), is targeted for launch into Low Earth Orbit (LEO) by 2027. o These flights are specifically designed to test emergency abort scenarios during various stages of launch and to assess the reliability of indigenous life-support and environmental control systems. § Continuous Efforts Towards Next-Generation Launch Vehicles: Although ISRO successfully launched the 6,100 kg (heaviest to date) satellite, Blue Bird Block-2, using the LVM3-M6 rocket in December 2025. o A well-planned shift in India's launch strategy is now focused on ISRO's Next Generation Launch Vehicle (NGLV), designed to place up to 30 tonnes of payload into Low Earth Orbit, marking a decisive step towards heavy lift and reusable category capabilities. • The NGLV program aims to build confidence through repetition and regularity, focusing on stage-level performance validation rather than payload novelty. o Additionally, the new small satellite launch site being developed in Kulasekarapattinam will be specifically dedicated to supporting SSLV and private launches. • This dedicated space launch site will institutionalize private sector participation by providing the industry with demand-driven launch facilities, further strengthening India's commercial launch ecosystem. § Commercial Development and Economic Scale: India's space economy is valued at $8.4 billion USD (2% of the global share) and is projected to grow to approximately $44 billion USD (8% of the global share) by 2033, driven by increasing private participation in satellite manufacturing, launch services, and downstream data applications. o For example, NewSpace India Limited securing commercial launch contracts for international customers and private companies like Dhruva Space executing end-to-end satellite missions for global clients—all enabled by policy reforms—indicate scalable commercial maturity. § Strengthening National Security through Space-Based Surveillance: India is advancing a constellation of space-based surveillance satellites and plans to deploy 52 remote sensing and intelligence satellites by 2029-30 to enhance national security and real-time situational awareness. o Recent progress includes the Indian Space Research Organisation supporting defense-dedicated Earth observation satellites and the operation of space security architecture under the Defence Space Agency, enhancing the monitoring of borders, maritime regions, and critical assets. § Increasing Role of the Private Sector in India's Space Projects: India's private space ecosystem has seen solid progress in recent years, supported by regulatory support from IN-SPACe and commercial facilitation by NewSpace India Limited. o IN-SPACe has launched the Satellite Bus as a Service (SBaaS) initiative to provide private companies access to flight-ready satellite platforms, further reducing entry barriers and fostering innovation. o More than 300 space startups exist in India, spanning various sectors including launch systems, satellite manufacturing, propulsion, Earth observation, and downstream analytics, illustrating the rapid expansion of the private sector. • Key achievements such as Skyroot's Vikram-S, Agnikul's Agnibaan SOrTeD, and Pixel's hyperspectral constellation development indicate increasing technological maturity and commercial readiness. § Framework for Sovereign Orbital Presence (Indian Space Station): India is currently working towards realizing its permanent sovereign presence in space through the developmental phase of the Indian Space Station (BAS). This process involves advancing from design concepts to hardware procurement, aiming to secure a strategic orbital outpost by 2035. o This pre-operational phase focuses on critical technology demonstrations such as docking and module integration, laying the foundation for future microgravity research and sidestepping the ISS for the lunar gateway. o The roadmap was formally approved by the Union Cabinet in September 2024, targeting the launch of the first module BAS-1 by 2028. § Fiscal and Regulatory Liberalization: The government has implemented financial structural reforms, including removing regulatory barriers and investing risk capital, to address the "death valley" faced by deep-tech sector startups. o India now allows up to 100 percent foreign direct investment (FDI) in space sectors such as satellite manufacturing and operation, satellite data products, and ground and user segments. § High-Tech Diplomacy and Global Monitoring: Space has now become a central pillar of India's foreign policy, such as through collaborative projects like iCET (Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology) with the United States, where high-resolution monitoring data is being used as a diplomatic tool. o For example, the NASA-ISRO SAR (NISAR) satellite (launching in 2025) maps the Earth's topography with centimeter-level accuracy every 12 days using dual-frequency L-band and S-band radar. o Additionally, India's participation in the Artemis Accords facilitates access to advanced training, technological advancements, and scientific opportunities. What are the major issues related to India's space sector? § Launch Reliability Crisis Affecting Strategic Credibility: The failure of PSLV-C62 (2026) marked ISRO's fifth launch failure in the past 7 years, directly impacting India's credibility as a reliable launch provider. o This failure occurred less than a year after the PSLV-C61 mission was declared a failure in May 2025 due to a third-stage motor issue. o In the commercial space market, reliability matters more than cost, resulting in India's 35% share (2017) in global small satellite launches dropping to almost zero by 2024, while SpaceX alone achieved over 130 successful launches in 2024. § Structural Constraints: India's space launch capacity remains structurally limited, as it relies almost entirely on the Satish Dhawan Space Centre located in Sriharikota. This situation is starkly different from China, which has 4-5 launch sites, and the United States, where multiple civilian and military spaceports operate. o This limits India's ability to respond immediately or conduct additional launches, which are critical for rapid satellite replenishment in times of crisis or war. o It also means that weather-related disruptions in Sriharikota can affect the entire mission calendar, causing simultaneous delays in civilian, commercial, and strategic launches. § Navigation Sovereignty at Risk: India requires seven operational NavIC satellites for assured regional coverage, but as of 2026, only four are fully functional, two are nearing the end of their lifespan, and NVS-02 (2025) failed to reach its final orbit, weakening its systemic reliability. o This degradation reduces missile guidance, UAV navigation, logistics precision, and civilian positioning services, perpetuating continued reliance on foreign systems, particularly those of the United States, despite lessons learned about strategic denial risks during the Kargil War. § Delayed Applications at the ITU and Loss of Orbital Sovereignty: India's delayed applications to the International Telecommunication Union are in stark contrast to early and large-scale claims made by the United States and China, creating the risk of permanently losing key geostationary orbit (GEO) slots and future access to spectrum. o This could exclude India from emerging mega-constellations and severely weaken its bargaining power in global space governance, as orbital locations are limited and delays translate into irreversible strategic losses. § Weak Military Space Structure: India's Defence Space Agency (established in 2019) is still not fully empowered. It primarily functions as a coordinating body and does not have full operational command authority. Additionally, it continues to operate in a siloed manner between the Army–Navy–Air Force data streams. o In contrast, the US Space Force operates as an independent armed service. • Even Pakistan has moved forward with a fully functional space command (2024) integrated with cyber warfare, highlighting India's relative institutional backwardness. § Excessive Dependence on Foreign Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR) Data: During Operation Sindoor, India faced significant limitations in its indigenous satellite revisit times (often up to 14 days for specific locations). o Such reliance increases the risk of data denial, selective access, and strategic surprise, especially during crises. o In contrast, China's Yaogan reconnaissance satellite network, with integrated ELINT capabilities, provides continuous space-based monitoring, giving it a decisive edge in ISR dominance. • This has forced the armed forces to rely on international commercial providers such as Maxar for daily high-resolution imagery used in target verification and damage assessment. o In contrast, according to a signal intelligence intercept conducted by the Atlantic Council in 2025, China provided near real-time high-resolution imagery from its Gaofen satellites to Pakistan with a latency of less than seven-tenths of a second, underscoring disparities in ISR support. • Additionally, India's space-based electronic intelligence (ELINT) capability is limited to a single dedicated satellite (EMISAT), with no permanent ELINT or space situational awareness group, further increasing reliance on foreign ISR data. § Commercial-Strategic Disconnect: India allows foreign platforms like Google Maps to gain deep market penetration, while domestic alternatives like MapmyIndia offer better local accuracy, and India is also welcoming satellite internet providers like Starlink. o This is strategically risky, as there have been instances of Starlink's access being selectively restricted during the Ukraine conflict, highlighting vulnerability to external control. o Such an open policy poses the risk of data leaks, revenue loss, and strategic dependence, in stark contrast to China, which strictly restricts foreign GNSS and mapping dominance to maintain sovereignty. § Gaps in Space Situational Awareness (SSA): India is expanding its means (Navigational Intelligence System, IRS), increasing its ability to monitor small debris (<10 cm), but this is still inadequate compared to the widespread increase in debris in ELO, which threatens BAS with Kessler Syndrome-like events. o The geographic and visual coverage of the current NETRA project is limited compared to the US and Russia, leaving Indian space assets more vulnerable to “blind spot” collisions in diverse orbits. • For example, ISRO has performed 122 Collision Avoidance Manoeuvres (CAM) for its geostationary satellites in the last 14 years. § Component Dependency and Supply Chain Vulnerabilities: Despite the "Atmanirbhar Bharat" initiative, dependence on imports for critical components in the space sector persists, such as high-end space-grade electronics, traveling wave tubes (TWTs), and carbon fiber, making programs vulnerable to geopolitical supply crises. o The slow pace of indigenization of “space-grade” semiconductors means that strategic missions are still tied to Western export control policies or supply constraints related to Taiwan. • For example, India's spending on imports of space technology is twelve times higher than the revenue from space-related exports. § The gap between conception and implementation in reforms: Although IN-SPACe has formally opened India's space sector for private participation, significant gaps remain in on-the-ground implementation. o Private sector competitors still do not have guaranteed and predictable launch access, demand signals for defense-grade satellites are still unclear, and there is a lack of funding compared to China's state- and military-supported space ecosystem. o All these obstacles combine to limit India's ability to develop large satellite constellations and achieve sustained dominance in the space sector. What measures are needed to strengthen India's space sector? § Establishing Launch Reliability and Increasing Launch Frequency: India should move beyond a mission-centric testing system and adopt fleet-level reliability engineering. This requires adopting block upgrades, rapid feedback cycles, and incremental design validation, drawing inspiration from systems like SpaceX. o A dedicated launch assurance fund should be created to ensure that launch programs are not disrupted due to launch failures. o Also, a clear division of roles of launch vehicles is necessary in the short term (PSLV for commercial missions, LVM3 for strategic and human space flights, and SSLV for rapid and small satellite launches), which will reduce bottlenecks and make the overall system more robust. § Development of Multi-Option and Rapid Launch Infrastructure: India should move beyond dependence on a single launch site and operationalize multiple spaceports on the east coast and also develop western and southern launch options. This will reduce the risk of weather-related disruptions and increase system resilience. o Facilities such as Kulasekarapattinam along with the Satish Dhawan Space Centre located in Sriharikota can provide rapid turnaround and decentralized launch capacity. o In parallel, a dedicated military launch window with pre-integrated satellites can be developed to achieve true 'launch-on-demand' capability, which will be critical for wartime rapid replenishment and crisis response. § War-Level Security of Orbital Slots and Spectrum: India should form a national ITU Task Force to ensure early filings for GEO, LEO and MEO orbits in the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), prioritizing mega-constellations and dual-use payloads. o Orbital slots and spectrum should be viewed as strategic immovable assets, as once these limited resources are occupied by early claimants, they cannot be reclaimed and weaken national space power in the long run. § Rebuilding Navigation Sovereignty (NavIC 2.0): India should accelerate the replenishment of NavIC satellites to ensure more than 7 operational satellites at all times, supported by on-orbit spares and rapid replacement cycles under the Indian Space Research Organisation. o NavIC's compatibility should be mandated and promoted in smartphones, vehicles, UAVs and critical infrastructure through standards and government procurement policies. • Also, deep integration of NavIC in missiles, drones, logistics networks and disaster relief systems is essential to ensure the system remains robust even in the event of GPS denial or conflict. § Building a Full-Spectrum Military Space Structure: India should establish a fully empowered space command with clear operational authority, specialized cadre and independent budget. o This command should move beyond single-satellite missions and adopt constellation-based structures for ISR, ELINT and space situational awareness, ensuring continuous coverage through formation flying and redundancy. o Most importantly, space-derived data should be integrated into a shared operational picture between the Army, Navy and Air Force, enabling real-time joint decision-making in multi-domain operations. § Reducing Reliance on Foreign Data and Satcom: India should develop an indigenous ISR capability infrastructure for border surveillance, maritime domain awareness, and crisis management, and foreign satellite data should be used only as a supplement. o Foreign satcom partnerships require strict conditions such as data localization, assured access guarantees, and kill-switch security to prevent service disruption in geopolitical crises. o Such measures are extremely important to protect strategic autonomy and avoid the weaknesses exposed during past military operations. § System Hardening Against Counter-Space Threats: Given the growing threat of jamming, cyber interference, and near-orbital activities, India should invest in satellite hardening—including redundancy, rapid reconstitution capability, orbital agility, robust encryption, and on-orbit servicing. o Along with this, both ground-based and space-based space situational awareness (SSA) networks should be expanded, enabling early identification of hostile activities. o Instead of relying on space-based retaliatory weapons, India should pursue a strategy of resilience through deterrence, ensuring that its space systems remain secure, adaptable, and functional even under attack. § Coordinating Commercial Development and Strategic Needs: To expand the private space ecosystem, India should adopt an anchor procurement model, in which the government gives long-term purchase commitments for civil and defense services, reducing the risk of private satellite groups. o The availability of export finance, insurance and credit guarantees will enable Indian launch and satellite companies to compete globally. o Also, the policy focus should not be limited to launch but should be on demand creation in downstream sectors such as agriculture, urban planning, disaster management and climate-related services, enabling India to add more value. Conclusion: In the coming decade, India's space sector needs to move away from mission-based achievements towards ecosystem-level capability building based on strategic clarity and implementation discipline. IN-SPACe's decadal vision and strategy appropriately underline the integration of the private sector, the expansion of launch capacity, and downstream value creation as key engines for the development of India's space economy. However, transforming this vision into concrete results will require an empowered military space administration, assured data sovereignty, and timely accountability between institutions. Only then will India be able to ensure strategic autonomy, economic competitiveness, and credible deterrence in an increasingly competitive and contested space domain.
