Reimagining Higher Education in India for Developed India 2047: This editorial is based on The Hindu's article published on 22/01/2026, "Second-generation reforms required in higher education to make India a developed nation." The article argues that although India has significantly expanded access to higher education under the National Education Policy 2020 (NEP 2020), intensive second-generation reforms focusing on institutional functioning, quality, and the research ecosystem are essential to achieve the goal of becoming a developed nation by 2047. As India progresses towards the vision of Developed India 2047, the higher education ecosystem stands at a critical juncture where quantitative expansion has been achieved, but equality in quality remains a serious challenge. With the world's largest youth population and rapidly increasing enrollments, universities are no longer just centers of teaching but are becoming major pillars of innovation, social mobility, and national competitiveness. However, if structural expansion is not aligned with institutional capacity, this situation may be limited to an increase in certificates instead of capacity building. Therefore, deep and well-considered second-generation reforms in the areas of governance, teaching methods, and research become inevitable to transform the demographic dividend into real developmental outcomes. Current Developments in India's Higher Education System: § Multidisciplinary and Research-Oriented Universities: Under India's higher education system, in line with the National Education Policy 2020 (NEP 2020), emphasis is being given to multidisciplinary and research-focused universities instead of subject-specific institutions. o This change strengthens problem-solving capabilities, promotes innovation across disciplines, and increases the employability of graduates. • Targeted funding and outcome-based reforms indicate that the policy approach is now moving beyond mere expansion to focus on institutional excellence. o For example, under the PM-USHA scheme, more than 600 projects have been approved, including grants of approximately ₹100 crore per institution to 35 selected state universities to develop them as Multidisciplinary Education and Research Universities (MERU), thereby strengthening multidisciplinary transformation and research capacity building. § Simplification of the Regulatory Framework for Academic Autonomy: India is moving towards an integrated and unified regulatory system in the higher education sector, which seeks to eliminate fragmented monitoring by multi-level bodies such as UGC, AICTE, and NCTE. This initiative is in line with the spirit of NEP 2020. o The proposed Developed India Education Foundation Bill, 2025 (formerly HECI) aims to streamline regulatory processes, reduce compliance burden, and enable multidisciplinary education. o It envisions separate vertical structures for regulation, accreditation, and academic standards, thereby promoting transparency and trust-based governance. • Matters related to funding will remain with the government, while medical and legal education have been kept outside its scope. § Development of a Talent Ecosystem for Research and Innovation: India is actively expanding doctoral education and competitive fellowship programs to build a robust research workforce. o Research quality, interdisciplinary collaboration, and global participation are being improved through enhanced funding and national missions. o The PMRF 2.0 scheme announced in the Budget 2025–26 aims to provide 10,000 new fellowships in the next five years, thereby attracting outstanding talent towards doctoral research in leading institutions such as IITs and IISc. • These initiatives are important steps in transforming India from a knowledge-consuming nation to a knowledge-creating nation. § Mainstreaming Artificial Intelligence and Digital Learning: Artificial Intelligence (AI) and digital technologies are being integrated into the curriculum, teaching methods, and institutional governance, enabling students to acquire future-oriented skills. o This is increasing the personalization of learning, research productivity, and industry coordination. o A dedicated Center of Excellence has been announced with an allocation of ₹500 crore for AI in education in the Union Budget 2025–26. • Simultaneously, the Automated Permanent Academic Account Registry (APAAR) is being developed as an essential digital backbone of the education ecosystem, integrating degrees, skills, and credit transfers. o Additionally, AICTE has declared the year 2025 as the “Year of AI,” directing all affiliated institutions to submit AI implementation plans and adopt the AI Affirmation Pledge, which mandates the inclusion of AI courses even in non-computer science subjects such as mechanical and civil engineering. § Meaningful Inclusion of Indian Knowledge Traditions and Languages: Under higher education reforms, Indian Knowledge Systems (IKS) are being incorporated into mainstream subjects. o Linguistic inclusion in teaching and research expands access and relevance. This synergy of tradition and modernity strengthens India's civilizational self-confidence. o For example, institutions such as IIT Kharagpur have launched postgraduate programs in IKS in collaboration with the Ministry of Education, for which curriculum development and faculty training initiatives are being conducted. § Democratization of Access to Knowledge and Research: Through the "One Nation, One Subscription (ONOS)" initiative, the government has centralized the subscription to research journals, thereby removing financial barriers to research in smaller and state-funded universities. o As a result, a student from a remote Tier-2 college can now access high-level global research similar to a student at IIT. This initiative provides access to over 13,000 research journals and covers more than 6,300 government academic and research institutions. • Approximately 18 million students, faculty members, and researchers are benefiting from this. § Academic Customization Through Credit Mobility: The Academic Bank of Credit (ABC) and multi-entry–multi-exit systems are institutionalizing lifelong learning and student mobility. o These reforms facilitate students to choose customized learning paths while maintaining academic coherence. Transparency and portability of certificates are being ensured through digital platforms. o For example, Delhi University has fully implemented credit transfer and modular degree structures. § Expansion of Internationalization and Global Academic Collaboration: The structure of India's higher education system is now becoming more global through student mobility, joint research, and institutional partnerships. o International experience strengthens academic standards and research impact, and globalizes knowledge standards in India. o Under the UGC's 2023 regulations, the University of Southampton has established its first foreign campus in Gurugram. • Additionally, at least 12 foreign universities have been approved to establish campuses in Bengaluru, Delhi-NCR, Mumbai, and Chennai. § Institutionalizing Student Welfare and Mental Health: Student welfare is now being considered an essential component of academic success and institutional quality. Counseling, guidance, and stress-management systems are being integrated into academic planning in higher education institutions. o In order to create a healthier and more adaptive learning ecosystem. o In this context, the UGC has issued an integrated mental health policy, which mandates the establishment of counseling centers in all higher education institutions. • Several central universities have also effectively implemented full-time mental health and mentorship frameworks. What are the main problems associated with India's higher education system? § The "Degree–Employability" Paradox: Many universities in India today function as "degree production centers," where theoretical and traditional learning is prioritized over the acquisition of practical skills. As a result, a workforce is being created that is equipped with certificates but not professionally competent. o This discrepancy is "creating graduates who are facing the problem of unemployment," due to which industries either have to bear excessive training costs or keep positions vacant despite high youth unemployment. o According to the TeamLease EdTech report "From Degree Factories to Employability Hubs" published in January 2026, approximately 75% of higher education institutions (HEIs) in India are not prepared to meet the needs of the industry. § Faculty-Level Constraints and 'Ad-Hoc Culture': There is a severe shortage of permanent teachers in the higher education system, due to which institutions have become excessively dependent on temporary (ad-hoc) teachers. Such teachers do not have sufficient incentives for research nor job security, which affects institutional memory, guidance systems, and academic continuity. o This situation also hinders India's aspirational goal of becoming a "Vishwaguru" (world leader). o In 2024, a parliamentary committee reported that 56% of professor positions are vacant in central universities, and more than 5,410 teaching positions are vacant in central institutions in total. § Accessibility Concerns Despite Increased Public Expenditure: Although the allocation for higher education has been increased in the Union Budget 2024–25, the trend of privatization and self-financed courses continues, which limits the real distributive impact of public expenditure. o A large portion of the increased financial resources is absorbed in institutional expansion and capital expenditure instead of scholarships and fellowships, due to which students from marginalized sections are affected by the increasing fee burden. • Consequently, access for economically weaker sections remains limited, and student discontent persists despite budgetary increases. § Mental Health and Student Welfare Crisis: Due to academic hyper-competition, caste-based discrimination, and social isolation in university campuses, they are being transformed into high-pressure environments, while institutional support mechanisms are inadequate. o The lack of functional counseling centers and preconceived notions about seeking help being a deficiency or weakness are turning student mental distress into a silent epidemic. o For example, a study by NIMHANS (Bengaluru) found that 12.3% of participants expressed suicidal thoughts in the past year, 5.2% attempted suicide, and among those who had such thoughts, 34.8% actually attempted it. § Research Stagnation and Bureaucratic Complexities: Although the National Research Foundation (ANRF) has been established, India's research output is hampered due to delays in grant disbursement, limited autonomy, and weak infrastructure in state universities. o In the current ecosystem, "safe" theoretical research papers are incentivized more than high-risk innovation or patentable technology, which limits India's research impact globally. o Additionally, India's research and development (R&D) expenditure is only 0.64% of GDP, which is extremely low compared to China's approximately 2% and limits innovation potential. § Challenges Related to Ranking and Accreditation: India's higher education quality assurance system is plagued by limited accreditation coverage and questions about reliability, which affects the utility of institutional quality indicators and rankings. o Although NAAC plays an important role in promoting standards, procedural flaws and integrity issues have eroded confidence, affecting the image of institutions domestically and internationally. o Currently, more than 30% of higher education institutions in India are not accredited by NAAC, due to which a large number of institutions are operating without formal quality recognition. • This also limits the impact of frameworks such as NIRF, and the number of Indian universities securing top positions in global rankings remains low. § Regional and Social Disparities in Accessibility (GER): Although the overall Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER) is improving, this figure masks deep regional and social inequalities. In contrast to high enrolment in southern states, access to higher education remains limited in interior and backward regions. o The collectivization of education has not been inclusive, as students in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities grapple with weaker infrastructure and quality problems compared to metropolitan institutions. o The north–south divide is clearly evident—where the GER of Tamil Nadu (47%) and Kerala (41.3%) is almost double that of northern states such as Bihar (17.1%) and Uttar Pradesh (24.1%). What measures are necessary to empower higher education in India? § Institutional Autonomy Through "Graded Self-Governance": To eliminate the "Affiliation Raj" prevalent in higher education, regulatory bodies will have to rapidly transition towards a maturity-based graded autonomy model. o Under this, a "light but effective" supervision mechanism should be adopted in place of a rigid, top-down inspection system, in which high-performing institutions have complete freedom at the academic, administrative, and financial levels. o Freeing operational decisions from bureaucratic approvals will enable universities to launch innovative courses quickly. This will enable the heads of institutions to function as academic entrepreneurs, not just as administrators, and enable quick response to global academic trends. § "Triple Helix" Innovation Cluster for Regional Development: Instead of functioning as separate units, universities should become the center of local innovation clusters under the "Triple Helix Model" based on government-academia-industry collaboration. o Institutions should be encouraged to link their research to the specific economic and developmental needs of their geographical region—such as agri-tech in rural areas, textiles or manufacturing research in industrial areas. o This will develop a symbiotic ecosystem where students will work on real regional problems, industries will get targeted research solutions, and universities will become inevitable drivers of local economic development. § Mainstreaming Apprenticeship-Based Degree Structure: It is essential to shift higher education from "theory-driven" to "competency-based" certification. Under this, general undergraduate courses (BA, BSc, BCom) should mandatorily include an apprenticeship component. o By linking the National Credit Framework (NCrF) to industry skill standards, 30–40% of degree credits can be earned in a workplace or corporate environment. • This will eliminate the employability gap, as students will develop the tacit knowledge and soft skills that are not possible through classroom teaching alone. § Modernization of Faculty System Through "Professor of Practice": To address the challenges related to the faculty system, a two-pronged strategy is necessary—on the one hand, continuous professional development (CPD) should be institutionalized for existing teachers, and on the other hand, the Professor of Practice (PoP) model should be generalized. o Under the PoP system, experienced experts from industry and the professional field can contribute to teaching without the traditional PhD requirement, which will incorporate practical realities into the courses. • At the same time, tenure for regular faculty should not only be linked to research publications but also to teaching innovation and institutional service, which will develop a balanced and guidance-focused academic culture. § Financial Stability Through Diversified Endowments: Public universities will have to reduce their dependence on state grants and develop commercial endowment management mechanisms. o On the lines of the Higher Education Financing Agency (HEFA) model, institutions should be empowered to create sustainable funds through their alumni networks, patent portfolios, and consulting services. o This will enable universities to make long-term investments in high-risk–high-return research infrastructure and student welfare and remain protected from financial instability. § Democratization of Quality Through "Phygital" Learning Ecosystem: The higher education of the future lies in the physical + digital (Phygital) model, which can break the "Iron Triangle" challenge of access, cost, and quality. Top institutions should act as hub universities, providing high-quality teaching materials to colleges (spokes) in remote areas through the Academic Bank of Credit (ABC). o With this network-based model, a student from a rural college will also be able to earn credit from a premier institution, thereby reducing geographical inequality. § "International Quality at Home" Through Twinning Programs and Joint Research: Instead of just sending students abroad, attention should be paid to providing the experience of international education and research within the country. o For this, twinning programs and joint degree regulations should be simplified and partnerships with foreign higher education institutions should be encouraged. o This will bring world-class courses and best practices to Indian universities, stop brain drain, and improve academic standards through collaborative online international learning (COIL). Conclusion: India's higher education journey is no longer limited to expansion, but also demands inclusion and excellence. The goal of Developed India 2047 will only be realized when universities can function autonomously, innovate fearlessly, and provide large-scale employment-oriented knowledge. The aim of second-generation reforms should be to transform higher education institutions from mere degree-awarding centers into powerful engines of research, skills, and social mobility. Only then will India be able to transform its demographic dividend into long-term developmental gains.
