Building India's Deep-Tech Stack • Government Policies and Interventions This editorial is based on the article ‘A budget for the deep tech ecosystem & the mitras building it’ published in The Hindu Business Line on 02/01/2026. This article highlights India's transformation towards building a full-fledged deep-tech ecosystem through strategic policy and infrastructural support. It also analyzes the key challenges and reforms needed to scale deep-tech innovation widely. India's deep-tech ecosystem is no longer dependent on isolated startups or subsidy-based innovation, but is being driven by a planned effort to develop end-to-end technological capabilities. The focus has shifted from just software-based capabilities to strategic areas such as semiconductors, AI infrastructure and advanced manufacturing where scale, skills and supply-chain are as important as capital. Recent policy signals indicate a transition away from consumption-based digitization to production-driven, capacity-focused growth. This shift establishes Deep Tech as both an economic instrument and a strategic asset for India. What is Deep Tech? § Introduction: Deep Tech refers to technologies that are based on fundamental scientific research and advanced engineering, aimed at solving complex and large-scale problems rather than enabling incremental or convenience-based solutions. o Unlike traditional digital startups, which mainly rely on business model innovation, deep-tech ventures are based on breakthroughs in fields such as Artificial Intelligence, Semiconductors, Biotechnology, Advanced Materials, Robotics, Quantum Computing and Clean Energy. § Key Features of Deep Tech o Science and Research Based: Deep Tech is based on fundamental research and development, often emerging from laboratories, universities or long-term industrial research. • Progress does not depend only on rapid market changes, but also on progress in physics, chemistry, biology, mathematics and engineering. o Long term and high risk: Development cycles are long, capital intensive and uncertain. Returns are realized in years, not quarters, so long-term capital (patient capital), state support and mission-oriented policy become important. o Difficult-to-copy competitive advantage: Deep Tech provides a sustainable competitive advantage through intellectual property, embedded technical knowledge, manufacturing capabilities and system-level integration, while simple software platforms can be copied relatively easily. o Dependence on Infrastructure and Ecosystem: Success requires specialized infrastructure, fabs, clean rooms, data centers, testing facilities as well as skilled human capital and flexible supply chains. § Key areas associated with Deep Tech: Deep Tech spans many industries, but it is most prominent in the following areas: o Advanced Materials: Development of new polymers, nano-materials or battery technologies. o Biotechnology: Synthetic biology, genomics and drug-discovery. o Artificial Intelligence: Advanced machine learning algorithms (beyond simple chatbots) and computer vision. o Robotics and Drones: Automation for agriculture, manufacturing and logistics. o Quantum Computing: Next generation processors that solve problems impossible for traditional computers. o Space Tech: Equipment related to satellite technology, rocketry and space exploration. regulatory framework governing deep-tech in India? § Data, Digital and AI Governance o Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023: Regulates the collection, processing and cross-border flow of personal data, which directly affects AI, Big Data and platform-based deep-tech firms. o IndiaAI Mission (Policy Framework): Sets standards for responsible AI and aims to establish indigenous AI models in line with global standards, while also addressing unique challenges and opportunities. § Intellectual Property and Innovation Protection o Patents Act, 1970: Basic framework for protecting deep-tech innovations in artificial intelligence, biotechnology, semiconductors and materials science. o National Intellectual Property Rights Policy, 2016: Promotes patenting, commercialization and industry-academia collaboration, which is important for the expansion of deep-tech. § Startup, Investment and Capital Regulation o Startup India Initiative: Provides regulatory exemptions, tax incentives and expedited IP support for deep-tech startups. • The government has removed the three-year existence condition for deep-tech startups to get recognition under DSIR's Industrial Research and Development Promotion Program. o Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) Regulations: Regulates Venture Capital Funds, AIFs and emerging frameworks for startup listing and exits. o National Deep Tech Startup Policy (Draft): It is a proposed framework to promote high-tech innovation by addressing challenges related to funding, research and development, intellectual property and regulations, which aims to enhance India's global competitiveness in areas such as artificial intelligence, semiconductors and space technology. o Research National Research Foundation (ANRF): It aims to provide high-level strategic direction for research, innovation and entrepreneurship in the fields of natural science, including scientific and technological interrelationships of mathematical sciences, engineering and technology, environment and earth sciences, health and agriculture, and humanities and social sciences. § Strategic and National Security Regulation o National Security Directive on the Telecommunications Sector: It orders telecommunications service providers (TSPs) to purchase equipment only from 'trusted sources' and products. o Foreign Direct Investment Policy (Press Notes): Screens foreign investment in sensitive deep-tech sectors such as defense technology, space and telecommunications. § Sector-specific Deeptech Framework o Indian Space Policy, 2023: Opens space launches, satellites and related applications to private deep-tech companies. o Biotechnology Regulation (DBT & CDSCO): Regulates genomics, bio-manufacturing and health-tech innovations. o Telecommunications Act, 2023: Updates regulations for next generation networks and satellite communications. o Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) Schemes: Provides regulatory-cum-financial assistance for deep-tech in semiconductors, electronics, advanced batteries and electric vehicles. What are the current developments in India's deeptech ecosystem? § Sovereign AI Infrastructure and 'Compute-as-a-Public-Good': India has decisively moved from being an 'AI consumer' to an 'AI builder', recognizing computing power as critical public infrastructure. o The 'IndiaAI Mission' effectively democratizes access for startups, prevents the monopoly of global technology leaders and enables the training of indigenous Large Language Models (LLM), which capture India's linguistic diversity and unique cultural datasets, which global models often fail to portray. o The country's national computing capacity has crossed 34,000 GPUs, which are available to startups at subsidized rates. • Indigenous models like Sarvam-1 are being deployed to create an independent AI ecosystem in India and reduce dependence on foreign APIs. § Semiconductor fabs start operating: India's semiconductor manufacturing story has now moved beyond just the 'planning' stage to the 'implementation' stage, where the first commercial fabs are close to production. This directly connects India to the global chip value chain. o This change reduces strategic vulnerability in electronics manufacturing and creates a domestic 'multiplier ecosystem' for fabless design startups, who can now prototype locally instead of waiting for slots at Taiwan's TSMC. o Tata Electronics' manufacturing unit located in Dholera started installing equipment at the end of 2025 and the first commercial 28nm chips are expected to be ready by the end of 2026. • Also, this facility has been opened for 'tape-out' for Indian startups, which supports chips in the 28nm-90nm range. § Privatization of space launch services: The Indian space sector has successfully transformed from ISRO's monopoly to a commercially vibrant 'Space 2.0' economy due to relaxation in regulation of Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) and technology transfer. o This has given private companies a chance to gain a share of the attractive global launch market, which is moving beyond just satellite manufacturing to provide 'entry-on-demand' services for small satellite constellations. o For example, Skyroot Aerospace has scheduled its first orbital mission for the Vikram-1 vehicle in early 2026. • Currently, the Indian space economy is valued at approximately US$ 8.4 billion, which constitutes a 2% share in the global space market, in which companies such as Agnikul Cosmos are commercializing 3D-printed engines for rapid deployment. § Institutionalization of Quantum Capabilities: The National Quantum Mission (NQM) has moved beyond the policy-making stage to the establishment of 'Thematic Hubs' (T-Hubs), which are functioning as Section-8 companies. o This unique structure allows educational institutions to work with corporate agility, thereby promoting deep-tech developments in quantum sensing and cryptography, which are important to future-proof national security against quantum-decryption threats. o Currently 4 T-Hubs are fully operational and are engaged in activities such as technology development, human resource development, entrepreneurship promotion, industry collaboration and international cooperation. § Bio-manufacturing and BioE3 Paradigm: The BioE3 policy (Biotechnology for Economy, Environment and Employment) signifies a strategic transition towards 'high-performance bio-manufacturing' to replace petrochemical-based industries. o By establishing 'Biofoundries', the state is enabling a seamless 'lab-to-market' system for synthetic biology, which is focusing on sustainable alternatives such as bio-plastics and smart proteins, which are important for the net-zero transition. o A centralized Biofoundry at ICGEB, New Delhi is now operational, providing 'Design-Build-Test-Learn' services to startups. • This policy supports 6 thematic areas, which will help the bio-economy reach $165.7 billion in 2024, with the goal of reaching $300 billion by 2030. § Indigenization of Deep Technology in the Defense Sector: Recognizing that modern warfare is not defined by the quantity of hardware but by technology, the Ministry of Defense has launched active grant mechanisms for 'strategic independence'. o The ADITI scheme (under iDEX) works to bridge the 'Valley of Death' for deep-tech startups in the defense sector by providing adequate risk capital. o An amount of ₹750 crore has been allocated for the year 2023–26, under which a grant of up to ₹25 crore is being given per startup. Companies like QuBeats have received support for the development of GPS-independent quantum navigation systems for the Indian Navy. § Green Hydrogen Valleys and Electrolyzer Self-Reliance: India is striving to emerge as a global export hub for green hydrogen, for which the most important component is the indigenization of electrolyzers. o By creating clusters of 'Hydrogen Valleys', the government is ensuring demand certainty (off-take assurance) for deep-tech energy startups, which is helping them to achieve economies of scale

and compete with cheaper Chinese alkaline electrolyzers. o The Union Ministry of Science and Technology has announced four 'Hydrogen Valleys' with an investment of ₹485 crore, which represent the entire value chain. Companies like GreenH (a company manufacturing PEM electrolyzers in Haryana) exemplify the success of this localization effort under the National Green Hydrogen Mission. § Research RDI Fund: A key structural solution to the 'funding winter' in Deep Tech is the operation of the Research National Research Foundation (ANRF) Fund. o Unlike common venture capital investments seeking quick returns, this government fund provides 'patient capital', which is essential for research and development-intensive areas such as materials science and clean energy, thereby reducing the risk of long-term innovation for private investors. o The Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) scheme with a financial pool of ₹1 lakh crore became operational in the second half of 2025. • It specifically targets emerging areas and provides long-term, low-cost funding to advance high-tech risk but high national strategic value deep technology projects. What are the major issues related to India's Deeptech Ecosystem? § 'Valley of Death' in development-stage financing: While early-stage grants (such as iDEX) are available, there is a serious 'missing middle' in Series B+ level development-capital, where deeptech hardware startups often fail. o Domestic venture capitalists remain risk-averse capitalists to long-lasting hardware cycles (5-7 years), they prioritize quick returns from fintech/SaaS, forcing key Indian deeptech startups to relocate their headquarters to the US or Singapore to gain access to long-term capital (patient capital). o Even after the operationalization of the ₹1 lakh crore ANRF fund, deep-tech startups get a disproportionately small share of venture capital, while hardware startups face longer and more uncertain development-stage financing cycles than software-based firms. § Impasse of 'L1' procurement process: The biggest potential customer of Deeptech (government) is still trapped in the archaic procurement system of 'Lowest Bidder' (L1). o Despite the aim of adopting innovative solutions, the administrative mechanism lacks the capacity to technically evaluate 'Quality-cum-Cost' (QCBS). As a result, many excellent indigenous deeptech products are disqualified simply because they cannot price-compete with cheaper, low-quality traditional alternatives or dumped imports made on a large scale. o The NASSCOM report of December 2025 stated that a higher number of revenue-previous startups are facing operational difficulties after receiving government grants such as iDEX, BIRAC, or TDB. • This is called the 'Grant Liquidity Trap', where the approved amount is not always available when it is most needed, which limits the working capital of existing revenue-deficient startups. o For example, iDEX had signed 430 contracts with 619 startups and MSMEs. However, this shows active participation, but it does not clarify how many of these have been converted into long-term commercial procurement contracts compared to initial prototype research and development grants. § 'Lab-to-Land' Commercialization Gap: India is facing a 'Productization Deficit', where, despite record-breaking patent filings, they are not being converted into commercial products due to strict intellectual property sharing norms in academia. o Professors and scientists at IIT and CSIR laboratories lack incentives or legal 'safe harbors' to start companies, creating a situation where unprecedented research is limited to journals, rather than generating economic value in the market. o Although India was ranked sixth globally in patent filings in 2025, commercial use of patents is significantly lower compared to global leaders such as the US. • In addition, it takes an average of about 58 months to dispose of a patent application in India, while it takes about 20 months in China and 23 months in the US. § Import Dependence of Critical Components: The 'Make in India' hardware ecosystem is critically vulnerable to geopolitical supply chain shocks as it still relies on imported 'intermediaries' such as sensors, precision motors and special gases.

o Startups are often just 'system integrators' rather than 'component manufacturers', which means that a single trade restriction imposed by China or Taiwan could halt an entire fleet of Indian drones or robotics. o Currently, India imports approximately 90-95 percent of its semiconductor and electronics components, with China, Malaysia, Taiwan, Thailand and Singapore being the major suppliers. § Lack of PhD talent and R&D skills: There is a huge shortage of 'deep science' talent capable of bridging the gap between theoretical physics/mathematics and engineering applications. o Although India produces millions of coders, there are very few researchers with PhD level expertise required for quantum computing or AI algorithm design, due to which startups are forced to hire expensive talent from Europe or America, which increases their costs. o For example, according to a Stanford report, India registered the highest increase in AI talent density globally at 252% during 2016 and 2024. • However, it is still not included in the top 15 countries in terms of overall AI talent density, the list is led by Israel and Singapore. § Testing Infrastructure Bottlenecks: Industrial-grade testing facilities (wind tunnels, anechoic chambers, radiation hardening labs) for hardware prototypes are often limited to public enterprises such as ISRO/DRDO. o Startups have to waste valuable months waiting for testing slots, which slows down their 'iteration cycles' and makes them non-competitive compared to global competitors who have rapid feedback loops. o For example, virtually every private spacetech company (Skyroot, Agnikul, Bellatrix) has to queue for access to ISRO facilities, as replicating them is economically impossible for a Series A startup. § Regulatory Uncertainty in the 'Gray Zone': Regulation lags behind innovation, creating legal 'gray zones' in areas such as space mining, generative AI liability or autonomous bio-manufacturing. o Lack of clear liability frameworks (for example, 'who will be responsible if a patient dies due to AI diagnosis?') prevents large enterprises from adopting deeptech solutions, which blocks the B2B market for startups. o For example, while the Indian Space Policy 2023 allows private companies (NGEs) to launch satellites, the long-pending Space Activities Bill has not yet become law. • Similarly, the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 holds doctors and hospitals liable for negligence. • However, the law is unclear for this—is the doctor negligent for relying on AI, or is the software vendor liable for the product defect—if an autonomous AI diagnostic device (e.g., to detect diabetic retinopathy) fails to diagnose? § Valuation Discrepancy and Intellectual Property Securitization: Indian financial institutions lack the basic capacity to evaluate 'intangible assets' such as intellectual property or algorithms and emphasize on traditional collateral (land/buildings) for loan financing. o This forces deeptech founders to excessively reduce equity in the early stages to purchase equipment, due to which they have very little 'stake in the company' by the time they reach Series-B, which ironically discourages future investors. o Because patents are intangible, banks often demand a significant cash amount in fixed deposits to provide bank guarantees. This almost eliminates the benefit of the loan, as the startup should already have the cash it is trying to borrow. What measures are needed to strengthen India's deep-tech ecosystem? § Enabling 'Patient Capital' through Sovereign Risk-Guarantee: To eliminate the 'Valley of Death' of hardware startup failure, the government should establish a dedicated 'Sovereign Deeptech Fund-of-Funds' that prioritizes long-term investment over quick exits. o Most importantly, this fund should provide 'First-Loss Default Guarantees' to private venture capitalists, which effectively reduces their risk of entering high-growth areas such as Quantum or Space-Tech.

o This mechanism shifts the financial paradigm from 'risk aversion' to 'risk sharing', which catalyzes the flow of private institutional capital into asset-rich innovations that take 7-10 years to mature. § Make Quality-cum-Cost (QCBS) Public Procurement Mandatory: The state should transform from a passive regulator to an active 'market maker'. o Implementing a strict framework of Quality-cum-Cost Based Selection (QCBS) ensures that indigenous deeptech products are evaluated on the basis of performance and lifecycle value rather than just initial price. o By reserving 'First Buyer' quotas for startups in defense and infrastructure projects, the government provides critical revenue clarity and commercial validation required for global expansion. § Building a 'National Testing Grid' (Lab-as-a-Service): To significantly reduce the burden of capital expenditure (CAPEX) on startups, all public research and development infrastructure (ISRO, DRDO, CSIR laboratories) should be mapped on a unified 'National Testing Grid'. o This digital portal will work on the 'Lab-as-a-Service' model, which will facilitate private startups to book wind tunnels, anechoic chambers or bio-safety labs on a 'pay-per-use' basis. o Democratizing access to these high-value assets accelerates the 'prototype-to-production' cycle and can prevent startups from replicating already expensive infrastructure. § Liberalizing Academic Spin-offs and IP Monetization Norms: To unlock the value embedded in universities, a standardized 'Professor-of-Practice' policy is required, which allows faculty members to co-found companies without leaving their academic positions. o Also, a 'National IP Exchange' should be created to function as a marketplace for inactive publicly funded patents and simplify licensing agreements. o This promotes a culture of 'translational research', ensuring that unprecedented scientific papers translate into commercial entities rather than remaining theoretical assets. § Establishment of Thematic Regulatory Sandboxes: For emerging 'gray zone' technologies such as generative AI, space mining or synthetic biology, the government should notify 'Live Regulatory Sandboxes'. o These controlled environments provide temporary legal exemptions to startups to test products with real customers under supervision, thereby circumventing strict traditional laws. o This 'adaptive regulation' approach allows policy to evolve in sync with technology, thereby preventing innovation barriers, as well as creating data to develop long-term safety guidelines. § Deep Science Talent and 'Industrial PhD' Fellowships: To address the shortage of specialized talent, it is necessary to launch 'Industrial PhD Fellowships', where researchers divide their time between university laboratories and deeptech startup research and development centers. o The government should provide funding for these positions so that a chain of 'scientific founders' can be created who are capable of bridging the gap between theoretical physics and engineering application. o This initiative creates 'talent' in specialized areas such as cryogenics and photonics, which is currently the biggest operational hurdle to the expansion of hardware ventures. § Component-Level Production-Linked Incentive (PLI): Moving beyond final assembly, the PLI framework should be extended to a 'Component-Linked Incentive' (CLI) scheme for deep-tech components such as sensors, precision actuators and special alloys. o Promoting domestic production of these critical core components protects the ecosystem from geopolitical supply shocks and trade restrictions. o This strategy builds a resilient 'sovereign supply chain', ensuring that Indian startups are not just system integrators dependent on foreign raw materials. § Intellectual Property Supported Financing and Valuation Standards: To bridge the loan gap, the financial regulator should codify standards for 'Intellectual Property Supported Financing', which would allow banks to accept patents and proprietary algorithms as valid collateral for loans. o This requires training a cadre of 'technical valuers' who can accurately assess the monetization potential of intangible assets. o Recognizing IP as a valid economic asset-class will make loan capital available to startups that are physically 'asset-light' but intellectually 'asset-heavy', preventing excessive equity-dilution.

Conclusion: India's deep-tech journey signifies a transformation from fragmented innovation to strategic capacity-building, where technology, talent and infrastructure are being integrated on a large scale. However, this momentum could come to a halt before reaching maturity if development-stage financial deficits, complex government procurement processes and commercialization hurdles are not addressed. In the upcoming phase, the role of the government should not be limited to granting but should be as a market-builder and risk-sharing partner. If implemented effectively, Deep Tech can become the strong foundation of India's economic strength, strategic autonomy and long-term global competitiveness.
