Developmental Challenges of Andaman and Nicobar Islands

This article is based on the article titled "A hidden epidemic in Andaman" published in The Hindu on 18/01/2026. This article reviews the interrelationships of the strategic geographical location, ecological prosperity, indigenous communities and defense importance of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and highlights the challenges of balancing national security with environmental stability and human welfare. Situated at the strategic maritime confluence of the Bay of Bengal and the Eastern Indian Ocean, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, as India's easternmost security outpost, ensure the security of important international sea routes. Yet behind the captivating image of blue waters and prosperous tourism, these islands face a serious social crisis, where the suicide rate is three times the national average, highlighting the deep-seated tensions inherent in this isolated geographical region. With a small and scattered population of approximately four lakhs, this paradox shows how strategic importance and natural beauty can coexist with deep human sensitivity.

Andaman and Nicobar Islands
§ This is an archipelago of more than 570 islands, spread over a distance of more than 700 km in the north-south direction at the confluence of the Bay of Bengal and the Eastern Indian Ocean.
o Situated approximately 1,300 km from the Indian mainland and near the Strait of Malacca (one of the world's busiest sea routes), these islands are located on India's far eastern border, serving as a natural maritime outpost monitoring important global sea routes.

What is the importance of Andaman and Nicobar Islands for India?
§ Environment and Biodiversity Sensitivity: The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are one of the most ecologically sensitive regions of India and are part of the globally recognized biodiversity hotspot in the India-Burma biogeographical region.
o Approximately 90% of the land area of the Great Nicobar Biosphere Reserve is covered with forests and includes diverse ecosystems, including tropical moist evergreen forests, mountain ranges up to 642 meters high (Mount Thulier), and extensive coastal plains.
• Despite accounting for only 0.25% of India's total land area, Andaman and Nicobar are home to 10% of India's fauna.
o These islands have extensive mangrove ecosystems, which are among India's richest ecosystems and provide natural coastal protection against cyclones, storm surges, and tsunamis, as seen during the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami.
• Along the coastline, coral reefs are the basis of high marine biodiversity and fisheries.

§ Anthropological Heritage: These islands host one of the world's last uncontacted tribes, representing an invaluable 'human heritage frontier' and defining India's commitment to indigenous rights and conservationism. Many of these tribes are classified as Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) due to their small numbers, pre-agricultural livelihoods, and extreme isolation.
o Tribes such as the Great Andamanese, Jarawa, Onge, Sentinelese, Nicobarese, and Shompen, representing diverse stages of human social development from completely isolated hunter-gatherers to culturally distinct horticulturalists, make these islands a unique repository of irreplaceable cultural and anthropological knowledge.

§ Military Power Projection (The 'Iron Wall' Defense): The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are India's forward maritime stronghold in the eastern Indian Ocean, providing convenient monitoring and deterrence near important sea routes.
o The Andaman and Nicobar Command is India's only tri-service command, integrating the Army, Navy, and Air Force to enable rapid joint operations.
o Naval and air bases such as INS Utkrosh and INS Baaz strengthen maritime domain awareness and power projection.
• All these assets together provide a stable foundation for India's EEZ security, HADR operations, and the Indo-Pacific strategy to realize the Sagar Vision.

§ Geo-economic Transformer (Trans-shipment Hub): These islands are favorably located to reduce India's dependence on foreign ports such as Colombo and Singapore by establishing a trans-shipment hub in Galathea Bay.
o This shift will offset the massive revenue losses to foreign centers and directly connect India to the primary east-west shipping route, significantly reducing logistics costs for Indian exporters.
o Currently, 75% of India's trans-shipped goods are handled abroad, resulting in a huge expenditure of foreign exchange.
• The proposed port will ultimately have the capacity to handle 16 million containers per year, and in the first phase, it will handle more than 4 million containers, resulting in significant savings in India's foreign exchange.

§ Geo-Strategic Chokepoint: The Andaman and Nicobar Islands have transformed India's defensive position into an aggressive advantage by dominating the routes of the Six Degree Channel and the Strait of Malacca.
o Due to this proximity, India can disrupt hostile naval activities or energy supplies during conflicts, creating a credible 'Malacca Dilemma' for adversaries like China, which is heavily dependent on this route for energy security.
o The Strait of Malacca is the shortest sea route between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific Ocean, facilitating the annual passage of an estimated 82,000 ships.
• More than 40% of global trade passes through this channel, including 80% of China's crude oil imports.
o In addition, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands act as an important monitoring center, filling 'blind spots' in the Indian Ocean Region (IOR) against submarine and irregular warfare threats.
o These islands provide sensor chains that monitor the activities of Chinese research vessels and submarines to maintain a free and open Indo-Pacific.
• For example, the Coco Islands (Myanmar), which are suspected to have a Chinese intelligence presence, are located just 55 km to the north.

§ Key to Strengthening India's Energy Security: The vast maritime area around the Andaman and Nicobar Islands is filled with unsaturated sedimentary basins that act as a strategic 'energy store' for India's future hydrocarbon needs.
o Exploiting these deepwater frontier areas reduces dependence on imports and affirms sovereign rights over marine resources, thereby countering efforts by hostile elements in the Andaman Sea to conduct illegal exploration.
o For example, in September 2025, the Union Petroleum Minister announced the discovery of natural gas in the Andaman Basin in the Shri Vijayapuram-2 well, approximately 17 km from the Andaman coast, confirming the region's hydrocarbon potential.

What are the major issues related to the Andaman and Nicobar Islands?
§ Irreversible Ecological Destruction: Large-scale infrastructure projects, especially the Great Nicobar Island (GNI) project, threaten to destroy rainforests and important marine nesting areas for endangered species.
o Critics argue that the 'compensatory afforestation' done in mainland states is not capable of truly replicating the complex biodiversity of the UNESCO Biosphere Reserve.
o For example, official estimates suggest that 8.5 to 10 lakh trees may be cut down. However, environmentalists say the actual number could reach 10 million. Also, this project may emit huge amounts of carbon dioxide.
• In addition, this project poses a threat to Galathea Bay, which is a major nesting site for Giant Leatherback Turtles.

§ Existential Threats to Sovereignty: Developmental encroachment on tribal reserve areas threatens the existence of particularly vulnerable tribal groups (PVTGs) such as the Shompen and Jarawa through forced contact and residential loss.
o Projects often disregard the Forest Rights Act (2006) and fail to obtain "free, prior, and informed consent" from communities whose 10,000-year-old heritage is at stake.
• For example, the Tribal Council of Little and Great Nicobar has accused the administration of falsely claiming agreement on tribal rights for a mega project.

§ Strategic Militarization vs. Diplomatic Tension: Attempts to transform the islands into an "impregnable aircraft carrier" to counter China's maritime expansion risk increasing regional tensions with Southeast Asian neighbors.
o Strengthening the Andaman and Nicobar Command may secure the important route of the Strait of Malacca, but it also encourages "gray-zone" competition and potential naval blockades, which could disrupt the Indo-Pacific balance.

§ Geologic and Climatic Sensitivity: This island group is located in seismic zone V, making it highly vulnerable to devastating earthquakes and tsunamis, which threaten the stability of billions of dollars worth of concrete infrastructure.
o Rising sea levels in the Bay of Bengal, which are currently more than 30% above the global average, are further increasing the risk of low-lying islands being submerged and natural barriers such as mangroves being destroyed.
• For example, the 2004 tsunami caused a permanent land subsidence of 15 feet at Indira Point.

§ Maritime Gray Zone: The vast, uncontrolled coastline creates an "unsecured border," making it vulnerable to illegal, unregulated, and unreported (IUU) fishing and infiltration by non-state actors such as Rohingya refugees.
o This forces the Indian Coast Guard to deploy high-value assets for low-level surveillance, weakening the security network and allowing foreign actors to map hydrographic data under the guise of fishing.
• For example, in December 2025, 8 Myanmar poachers were arrested in the North and Middle Andaman district during "Operation Coral Shield," from whom 800 kg of sea cucumber was seized.

§ Logistical Sensitivity and Food Security: These islands are highly dependent on essential goods sourced from the mainland, making the supply chain vulnerable to maritime disruptions and seasonal weather.
o This distance results in extremely high costs of living and a lack of nutritional security as local agricultural productivity is hampered due to limited arable land and high salinity.
o High transportation and logistics costs drive up the prices of basic commodities because agricultural production is limited due to limited land and challenging terrain, increasing dependence on food supplies imported from the mainland.

§ Lack of Digital Connectivity: Despite the commissioning of undersea cables, the archipelago still faces unreliable internet bandwidth and "digital shadows" in remote islands, which hinders governance and the digital economy.
o Inadequate air and sea connectivity between the islands limits the economic benefits from Port Blair to the southern islands, leaving peripheral communities isolated from health care and emergency services.
• For example, the CANI (Chennai-Andaman and Nicobar Islands) cable provides Port Blair with a speed of 2 x 200 Gbps, but remote islands still experience low speeds; inter-island ferry services are delayed by an average of 12-24 hours during the monsoon.

§ Water Crisis: These islands are struggling with a shortage of fresh water, and the planned increase in tourism and military personnel ignores limited aquifer recharge rates, creating a "Resource Trap."
o For example, despite heavy tropical rainfall, the geological layers in the islands are inadequate for storing groundwater and infrastructure for large-scale storage, leading to severe water shortages during the summer.
o Greater dependence on surface runoff and small reservoirs means that even a slight delay in the monsoon can create a humanitarian crisis for the developed urban population.
• For example, piped water supply in Port Blair is regularly disrupted during peak dry months.

§ Threat of Biological Invasion: The introduction of non-native species has led to an "ecological invasion" where invasive plants and animals outcompete native species, degrading forest systems essential for the island's climate resilience.
o This "bio-security" failure weakens natural ecosystem services such as soil conservation and water purification, which are important for supporting proposed large infrastructure projects.
o For example, Chital (spotted deer), introduced in the 1930s, has no natural predators and prevents forest regeneration due to overgrazing. Marine species coming from ships' ballast water pose a threat to the coral reefs that protect the islands from storm surges.

§ Social Health Crisis: These islands are currently struggling with a serious "silent epidemic," where geographical isolation, limited economic opportunities beyond government jobs, and high rates of alcohol consumption have created a "stressed" society. This social instability poses a direct threat to the workforce stability required for proposed mega-projects, as the local population is facing a mental health crisis and the psychiatric infrastructure to cope with the trauma is severely inadequate.
o For example, according to NCRB 2023 data, Andaman and Nicobar Islands has the highest suicide rate in India, at 49.6 per 100,000 population.
• South Andaman district is the most vulnerable, with a suicide rate of 51.09, more than four times the national average.

What principles should guide the development of Andaman and Nicobar Islands?
§ Strategic Self-Reliance, Not Over-Militarization: Development in sensitive areas should aim to strengthen maritime domain awareness and coastal surveillance, rather than promoting excessive infrastructure construction that damages the environment.
o Dual-use resources such as coastal radar chains, satellite-linked 'AIS' systems, advanced civilian ports with naval access, and disaster-response vessels should be prioritized.
• This approach strengthens security while avoiding excessive military bases, dredging, or construction activities that can damage sensitive coastal and island ecosystems.

§ Maintaining the Ecological Primacy of Andaman: Island-specific carrying capacity limits—such as: freshwater availability (often less than 50 million liters per day), waste assimilation capacity, and coastal load—should guide land-use zoning and project size.
o Resilience to climate change and the precautionary principle are important, as the loss of coral, mangroves, and rainforests can permanently reduce coastal security and food security.
o Therefore, development paths should be low environmental impact, phased, and ecologically limited.

§ Enhance Tribal Conservation: Development must uphold the autonomy and informed consent of indigenous communities, particularly Private Tribal Groups (PVTGs), many of whom number less than 500, making cultural loss irreversible.
o Constitutional safeguards under Articles 244 and 342, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (Protection of Aboriginal Tribes) Regulation, 1956, and the Forest Rights Act, 2006 must be implemented in letter and spirit, not ignored for strategic or economic convenience.
o Displacement or forced integration can lead to the risk of disease, the collapse of livelihoods, and the erosion of traditional ecological knowledge, which maintains more than 80% of intact forest ecosystems. Therefore, tribal existence is inextricably linked to human rights, biodiversity conservation, and long-term island sustainability.

§ Promoting Island-Specific Governance: Policy frameworks must go beyond mainland templates and reflect island constraints such as limited land, water (<50-60 MLD), high logistics costs, and disaster risk.
o Strengthening local councils, decentralizing planning and budgetary powers, and improving connectivity between islands are critical to addressing the problem of 25-40% staffing shortages and slow service delivery.
o For example, the Maldives uses atoll-level decentralized planning linked to carrying capacity, while Seychelles integrates marine spatial planning with local governance.
• Adopting such models can enable responsive, participatory, and ecologically-based governance in Indian island regions.

§ Towards Sustainable Economic Diversification: Economic diversification should prioritize low-impact, high-value models in tourism, fisheries, and the blue economy, rather than quantity-based development.
o Evidence from island economies suggests that capping the number of beds (<50 beds/sq km) and community-owned ecotourism increases local income 2-3 times more than mass tourism.
o Sustainable fisheries through no-take marine areas covering 20-30% of reefs and value-added processing can increase fishermen's income by 25-40% without depleting stocks.
• Therefore, it is necessary to adopt a long-term cost-benefit horizon (30-40 years) in project evaluation that internally incorporates ecological degradation, disaster risk, and social displacement.

§ Putting Social Welfare at the Center of Andaman's Development: Beyond physical infrastructure, development must prioritize mental health, access to health care, education, and livelihoods, especially in the most isolated islands.
o Limited availability of specialist care and prolonged mainland referrals exacerbate stress and untreated illnesses. Suicide rates in some island districts are reported to be almost four times the national average, indicating deep social and psychological deficits.
o Therefore, social cohesion, community support systems, and welfare outcomes should be seen as key indicators of development, not secondary welfare concerns.

Conclusion:
The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are a unique confluence of strategic-geographical location, ecological prosperity, and ancient cultural heritage. The future of these islands cannot be secured through narrow militarization or infrastructure-based development alone. Sustainable development in the islands lies in harmonizing India's maritime and security ambitions with ecological conservation, constitutional protection of indigenous communities, and human-centered governance. By viewing the islands not just as a strategic asset, but as a vibrant socio-ecological system, India can ensure adaptable, ethical and long-term development in the Eastern Indian Ocean.
