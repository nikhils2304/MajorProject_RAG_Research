The US intervention in Venezuela and the crisis of multilateralism This article is based on the article "The Venezuela test for UN & international law" published in The Hindustan Times on 07/01/2026. This article analyzes the US operation in Venezuela without the permission of the United Nations, which challenges international law and the protection of the Global South. In the context of India, it highlights serious concerns about the credibility of systems that uphold sovereignty and multilateralism. The recent US-led Operation Absolute Resolve resulted in the arrest of the President of Venezuela, which has reignited serious debates about the prohibition of the use of force in international law. Although Washington presents this action as law enforcement, it involved the use of military force on sovereign territory without the permission of the United Nations Security Council. Such unilateral actions pose an enormous threat to countries in the Global South that rely on charter-based security measures against power imbalances. For India, which has consistently supported multilateralism and regional sovereignty and is a leading voice seeking greater representation in the United Nations Security Council, this development raises serious concerns about the weakening credibility and effectiveness of international legal frameworks. What are the potential factors behind the US action against Venezuela? § Geopolitical strategy - "Don-Row" theory: A major motivation is the reaffirmation of US dominance in the Western Hemisphere. o The recent US administration has explicitly mentioned the Monroe Doctrine (the 1823 policy that opposed European/external intervention in America), now being called the "Don-Row Doctrine". o America considers Venezuela's close ties with Russia, China and Iran as a direct threat to regional security. • For example, China has given Venezuela more than $60 billion in loans since 2007. America considers these loans to be "exploitative loans" aimed at creating lasting dependence. § Resource Security and Oil Strategy: Venezuela has the world's largest oil reserves (estimated at 303 billion barrels). The desire to secure and stabilize these energy sources greatly influences American actions. o In December 2025, the US President claimed that Venezuela should return the oil, land and other assets that, according to him, were taken from the United States. o By "managing" or overseeing the change of power in Venezuela, America has a significant impact on global oil prices. • The administration aims to increase the production of extra sour crude, which US Gulf Coast refineries are specifically designed to process, in order to reduce energy costs for America. § Law Enforcement and "Narco-Terrorism": America has begun to present its actions not only as diplomacy, but also as a criminal justice case. o Nicolas Maduro and several high-ranking officials have been tried in US federal courts on charges of conspiracy to commit narco-terrorism. o America claims that the "Cartel of the Sons" (allegedly operated by Venezuelan officials) conspired with groups such as the FARC to supply cocaine to America. Can the use of military force on a sovereign territory be justified under the "law enforcement" objective? Arguments in favor: § Supporters of the American action argue that the traditional laws of war must evolve to meet modern, "hybrid" threats that do not involve traditional armies. o Functional equivalence of "armed attack": America argues that the "invasion" of fentanyl and the activities of state-sponsored gangs cause more American deaths per year than a traditional military attack. • By calling it "narco-terrorism", the administration presents drug trafficking as an attack under Article 51 of the UN Charter, thereby justifying military action in self-defense. o "Unwilling or unable" principle: This legal principle states that if a sovereign state (Venezuela) is either unwilling or actively cooperating with criminal groups to stop them on its soil, then it loses its right to full sovereignty. • From this perspective, America is not "invading" a country; Rather, it is "neutralizing" a threat that the local government is refusing to handle, effectively acting as a global policeman where the local system has failed. o The Ker-Frisbie Doctrine: From a purely American legal perspective, the "manner" of capturing a person does not matter as much as the "person" capturing him. • Under the Ker-Frisbie precedent, US courts have ruled that even if a defendant (such as Maduro) is brought to America by military force, the court still has the right to try him. • This provides a strong domestic incentive to use force to circumvent lax or blocked extradition processes. Arguments in opposition: § Critics, including the UN Secretary-General and various international legal bodies, argue that using the military for law enforcement is a "dangerous precedent" that undermines the global order. o Violation of Article 2(4) of the UN Charter: The basis of international law is the prohibition of the use of force against the territorial integrity of any State. • Law enforcement is a domestic power. Critics argue that if any nation can base "crime" to invade a neighbor, then the concept of sovereign borders becomes meaningless, creating a "law of the jungle" situation, where the most powerful nations act without restraint. o Lack of kinetic boundary: International law defines "armed attack" as a physical military attack (bombs, soldiers, missiles). • Most legal scholars reject the idea that narcotics or migration can be "redefined" as an armed attack. • By doing so, America is accused of "legal manipulation", justifying an operation that is actually a regime change operation by changing the definitions of words. 
o Attempt to evade the International Criminal Court (ICC): Opponents argue that if America were truly interested in "justice" and "human rights", it would have supported the ICC's ongoing investigation rather than launching a unilateral raid on Venezuela. • By arresting a head of state for trial in New York courts, America is being seen as prioritizing its own political interests and drug laws over the international frameworks established for the prosecution of mass atrocities. What impact will the tension between America and Venezuela have on India? § Energy Diversification and Refining Strategy: The immediate impact on India's energy security will be minimal as India has actively diversified its oil reserves away from Venezuela to avoid US secondary sanctions. o Recent data shows that Venezuela's share in India's crude oil market has declined significantly due to sanctions and trade barriers, falling to approximately 0.3% in fiscal year 2026 (April-October 2025), while in previous years this share was much higher. o Now India is awaiting clarity on potential new supply arrangements under US oversight, as India's refining sector, especially highly complex facilities like Reliance Industries' Jamnagar complex, are technically well-suited to process Venezuelan crude sour crude "Merey" oil. § Activating Blocked Upstream Sovereign Assets: India's government-owned ONGC Videsh (OVL) has long-standing investments in Venezuelan oil fields that have become "zombie assets" due to payment blockages. o For example, OVL has equity stakes in two Venezuelan oil fields: San Cristobal (40% stake) and Carabobo-1 (11% stake). These projects have been affected by low production and sanctions. o The US-led restructuring of Venezuela's oil sector could enable the repatriation of large amounts of outstanding dividends. § Geopolitical balance and strategic autonomy: The increasing US intervention in Venezuela has put India in a position of diplomatic balance. o New Delhi must carefully manage its relations with Washington, while also respecting its long-standing principles of sovereignty and non-interference. 
o India's official statements have expressed concern over the developments without directly condemning either side, reflecting a cautious approach that prioritizes broader strategic interests. § Macro-economic and oil market impact: Although India's current direct trade with Venezuela is limited, any change in the dynamics of Venezuela's oil production and exports could affect global crude oil prices. o Venezuela has the world's largest proven oil reserves, and increased production under a more stable structure could put downward pressure on international oil prices in the medium to long term. • This situation also highlights the failure of multilateral institutions such as the United Nations to curb unilateral interventions, leaving India and other Global South nations vulnerable to disruptions in energy supplies and global strategic dynamics. Why are multilateral organizations like the United Nations failing to prevent unilateral military interventions? § Structural inertia — “Veto Trap”: The United Nations Security Council (UNSC) becomes structurally inactive when a permanent member (P5) is either the aggressor itself or protects the aggressor state. o America, Russia and China use the veto power to protect their respective strategic interests, as a result of which the Security Council has become a forum for deadlock instead of an effective security provider. o For example, between 2015 and 2024, the veto was used a total of 47 times in the context of 37 draft resolutions and amendments. § Legal reinterpretation — undue gain in the name of “law enforcement”: Powerful nations are redefining military invasions as “cross-border law enforcement” to circumvent the prohibition on the use of force under Article 2(4) of the United Nations Charter. o By presenting interventions as "remedies of criminal acts" rather than acts of war, they seek to establish the action as a moral right, avoiding the need for UN approval. o For example, America justified "Operation Absolute Resolve" in Venezuela by citing Article 51 (self-defense) against "narco-terrorism". 
§ Financial pressure and liquidity crisis: A major reason for the failure of multilateralism is also that the United Nations is struggling with the most serious liquidity crisis in its history, which has weakened its ability to monitor or intervene in rapid conflicts. § When major donor countries withhold determined contributions to express disagreement with United Nations policies, the organization loses the operational capacity to deploy peacekeepers or neutral observers. o Currently, due to arrears of $2.4 billion in the regular budget and $2.7 billion in the peacekeeping fund, the United Nations has been forced to cut expenditure, freeze recruitment and curtail some services. § Rise of regionalism: Multilateralism is also weakening because the world has moved away from a “universal” security model towards a fragmented system, where regional groups prioritize their own rules over the mandates of the United Nations. o Deeming the United Nations slow and geographically distant, smaller but specific alliances take direct action to protect their spheres of influence, often bypassing the United Nations altogether. o Interventions by regional organizations such as the African Union (AU), NATO (NATO) and the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) without UNSC approval are examples of this. § Loss of credibility- "Lack of Accountability": Multilateralism is also eroding because the nature of conflicts has shifted towards "gray-zone" aggression, which includes the use of private military companies (PMCs) and cyber warfare, which deliberately operate outside the United Nations' traditional "war" definitions. § Since the United Nations Charter was primarily designed to regulate direct state-to-state military conflict, it does not provide a binding accountability framework for such "unacceptable or ambiguous" uses of force or non-kinetic attacks that can seriously affect the stability of a nation without firing a shot. § For example, the 2017 NotPetya cyber attack—believed to have originated in Ukraine—quickly spread globally and caused billions of dollars in damage, disrupting businesses, hospitals and government systems. o This incident shows that widespread damage can be caused through cyber campaigns, accountability can be blurred and traditional frameworks of international intervention can be seriously challenged. How can multilateralism be improved to promote a fair and equitable global order? 
§ Strengthening legal clarity and addressing gaps: International law must evolve to clearly distinguish between criminal law enforcement and the use of force, removing the ambiguity of "self-defense against non-state actors". o Clear codification can prevent powerful countries from redefining military aggression as anti-narcotics or counter-terrorism operations. o Treaty-based reforms may include the "harm threshold" principle, which will define what kind of cross-border intervention is justifiable. § Revitalizing the United Nations Security Council and reforming the veto: Reforming the procedures of the United Nations Security Council, such as introducing a "veto override" mechanism for mass atrocities or unilateral interventions, can restore credibility. o Rotational or weighted veto systems may limit the ability of P5 nations to shield themselves from accountability. o Increasing transparency in deliberations and mandating public accountability for the veto will impose a sense of responsibility on permanent members. o Structural reforms should be consistent with the principle of collective security, while protecting the expression of smaller countries. Strengthening decision-making processes ensures that the UN's legitimacy is not eliminated due to deadlock between superpowers. § Promoting compliance through financial and trade measures: Balanced economic disincentive measures can be adopted against nations that violate multilateral norms, including restricting access to international financial systems and trade forums. o The inclusion of incentives in the global economic structure aligns national interests with collective norms. o This approach leverages the interconnected global economy to enforce norms without relying solely on military control. Thus financial influence emerges as a viable compliance tool. § Codifying accountability for gray-zone and hybrid threats: Multilateral frameworks should explicitly address hybrid aggression, including cyber attacks, proxy conflicts and private military interventions. o The creation of a binding "Gray-Zone Accountability Protocol" will ensure that states cannot evade legal scrutiny through technical or non-kinetic warfare.  
o Independent verification agencies can monitor violations and recommend appropriate penalties. Such codification will bridge the gap between traditional law of war and modern realities and provide protection to vulnerable nations from subtle but destabilizing interventions. § Institutionalizing Transparency and Normative Monitoring in Global Governance: Multilateral institutions should adopt continuous monitoring of international military, economic and cyber activities through accessible and accessible reporting platforms. o Real-time data available on military activities, sanctions and cyber incidents increases accountability and has a deterrent effect on covert operations. o Independent audit assessments by neutral experts can provide legitimacy. Transparency institutionalizes peer pressure as an effective means of compliance and promotes trust between states. Conclusion: The Venezuela incident demonstrates how fragile multilateralism becomes when powerful states act unilaterally. Maintaining sovereignty, legal clarity and collective accountability is essential to prevent a chaotic global order. Strong institutions, transparency and enforceable norms can restore the credibility of international frameworks. India's commitment to multilateral principles underscores the need for principled diplomacy in a turbulent world. "Justice and rules are the real foundations of peace; Without these only might governs, and then no one is safe.”
