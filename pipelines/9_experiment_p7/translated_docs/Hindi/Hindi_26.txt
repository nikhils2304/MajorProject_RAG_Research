India’s Foreign Policy in 2025: From Diplomatic Promise to Structural Stress

Context (Introduction)

India's foreign policy in 2025 highlighted the growing gap between diplomatic ambition and global constraints, as economic pressures, great power uncertainty, and neighborhood instability undermined outcomes despite sustained high-level engagement.

Economic and Energy Security: Increasing External Sensitivities

*   Trade Pressures and Export Tensions: The imposition of 25% reciprocal tariffs on Indian goods by the US impacted labor-intensive sectors such as textiles, gems and jewelry, and seafood; Commerce Ministry data indicates these sectors employ over 45 million workers, increasing domestic job risks.
*   Stalled Mega Trade Deals: Despite commitments, bilateral trade agreements with the US and the European Union remained unsigned, reflecting a trend termed “new generation protectionism” by the WTO, driven by the domestic politics of developed economies.
*   Pressures on Remittances and Mobility: Restrictions on H-1B visas weakened remittance inflows, which, according to RBI data, contribute nearly 3% of India’s GDP and aid in current account stabilization.
*   Russian Oil Dependence: India imported over $50 billion worth of crude oil from Russia in 2025, helping control inflation but increasing the risk of secondary sanctions, as warned by the International Energy Agency.
*   Sanctions as Economic Tools: The US surcharge on Russian oil imports exemplifies the “weaponized interdependence” economists describe, where trade relationships are used for geopolitical leverage.
*   Limited Economic Diversification: FTAs with the UK, Oman, and New Zealand improved market access, but they represent less than 6% of India’s total trade, limiting their broader economic impact.

Great Power Relations: Alignment Without Assurance

*   Conditional US Partnership: While defense interoperability continued, India faced trade penalties, confirming RAND Corporation’s assessment that US partnerships are becoming increasingly transactional rather than strategic.
*   Diminished Strategic Salience: The 2025 US National Security Strategy mentioned India narrowly in an Indo-Pacific context, whereas the 2017 NSS described India as a “leading global power,” signaling a downgrading of priority.
*   China Engagement Without De-escalation: The restoration of flights and visas with China did not lead to troop withdrawals along the LAC; the Ministry of Defence confirms over 60,000 troops remain in forward deployments.
*   Sustained Security Costs: Ongoing border tensions increased India’s defense spending to nearly 2% of GDP, straining developmental expenditures, as flagged by the Fifteenth Finance Commission.
*   Russia’s Strategic Constraints: Despite strong image-building, limited outcomes from India-Russia summits reflect Moscow’s capacity constraints amidst a protracted conflict in Ukraine, as noted by SIPRI.
*   Pressure on Strategic Autonomy: The limits of India’s balancing act were exposed as competing powers demanded clearer alignment, shrinking traditional non-aligned space.

Global Strategic Order: Eroding Norms and Rising Uncertainty

*   Erosion of the Rules-Based Order: Global acceptance of ceasefire resolutions criticized by UN experts in Ukraine and Gaza signals a weakening commitment to sovereignty and accountability.
*   Multilateral Paralysis: Gridlock at the UN Security Council and the paralysis of the WTO dispute settlement system reduced faith in rules-based solutions, as highlighted in UN Secretary-General reports.
*   China’s Alternative Governance Efforts: China's Global Governance Initiative exemplifies its attempt to reshape norms, particularly in development finance and digital governance.
*   Shrinking Middle Power Space: Think tanks like Brookings note that polarization has reduced opportunities for bridge-building diplomacy for middle powers like India.
*   India’s Normative Ambiguity: India's calls for UN reform lack a clear blueprint for a post-Western global order.
*   Value-Interest Tensions: Balancing sovereignty, democracy, and strategic interests became more difficult in an increasingly transactional order.

Regional and Neighborhood Security Challenges

*   Sustained Terrorist Threats: The Pahalgam attack confirmed intelligence assessments that cross-border terrorist infrastructure remains despite earlier deterrent actions.
*   Limited Diplomatic Support: Reactions post-attack offered sympathy but lacked support, reflecting IISS findings that states avoid endorsing cross-border retaliation.
*   Political Upheaval in South Asia: Transitions in Bangladesh and Nepal reduced predictability; Ministry of External Affairs reports point to a slowdown in project implementation and diplomatic engagement.
*   Instability in Myanmar: Military-managed elections limited India’s democratic contacts, weakening its ‘Act East’ leverage.
*   External Players in South Asia: Increasing defense cooperation between Pakistan and third countries reduced India’s regional influence.
*   Inconsistent Neighborhood Focus: Experts argue India's episodic engagement contrasts with China's sustained economic influence.

Structural Weaknesses in India's Diplomacy

*   Over-Reliance on Image Building: High-visibility summits failed to translate into binding outcomes, fueling criticism of “demonstrative diplomacy.”
*   Weak Coalition Building: India struggles to mobilize collective responses, while smaller middle powers leverage issue-based coalitions.
*   Credibility Deficit: International scrutiny of domestic democratic practices undermined India's normative leverage, as noted in V-Dem and Freedom House reports.
*   Reactive Policy Orientation: Foreign policy responses often follow crises rather than set the agenda.
*   Underutilized Economic Diplomacy: Trade, technology standards, and development finance are not fully integrated into strategic diplomacy.
*   Communication Gaps: Ambiguity in official narratives during security crises affected credibility.

Way Forward: Expert-Guided Strategic Reorientation

*   Economic Diplomacy at the Core: NITI Aayog and OECD studies emphasize aligning trade, supply chains, and technology partnerships with foreign policy goals.
*   Diversified Trade Framework: WTO data indicates rising protectionism; experts advise deeper FTAs with middle powers in Africa, Latin America, and ASEAN.
*   Clear Strategic Signaling: Strategic analysts advocate defining redlines and thresholds to enhance deterrence.
*   Neighborhood First 2.0: ORF and IDSA suggest sustained infrastructure, energy, and connectivity investments to stabilize South Asia.
*   Coalition-Based Multilateralism: Brookings recommends issue-based coalitions on climate, digital public infrastructure, and development finance.
*   Normative Consistency: UNDP governance assessments underscore that external advocacy gains credibility only when aligned with domestic democratic practice.

Conclusion

India’s foreign policy challenges in 2025 stem less from diplomatic failure and more from structural shifts in global politics. Strategic clarity, economic diplomacy, and coalition building are necessary to transform engagement into lasting influence.

Doping Menace in Indian Sports: Institutional Gaps and Reform Imperatives

Context (Introduction)

Despite aspirations to host mega sporting events, India’s repeated ranking as a global leader in doping cases has exposed deep institutional, ethical, and governance deficits in its sports ecosystem, necessitating urgent systemic reform.

Current Scenario: Scale and Trends of Doping in India

*   High Global Ranking in Doping Cases: According to the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) 2024 Report, India recorded 260 Adverse Analytical Findings (AAFs) out of 7,113 tests (3.6%), ranking highest globally for the third consecutive year.
*   Inconsistent Positivity Rate: While India ranks only 7th globally in the number of tests, its positivity rate is significantly higher than sporting superpowers like China (24,214 tests, 91 AAFs), indicating systemic issues beyond testing numbers.
*   Sustained Post-COVID Trend: AAFs increased from 125 (2022) to 213 (2023), suggesting doping is not incidental but structurally embedded.
*   Recent Improvements, But Fragile: NADA claims a reduced positivity rate of 1.5% in 2025 (110 positives in 7,068 tests), though experts caution against over-interpretation without independent audits.
*   International Reputation Risk: Repeated scrutiny by the IOC threatens India’s credibility as a future Olympic host, especially ahead of the 2030 Commonwealth Games and 2036 Olympic bids.
*   Health and Career Outcomes: Medical literature cited by WADA links long-term doping to cardiac, hormonal, and psychological damage, making it a public health concern.

Structural and Ethical Drivers of Doping

*   Incentive-Driven Sports Ecosystem: The Ministry of Sports acknowledges that government jobs under sports quotas and assured pensions incentivize “win-at-all-costs” behavior, particularly among low-income athletes.
*   Monetization of Medals: High cash rewards for international medals, without equivalent ethical oversight, distort athlete motivation.
*   Accomplice-System Involvement: NADA reports confirm cases where coaches and support staff were suspended for aiding in doping, pointing to institutional malfeasance.
*   Lack of Awareness: Despite initiatives like the ‘Know Your Medicine’ app, WADA’s compliance reviews highlight inadequate baseline education on prohibited substances.
*   Testing Avoidance Culture: Instances of evading sample collection indicate weak deterrence and enforcement capacity.
*   Erosion of Ethics in Sports Culture: From a GS-4 perspective, doping reflects a compromise of core ethics – honesty, fairness, and respect for rules.

Institutional Limitations of NADA

*   Lack of Functional Independence: NADA operates under the Ministry of Sports, raising conflict-of-interest concerns, contrary to WADA’s emphasis on operational autonomy.
*   Financial Constraints: India’s anti-doping expenditure is modest compared to global best practices, limiting advanced testing and intelligence-based investigations.
*   Capacity Gaps: Shortages of trained doping control officers and forensic expertise reduce effectiveness against evolving synthetic substances.
*   Weak Deterrent Mechanisms: Delayed adjudications and limited lifetime bans reduce punitive impact.
*   Reactive, Not Preventative, Approach: Current efforts are testing-centric rather than ecosystem-wide prevention and behavior change.
*   Pressure of Compliance from IOC: Repeated comments from international bodies indicate incremental improvements will not suffice.

Reform Measures Undertaken

*   Legislative Strengthening: The National Anti-Doping (Amendment) Act, 2025, aims to align domestic law with WADA Code requirements.
*   Expanded Testing Regime: NADA has increased post-pandemic testing coverage, improving detection probability.
*   Awareness Campaigns: Targeted outreach programs for athletes and coaches have been enhanced.
*   Digital Interventions: Apps and online modules attempt to reduce inadvertent doping.
*   Staff Accountability: Action against complicit support staff signals a shift towards ecosystem responsibility.
*   Alignment with International Norms: Legal harmonization has reduced the risk of WADA compliance sanctions.

Way Forward:

*   Ensuring Institutional Independence: Sports law experts and WADA guidelines advise transforming NADA into a legally independent authority, free from executive control.
*   Enhanced and Predictable Funding: Comparative studies show countries with lower positivity rates invest significantly more in anti-doping R&D and intelligence.
*   Shift from Detection to Prevention: UNESCO’s sports integrity framework emphasizes embedding ethics education from the junior level.
*   Incentive Structure Reform: Experts suggest decoupling employment benefits from short-term medal outcomes and linking them to clean sports compliance.
*   Strengthening Adjudication and Penalties: Fast-track tribunals and stricter bans can enhance deterrence.
*   Technology and Intelligence Integration: AI-based profiling and international data-sharing, prevalent in Europe, can counter next-generation doping methods.

Conclusion

India’s doping crisis is not just a sports failure but an institutional and ethical failure. Without an independent, well-funded NADA and incentive-aligned reforms, India’s global sporting aspirations will remain undermined by a credibility deficit.
