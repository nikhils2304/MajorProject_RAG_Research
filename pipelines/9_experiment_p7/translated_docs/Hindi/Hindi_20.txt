Marked by increasing materialism and individualism, this article is based on the article titled ‘India, Germany to simplify defence trade’ published in The Hindu on 12/01/2026. This article analyzes the evolving strategic partnership between India and Germany, highlighting the growing cooperation in the fields of defense, technology, trade, and green transformation. It also analyzes the major differences and outlines measures to strengthen bilateral relations in a rapidly changing global landscape. India-Germany relations are witnessing strategically important growth due to shared democratic values and increasing cooperation in the economic, technical, and security sectors. Bilateral trade has exceeded US$50 billion, making Germany India's largest trading partner in the European Union. Recent high-level meetings have resulted in the signing of MoUs in the fields of defence, clean energy, skills, and innovation, as well as accelerating the India-European Union Free Trade Agreement. Thus, this partnership is evolving into a comprehensive strategic collaboration, not limited to trade alone. How have India-Germany relations changed over time? § Phase I: Establishment and Development Cooperation (1951–1970) o India and Germany established diplomatic relations in 1951. o In the post-war period, Germany emerged as an important development and technical partner for India, providing support in industrial training, infrastructure, and capacity building through institutions such as KfW. o These relations were mainly economic and developmental, with limited political or strategic significance. § Phase II: Cold War Obstacles (1970-1989): o Cold War alliances limited strategic relations, as India maintained close ties with the Soviet Union while Germany remained a member of NATO. o Despite this, cooperation continued in the areas of economic assistance, renewable energy, and technical education, but political and defence relations remained limited. § Phase III: Post-Cold War Reconstruction (1990-1999): o Germany's reunification and India's economic liberalization in 1991 proved to be a significant turning point. o There was a rapid expansion in trade, investment, and technology cooperation, especially in the manufacturing and engineering sectors. o Political engagement deepened as both countries adapted to a multipolar global order. § Phase IV: Strategic Partnership Phase (2000-2010): o In 2000, India and Germany formally declared a strategic partnership, which institutionalized political dialogue and expanded cooperation in science, technology, education, and multilateral forums. o In this phase, relations developed long-term strategic objectives. § Phase V: Institutional Strengthening through Inter-Governmental Consultations (IGC) (2011–2019): o The launch of Inter-Governmental Consultations (IGC) in 2011 elevated relations to the level of a high-trust partnership. o Cooperation has expanded in the areas of renewable energy, climate action, urban development, skill development, and higher education, and Germany has emerged as a key partner in India's sustainable development agenda. § Phase VI: Strategic Convergence (2020-Present): o There has been a shift in relations in Indo-Pacific convergence, adapted supply chains, defence industrial cooperation, green hydrogen, critical technologies, and skilled mobility. o Germany has committed to providing India with €10 billion by 2030 under the 2022 Green and Sustainable Development Partnership. o The celebration of 25 years of strategic partnership (2025) and 75 years of diplomatic relations (2026) reflects the maturity of the relationship. What are the main areas of cooperation between India and Germany? § Defence and Security Cooperation: Defence cooperation has emerged as a major pillar of India-Germany relations, including military cooperation, strategic dialogue, and defence industrial cooperation. o The Joint Declaration of Intent on Defence Industrial Cooperation Scheme indicates a shift from a buyer-seller model to a co-development model. o Germany's participation in Milan (Naval Exercise), Indian Ocean Naval Symposium and Tarang Shakti (Air Exercise), as well as cooperation in advanced defence technologies such as Eurodrone MALE UAV, demonstrates the Indo-Pacific convergence of India-Germany relations. o Initiatives such as reciprocal logistics support, peacekeeping training, and DRDO–BAANBW cooperation strengthen interoperability and strategic trust. § Counter-Terrorism Cooperation: India and Germany have unequivocally condemned all forms of terrorism and violent extremism, including cross-border terrorism. o Germany strongly condemned the recent terrorist attacks in Jammu and Kashmir and Delhi, reiterating its policy of zero tolerance towards terrorism. o Ratification of the Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty and progress under the Counter-Terrorism Joint Working Group promote legal and institutional cooperation. o Both sides are committed to strengthening cooperation against terrorist entities, including those listed under the United Nations 1267 Sanctions Committee. § Trade, Investment and Economic Cooperation: Economic cooperation is the foundation of India-Germany relations, with bilateral trade in goods and services exceeding US$50 billion in 2024, accounting for more than a quarter of India's trade with the European Union and this momentum is expected to continue in 2025. o Germany has established itself as a gateway to Europe and advanced technologies for Indian companies. o Support for the early conclusion of the India-EU Free Trade Agreement (FTA) and strengthening of the German-Indian CEO Forum demonstrates efforts to institutionalize private sector cooperation in the manufacturing, defence, infrastructure, pharmaceuticals, and energy sectors. § Technology, Innovation, Science, and Research: India-Germany relations have rapidly adopted an innovation-based approach, based on the Innovation and Technology Partnership Scheme covering semiconductors, digitization, health, bio-economy, and emerging technologies. o The Semiconductor Ecosystem Partnership and the Joint Declaration of Intent on Critical Minerals aim to strengthen cooperation in research and development, manufacturing, recycling, and supply chains in third countries. o The expansion of the Indo-German Science and Technology Centre (IGSTC) has strengthened industry-academia relations and inclusive innovation in areas such as artificial intelligence, advanced manufacturing, and sustainable production. • The proposed India-Germany Centre of Excellence and ISRO–DLR space cooperation further institutionalize long-term scientific and technical cooperation. § Green and Sustainable Development Partnership: The Green and Sustainable Development Partnership is a key pillar of India-Germany relations, with approximately €5 billion of Germany's €10 billion commitment (2022-2030) already spent or allocated by mid-2026. o This partnership supports climate change mitigation and adaptation, renewable energy, sustainable mobility, biodiversity, circular economy, and skill development, and is closely linked to Indian initiatives such as the PM e-Bus Service, the National Green Hydrogen Mission, Solar Rooftop Expansion, Metro Rail Projects, and Battery Storage. o The Joint Working Group on Solar Energy Production, Wind Energy, and Energy Storage strengthens cooperation on technologies, standards, and adapted supply chains. § Global Governance and Strategic Convergence: India and Germany reaffirmed their support for a free, open, and rule-based Indo-Pacific based on UNLCOS and international law and institutionalized strategic coordination through a new Indo-Pacific Consultation Mechanism, complemented by Germany's participation in the Indo-Pacific Ocean Initiative. o Shared support for the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC) reflects convergence on transparent, sustainable, and resilient connectivity. • On the issue of global governance, both countries reiterate their support for reforms of the United Nations Security Council under the G4 group, including expansion of permanent and non-permanent categories, and early text-based negotiations. o On global issues, India and Germany expressed concern over the war in Ukraine, supported efforts for a just peace under the UN Charter, welcomed progress on Gaza in line with UN Security Council resolutions, and reiterated their commitment to a two-state solution. § Education, Skill Development, Mobility, and People-to-People Relations: People-to-people relations remain a key pillar of India-Germany relations, reflected in the growing presence of Indian students and skilled professionals in Germany. o The India-German Comprehensive Plan on Higher Education strengthens institutional partnerships, joint degrees, and research collaboration, while India has invited German universities to establish campuses under the NEP structure. o The Migration and Mobility Partnership facilitates ethical skilled migration, especially in the healthcare sector, and addresses labour market needs through initiatives such as the India-German Centre of Excellence for Skill Development in Renewable Energy. What are the main areas of disagreement in India-Germany relations? § Strategic Autonomy vs. Alliances: India and Germany differ in their approach to security and strategic alignment. India adheres to the principle of strategic autonomy, seeks flexibility in its external relations, and opposes formal military alliances. o In contrast, Germany is deeply embedded in the security structures of NATO and the European Union, which shape its defence policies and threat perceptions. o For example, in the Russia-Ukraine conflict, Germany has taken a strong stance against Russia, providing military aid to Ukraine and supporting strict sanctions within the EU framework. o India refrained from explicitly condemning Russia, expressing concern over the suffering of civilians and supporting a peaceful solution. § Trade Protectionism: Germany generally aligns with the EU's preference for sanctions, regulatory conditions, and trade standards as foreign policy instruments. o However, India remains wary of extraterritorial trade restrictions, as it considers them a hindrance to global supply chains and development goals. o For example, EU regulatory mechanisms such as the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), data protection standards, and environmental conditions, which India sees as potential non-tariff barriers affecting its exports. o The EU's imports of India's steel and aluminium have decreased by 24.4%, from US$7.71 billion in fiscal year 2024 to US$5.82 billion in fiscal year 2025. • This decline highlights weak European demand and stricter trade standards, and the impact of this slowdown has also been felt on India-Germany bilateral trade. § Differences in Climate Action: India and Germany cooperate extensively on climate change, yet differences persist regarding pace, responsibility, and financing. o While Germany supports a rapid and legally binding phase-out of all fossil fuels to meet the 1.5-degree Celsius target, India believes that 'climate justice' requires a distinction between the luxury emissions of the West and the subsistence emissions of the developing world. o This difference manifests itself in intense negotiations over timelines, where New Delhi insists on a 'phased reduction' approach that allows for the continued use of coal to overcome the energy crisis before a complete transition. o Germany advocates for rapid emissions reductions and stringent climate standards, while India emphasizes climate justice, equity, and common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR). • India seeks guaranteed climate finance and technology transfer, while Germany often operates within the scope of broader EU financial and regulatory constraints. o Issues of Defence Exports and Technology Transfer: Although defence cooperation is expanding, Germany's stringent export control regime and wary approach to sensitive technologies sometimes slow progress. • India seeks expedited approvals, comprehensive technology transfer, and local manufacturing rights, while Germany balances commercial interests with domestic political oversight and alliance considerations. This sometimes leads to conflicts in the timeline and scope of defence industry cooperation. § Indo-Pacific and China's Perspective: India views China primarily from the perspective of regional integrity, border security, and regional power balance, while Germany's China policy has historically been more influenced by economic interdependence, although this is now changing. o Although both countries support a free and open Indo-Pacific, Germany's participation is still evolving from an economic perspective to a strategic perspective, leading to differences in the perception of urgency and threat. § Commercial Consulate and Legal Disputes (Ariha Shah Case): A particular and persistent problem in bilateral relations is the legal dispute over the upbringing of Ariha Shah, who was taken into foster care in Germany in 2021. o The case has become a symbol of cultural and legal divergence, with the Indian government and public considering it a violation of cultural and linguistic rights. o India is demanding the return of the girl to an Indian foster home to protect her identity, while Germany says its judicial process is independent and focused entirely on child welfare law. What measures are needed to strengthen India-Germany relations? § Institutionalizing Strategic Dialogues: India and Germany should further institutionalize strategic dialogue beyond ad hoc high-level visits. o The recently announced Indo-Pacific Consultation Mechanism and Track 1.5 Foreign Policy and Security Dialogue should be regular, results-oriented, and linked to defence planning, maritime security, and cyber security cooperation. § Advancing Defence Industrial Cooperation: An important measure is the effective operation of the Defence Industrial Cooperation Roadmap. o Both sides should expedite co-development and co-production projects to move purposefully towards implementation, enabling deeper technology transfer and joint intellectual property creation. o Germany can greatly benefit from India's skilled workforce and cost competitiveness, while India can benefit from German expertise in precision engineering, advanced materials, and defence electronics. § Early Implementation of India-EU Free Trade Agreement (FTA): India and Germany should strive to complete and implement the India-EU Free Trade Agreement expeditiously and in a balanced manner. This will reduce tariff and non-tariff barriers, improve market access, and provide regulatory certainty to businesses. o Both countries should expand cooperation in small and medium enterprises, start-ups, and innovation ecosystems through joint incubators, easy access to finance, and digital platforms. § Better Coordination on Climate Action: India and Germany should deepen climate cooperation by ensuring that ambitious climate goals are met with adequate finance, technology transfer, and capacity building. o Expanding concessional financing under the GSDP, increasing joint initiatives in green hydrogen, battery storage, and urban mobility, and co-developing climate-resilient technologies can help bridge the development gap. § Strengthening Cooperation in Advanced and Critical Technologies: India and Germany must intensify cooperation in semiconductors, artificial intelligence (AI), quantum technologies, critical minerals, space, and biotechnology to future-proof their partnership. o The establishment of joint research centres, shared standards, and coordinated industrial strategies will reduce dependence on focused global supply chains. o Facilitating the smooth movement of researchers, engineers, and innovators, as well as protecting intellectual property, will further strengthen trust and innovation-based development. § Promoting People-to-People Relations and Skilled Mobility: People-to-people engagement remains an important long-term stabilizing factor in bilateral relations. o Ethical and mutually beneficial skilled migration frameworks (especially in the healthcare, renewable energy, and advanced manufacturing sectors) should be expanded under the Migration and Mobility Partnership Agreement. § Managing Differences through Dialogue and Reciprocal Sensitivity: Regular diplomatic dialogue can help resolve differences on issues such as Russia-Ukraine, human rights discussions, sanctions, and trade rules such as CBAM. o Reciprocal sensitivity to each other's strategic compulsions, domestic constraints, and development priorities will eliminate differences and maintain trust. § Expanding Global Governance and Cooperation in the Global South: India and Germany should increase coordination in multilateral forums on UN reforms, climate finance, health security, and digital governance. o Promoting triangular development cooperation in Africa, Asia, and Latin America will link India's development experience with Germany's financial and technical capabilities. Conclusion: India-Germany relations are moving towards a three-dimensional partnership in defence, digitalization, and carbon emission reduction, based on shared democratic values and strategic trust. By combining India's scale, skills, and development momentum with Germany's technology, capital, and industrial strength, this partnership can be future-ready. Constructive dialogue to address differences will strengthen cooperation amidst global uncertainty. India and Germany together can emerge as key architects of a secure, sustainable, and rule-based global order.
