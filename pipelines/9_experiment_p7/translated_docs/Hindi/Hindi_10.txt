This article, adapted to the Indian Himalayan region, is based on the article titled “A dangerous march towards a Himalayan ecocide” published in The Hindu on 23/01/2026. The article highlights how unsafe land use, uncontrolled expansion of infrastructure, and ecological neglect are transforming the climate crisis in the Himalayan region into frequently occurring disasters. The article emphasizes the need to prioritize disaster-resilient capacity and science-based planning in place of disaster-prone projects in India's most sensitive and important ecosystems. The Himalayas, one of the world's most climate-sensitive mountain ranges, is warming at a rate nearly 50% faster than the global average, turning rare disasters into frequent occurrences. In 2025 alone, climate-related extreme events killed more than 4,000 people in India, with Himalayan states accounting for the largest share. What appears today as a natural disaster is rapidly transforming into a man-made risk, driven by unsafe land use and excessive infrastructure expansion in sensitive geo-environments. Thus, the Himalayan crisis is no longer just an environmental challenge, but a decisive test of India's development vision and disaster management capacity. What is the importance of the Himalayan ecosystem for India? § Water Security as the Water Tower of Asia: The Himalayas act as an important 'Third Pole', storing vast reserves of fresh water that nourish perennial river systems (Indus, Ganges, Brahmaputra), supporting India's agricultural heartland. o This cryospheric reservoir releases meltwater during the dry pre-monsoon months, acting as a crucial buffer against drought for the 'food bowl' of the northern plains. o For example, the Hindu Kush Himalayas support approximately 240 million mountain dwellers and approximately 1.65-1.9 billion downstream beneficiaries, including India. § Climate-Related Temperature Controller: This mountain range acts as a vast climatic barrier, preventing icy Central Asian katabatic winds from turning northern India into a cold desert, while simultaneously trapping moisture-laden south-west monsoon winds. o This 'mountain uplift' is the primary driver of rainfall in the subcontinent, regulating the necessary wet-dry cycles for the Kharif crop cycle. o This mountain range ensures that the Gangetic plains receive more than 1000 mm of rainfall annually.

§ Strategic 'Geopolitical Bulwark' and Border Defense: The Himalayas provide a natural high-altitude fortress along the Line of Actual Control (LAC). o This region has now become the center of India's 'dual-use infrastructure' strategy, which works to prevent displacement by balancing military mobility and civilian connectivity in border areas. o For example, the Sela Tunnel, commissioned in 2024, ensures all-weather connectivity to Tawang. Also, under the Vibrant Villages Program, ₹4,800 crore has been allocated for the development of 663 border villages to protect this 'living bulwark'. • This move is also India's strategic response to the 628 'Xiaokang' defense villages built by China near the LAC. § Center of Green Energy: The Himalayas are at the heart of India's renewable energy transition. o Its steep altitudinal gradients generate high kinetic energy that is ideal for run-of-river projects and can stabilize grid uncertainty arising from solar and wind energy. o The estimated hydropower potential in this region is 115,550 MW, of which approximately 46,850 MW (≈40%) is already installed, making the region the country's largest hydropower reservoir. § Biodiversity Reserve and Medicinal Wealth: As one of the four global 'biodiversity hotspots' located in India, the Himalayas serve as a genetic reserve for endangered flora and potential medicinal discoveries (AYUSH). o This biological richness provides environmental services such as pollination and soil stabilization. • For example, out of approximately 10,000 species of vascular plants in the Himalayan biodiversity hotspot, approximately 3,160 species (representing 71 genera) are endemic, i.e., not found anywhere else on Earth. § Cryospheric Sentinel for Global Warming: The health of the Himalayan glaciers acts as the most visible 'climate dashboard' for India, providing early warning of global tipping points that affect sea level rise and coastal security thousands of kilometers away. o The melting rate of glaciers here is directly related to the future risk of inundation of coastal metropolises such as Mumbai and Kolkata due to thermal expansion and meltwater.

§ Socio-Economic Corridor and Sustainable Tourism: This region supports a unique 'high-altitude economy' driven by spiritual tourism and horticulture. o Sustainable management is extremely important here to prevent 'demographic migration', in which local people migrate due to disaster risks, leaving strategic border areas deserted. o The Char Dham Project is a highway project under construction to provide all-weather accessible connectivity to four pilgrimage sites namely Kedarnath, Badrinath, Yamunotri and Gangotri. What measures has India taken for sustainable development in the Indian Himalayan region? § Shift to Environment-Friendly Mobility - 'Parvat Mala' (National Ropeway Program): The government is rapidly using aerial ropeways instead of terrain-damaging road widening to reduce slope instability and geodynamic triggering. o It aims to construct 1,200 km of ropeways through more than 250 projects (Budget 2024-25). • For example, the Sonprayag-Kedarnath ropeway has reduced travel time from 8 hours to 36 minutes, reducing human pressure on trekking routes. o This 'zero footprint mobility' transport model completely bypasses highly vulnerable terrain and greatly reduces landslide risk compared to traditional four-lane highways. § Community-Led Conservation 'SECURE Himalayas' Project: Moving beyond fencing forests, this initiative integrates snow leopard conservation with sustainable livelihoods to reduce human-wildlife conflict. o It includes key landscapes (e.g., Gangotri-Govind, Kanchenjunga) and has trained over 1,000 local youth as nature guides and monitors. o Recognizing local communities as the 'first line of defense', it monetizes conservation through ecotourism and medicinal plant value chains, ensuring that protecting biodiversity becomes economically viable for residents living at high altitudes.

§ Judicial 'Carrying Capacity' Mandate and Legal Rights: Governance has seen a historic shift with the Supreme Court legally recognizing the 'right to be free from adverse climate impacts' as a fundamental right (M.K. Ranjit Singh v. Union of India case). o Due to this, the government has had to stop the 'unlimited development' model. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change has proposed measures to accurately assess the carrying capacity of 13 Himalayan states. • In Ashok Kumar Raghav v. Union of India case, the Supreme Court called for the advancement of 'carrying capacity'-based action plans to curb uncontrolled development in the Indian Himalayan Region (IHR). § Decentralized Energy Transition - 'PM Surya Ghar' in the Hills: To reduce dependence on grid transmission lines (which require deforestation) and diesel generators in remote areas, the government is promoting decentralized rooftop solar microgrids. o The government has issued guidelines providing for an additional subsidy of 10% per kilowatt for 'special category states' including North Eastern states and hilly regions such as Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh. o This strengthens energy security during disasters and reduces diesel-generated 'black carbon' emissions, which accelerate glacial melt. § Hydrogeological Rejuvenation - 'Springshed Management' Framework: Recognizing that in the background of glacier melt, the NITI Aayog has focused on reservoir recharge rather than dam-based solutions, as 'springs' are the immediate lifeline in mountainous areas. o This 'spring development' approach maps underground aquifers to revive drying springs, ensuring water security for hill agriculture without disrupting natural river flows. o According to a 2018 report by NITI Aayog, approximately 50% of springs in the International Human Resource (IHR) are either drying up or have dried up. • For example, this project has successfully revived more than 50 lakes and dozens of springs in Sikkim, establishing Sikkim as a national model. § 'Security through Stability' - Vibrant Village Program (VVP): Moving beyond traditional military strengthening, this program views border security as an ecological and demographic challenge. o This strategy aims to prevent 'immigration' from sensitive border areas, for which 'climate-smart' livelihoods such as eco-tourism and organic clusters are being promoted. o This keeps border villages populated and plays a strategic 'eye and ear' role without heavy and environmentally harmful military infrastructure. • For example, Kibithu (Arunachal) has been developed as a 'smart village' pilot project. § Circular Economy Initiative - 'Deposit Refund Scheme' (DRS) and EPR: To address the plastic crisis contaminating Himalayan water sources, states like Himachal Pradesh are testing 'buy-back' models. o This policy obligates producers to pay for waste collection and incentivizes locals to collect non-biodegradable packaging, effectively placing a 'price tag' on waste to create a self-reliant waste collection economy in remote areas. o The Himachal Pradesh Cabinet has launched the 'Deposit Refund Scheme 2025' to deal with waste management, under which cash refunds will be provided on returned plastic waste. § Technology-Integrated Adaptation - 'Glacial Lake Early Warning System' (GLEWS): In view of the increasing threat of Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs), the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) has adopted automated monitoring, moving away from reactive relief. o Under this, over-the-horizon radar and satellite-linked sensors are being installed on high-risk glacial lakes, enabling timely warning to downstream areas. o Under the National Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF) Risk Reduction Program, early warning systems have been operationalized at South Lhonak Lake and Shako Cho (Sikkim), integrating remote sensing and community warning mechanisms. § Scientific Capacity and 'Knowledge-Policy' Bridge (NMSHE Phase-II): The National Mission for Sustaining the Himalayan Ecosystem has shifted from passive observation to active 'evidence-based policymaking'. o This institutional mechanism now obligates Himalayan states to align their State Action Plans on Climate Change (SAPCC) with rigorous district-level vulnerability maps, replacing ad hoc decisions with long-term 'climate-risk-informed' governance.

o Under Phase-II (2021-2026), NMSHE activated key thematic task forces (e.g., Himalayan agriculture, forest resources, and plant diversity, etc.) and IIT Roorkee's new Center of Excellence now provides disaster risk data directly to state disaster management authorities in real time. What are the challenges related to the Indian Himalayan region? § Cryosphere Collapse and Climate Instability: The cryosphere on the 'Third Pole' is witnessing an irreversible decline, where rising temperatures are not only melting glaciers but also changing precipitation phases (snow to rain), destabilizing water security for agriculture and energy sources in downstream areas. o This change creates a "heavy-drop drought" paradox, where intense local floods are followed by prolonged water crises, threatening the status of India's "water tower". o For example, the Hindu Kush Himalayas recorded a 65% faster decline in snow mass (according to ICIMOD 2024). • In addition, the South Lhonak GLOF (Sikkim, October 2023) released 50 cubic million water, destroying the Teesta III dam. § Infrastructure-Induced Slope Instability ('Subsidence' Syndrome): Rapid urbanization and strategic mega-projects (highways, tunnels) ignore shear zones and tectonic sensitivity, leading to increased land subsidence and slope failures. o Heavy engineering approaches (blasting and vertical cutting) without adequate debris disposal or drainage planning have activated dormant landslides, turning livable cities into disaster zones. • For example, an analysis of Joshimath's subsidence shows that between December 2022 and December 2024, parts of Joshimath subsided by more than 30 centimeters. § Exceeding Carrying Capacity and Pressure of Overtourism: Unregulated religious and adventure tourism has violated ecological carrying capacity, creating a severe imbalance of resource wastage in high-altitude areas.

§ The 'pump-and-dump' tourism model exploits local water sources for luxury consumption, while leaving behind non-biodegradable waste that leaks microplastics into water sources, polluting the ecosystem at the source itself. o For example, the physical carrying capacity of Kedarnath is approximately 24,500 visitors per day, but its actual carrying capacity is 9,833. o According to estimates by NITI Aayog and the World Bank, 5-8 million metric tons of waste is generated annually in the Indian Himalayan region. Furthermore, most of this waste is non-recyclable. § Black Carbon and Albedo Feedback Loop: Regional pollution from biomass burning and vehicle emissions deposits black carbon on glaciers, causing the surface to darken and reducing albedo (reflectivity). o This accelerates the melting process of snow even at sub-zero temperatures, creating a dangerous feedback cycle where 'polluted snow' absorbs more heat, causing the local temperature increase rate to be much higher than the global average. o A new study by Climate Trends (titled 'Impact of Black Carbon on Himalayan Glaciers') shows that the increase in black carbon emissions has accelerated the melting process in the Himalayas by 4 degrees Celsius in the last two decades, with high concentrations seen in the eastern and central Himalayas. § Homogenization of Biodiversity and Fragmentation of Habitat: Linear infrastructure (railways, transmission lines) is cutting off wildlife corridors, forcing animals towards human habitations and increasing genetic isolation in species such as the snow leopard. o At the same time, global warming allows invasive species to climb to higher altitudes, competing with native alpine vegetation, homogenizing the region's biodiversity. o A 2024 study by the Indian Institute of Remote Sensing (IIRS) recorded an 11% decline in natural forest area between 1991 and 2023 in rapidly urbanizing western Himalayan regions, mainly due to a 184% increase in urbanization and infrastructure development. • Furthermore, the number of large forest areas across India has decreased by 71.5% due to projects such as railways and power lines, increasing the 'edge to interior' ratio and habitat fragmentation especially in Himalayan regions. § Institutional Fragmentation and Policy Weakness: Himalayan governance is still siloed, with roads, hydropower, and tourism handled by separate ministries. There is a lack of an integrated, Himalayan-specific building code or integrated ecological monitoring. o The weakening of the importance of Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) for 'strategic' projects has weakened legal safeguards, enabling ecologically irreversible interventions.

o Under the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023, projects within 100 km of the LAC/International Border have been exempted from forest clearance, allowing strategic projects in sensitive terrains such as Changthang (Ladakh) to avoid full EIA (Environmental Impact Assessment). § 'Seismic Gap' and Reservoir-Induced Risk: The central Himalayas lie over an active seismic gap, where accumulated tectonic pressure is constantly exacerbated by the water load of massive dams, increasing the risk of reservoir-induced seismicity. o Rigid concrete structures without adequate seismic protection can turn future earthquakes into cascading failures. o NGRI (2025) has marked this region as a potential zone for an earthquake of approximately 8.5 magnitude, while over 1,500 micro-tremors near the Tehri Dam indicate increasing pressure on local fault lines. § 'Winter Fire' and Carbon Sink: Traditional summer fires are now erupting in winters due to 'snow deficits' and dry biomass accumulation, turning the Himalayan carbon sink into an emitter. This affects dormant seed reserves and pine trees, increases invasive expansion in oak forests, causes soil desiccation and destroys the natural water-absorbing capacity. o Unusual dry winters and low rainfall/snowfall in Uttarakhand and the central Himalayas are causing crop losses, water stress and negative impacts on glacier mass balance. o Evidence of the severity of this climate anomaly is also seen in forest fire incidents in high-altitude protected areas such as the Nanda Devi National Park. o Evidence of this climate anomaly is found in increased forest fire incidents in high-altitude areas in the Nanda Devi National Park. There were 440 forest fire incidents in the Uttarakhand Himalayas between January 14–20, 2026, compared to 45 fire incidents in the same period in 2025, an increase of almost tenfold. What measures are necessary for the sustainable management of the Indian Himalayan region? § Enforcing Himalayan-Specific Land Use and Building Codes: India needs a legally binding Himalayan Mountain Code that replaces common plains norms with prawn-sensitive, geology-aligned standards. o This code should regulate road width, hill cutting angles, tunnel density, building height, and permissible loads in seismic and landslide zones. Development permits should be granted based on micro-zoning and cumulative impact thresholds. • Such a code would turn 'caution' into enforceable law instead of advice. § Transitioning from Infrastructure-Based Development to Adaptation-Based Planning: The development strategy must shift from asset creation to risk reduction, making disaster adaptation the primary design parameter. o Infrastructure should be assessed not on the basis of speed or capacity, but on the basis of life-cycle stability, maintenance, and consequences of failure. o This requires involving geologists, ecologists, and disaster scientists in the project's design phase itself, rather than in post-approval remediation efforts. Planning that prioritizes adaptation reduces long-term financial and human costs. § Institutionalizing Cumulative Impact and Basin-Level Assessments: Project-wise approvals should be replaced by landscape and river basin level planning, especially for hydropower, road and tourism clusters. o Basin authorities should limit infrastructure density based on slope stability, sediment load, and carrying capacity. o This can prevent the effect of 'death by a thousand small cuts', where individually approved projects collectively destabilize entire valleys; integration across different sectors is the only way to prevent systemic risk. § Treating Natural Solutions as Main Infrastructure: Forests, wetlands, alpine meadows, and riparian areas should be considered critical natural infrastructure rather than ancillary conservation assets. o Slope stabilization through afforestation, flood control through wetlands, and avalanche control through forest belts are often cheaper and more sustainable than engineering solutions. o Budget allocations should be clearly earmarked for ecosystem restoration as a disaster risk reduction strategy. This makes ecological improvement consistent with economic prudence. § Regulating Tourism According to Carrying Capacity and Spatial Zoning: The tourism policy should move from a volume-based model to a capacity-regulated and seasonally staggered model.

o There should be strict limits on pedestrians, vehicles, and construction in sensitive areas, enforced through permits and pricing signals. o Tourism infrastructure should be decentralized to reduce pressure and stabilize local livelihoods. Well-planned tourism protects both the ecosystem and the economic base that depends on it. § Restructuring Hydropower and Transportation with Context-Sensitive Design: Hydropower and road projects must adopt terrain-adaptive design, narrow roads, flexible alignment, minimal tunneling, and distributed energy systems over massive structures. o Geological uncertainty should be considered as a design constraint, not an inconvenience. o Preventing instability-generating designs is more important than remediating unstable projects beforehand. This reduces the likelihood of irreversible risk becoming permanent. § Empowering Local Institutions and Community Participation: Local communities must move from being project-affected stakeholders to co-managers of landscapes. Assigning planning authority, disaster funds, and ecological monitoring roles to Panchayats improves compliance and early warning. o Indigenous knowledge on slope behavior, water flow, and forest management should be the basis for formal decision-making. Decentralized management enhances legitimacy and grassroots effectiveness. § Integrating Climate, Disaster, and Development Governance: Climate adaptation, disaster risk reduction, and infrastructure planning for the Himalayan region should be integrated into a single governance framework. Divided jurisdictions weaken accountability and promote policy omissions. o Integrated planning enables long-term perspective setting, benefit-loss assessment, and transparent prioritization. Protecting the Himalayas requires operating it as an integrated geo-system, not as separate administrative zones. Conclusion: Protecting the Himalayas is no longer a matter of choosing between development and conservation, but a decisive choice between foresight and failure. When sensitive mountain regions are burdened with plains-like ambitions, disasters become policy outcomes, not natural events. Only adaptive and eco-centric governance can pave the way for protecting lives, livelihoods, and national security. Stability in the Himalayas is no longer an ideal, but the last line of defense.
