Urban Governance Reforms for Developed India 2047 This article is based on the article titled "Missing municipal governance" published in The Financial Express on 18/01/2026, which limits the role of elected mayors and concentrates administrative power in the hands of a relatively unaccountable bureaucracy. This structural disconnect has resulted in the creation of cities with unequal and inadequate services, raising questions about India's vision of becoming a developed nation by 2047. India's urban centers are still mired in the governance system of the colonial era, where budgets larger than those of states are controlled by bureaucrats, and the role of elected mayors often remains formal. The ill effects of this are clearly visible in metropolitan cities like Mumbai, where millions of people are living in war-zone-like conditions, lacking basic amenities like housing, clean water, and electricity. This situation is reflected in almost all major cities of India in the form of administrative failures. Despite the vision of becoming a 'developed nation' by 2047, the fundamental incongruity between administrative structure and accountability has deprived urban India of effective city governance. What is the current urban governance infrastructure in India? § Constitutional basis - 74th Amendment Act (1992) o This Act granted constitutional status to Urban Local Bodies (ULBs), transforming India's governance system from a two-tier (center-state) to a three-tier structure. Under this, Part IX-A and the Twelfth Schedule were added to the Constitution. § Three-tier structure: o Nagar Panchayat: For areas transitioning from rural to urban. o Nagar Parishad: For small urban areas. o Municipal Corporation: For large urban areas. § Devolution of Powers (12th Schedule): o It identifies 18 functional subjects for urban local bodies, including urban planning, land use regulation, water supply, public health, and slum improvement etc. § Mandatory institutional arrangements: o State Election Commission: To conduct regular municipal elections. o State Finance Commission (SFC): To recommend the distribution of taxes between the state and local local bodies. o District and Metropolitan Planning Committees (DPC/MPC): To prepare a comprehensive development plan by consolidating the plans of rural (panchayats) and urban bodies. § Institutional structure: o Despite constitutional provisions, in practice, administrative power is often shared or contested between elected representatives and bureaucratic appointments. § Executive structure: o Commissioner System: In most municipal corporations, executive power is vested in the Municipal Commissioner appointed by the state, while the Mayor often remains a formal head with limited powers. o Mayor-in-Council System: This has been experimented with in some states (such as West Bengal), in which the Mayor has executive powers similar to the Chief Minister. § Para-Statal Institutions: A key feature of Indian urban governance is the presence of state-controlled 'Para-Statal' institutions (such as development authorities like DDA/BDA, Water Supply Boards). o These institutions often take over key functions like planning, water supply, and transport from the Urban Local Bodies (ULB), as a result of which clear accountability cannot be established. § Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV): Widely formed under the Smart City Mission, these corporate bodies are established to implement specific projects. Its objective is to ensure rapid and centralized execution of projects while partially limiting traditional municipal processes. § Program Structure (Main Missions) o The Central Government primarily operates urban reforms through "Centrally Sponsored Schemes," which ensure financial provisions linked to specific governance reforms. o AMRUT 2.0 (Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation): Focuses on water security. Its objective is to provide universal coverage of water supply and to make cities "water secure" through a circular economy of water. o Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban (SBM-U) 2.0: Focuses on "garbage free cities," which includes solid waste management, remediation of waste sites, and used water management. o Smart City Mission: This mission has almost completed its financial assistance period (March 2025). It institutionalized the Integrated Command and Control Center (ICCC) for data-driven city management and implemented the SPV model described above. o Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana-Urban (PMAY-U): Its objective is to provide permanent houses suitable for living in all seasons to all eligible beneficiaries, often using a beneficiary-led construction model. § Latest Financial Developments o Fifteenth Finance Commission: Now grants given to urban local bodies are often linked to executed reforms. o Urban Infrastructure Development Fund (UIDF): Managed by the National Housing Bank, this fund has been created for the construction of urban infrastructure in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities, which complements the resources of smaller urban local bodies. § Emerging Reforms in Urban Governance o Greater Bengaluru Authority: This effort is to integrate various service provider institutions so that limited resources can be utilized to the maximum. o WATCO (Odisha Water Corporation): A state-owned, non-profit company that serves as a prime example of a specialized, modern utility provider for urban water supply and sewerage. o National Urban Policy Framework (NUPF): This provides a strategic, holistic guide to states for managing rapid urbanization. What are the key issues related to urban governance in India? § Fiscal Weakness and Structural Dependency: The Constitution of India specifies the taxes to be divided between the Central and State Governments, but it does not specify the revenue base for local bodies. o Due to lack of financial autonomy, Indian municipalities have become functionally inactive and are excessively dependent on discretionary grants instead of "own revenue" sources like property tax. o This financial inadequacy hinders long-term infrastructure investment, as a result of which municipal bodies have to depend on the central or state governments even for basic operational expenses, which weakens the original spirit of decentralization enshrined in the 74th Constitutional Amendment. o For more than the last decade, the revenue and expenditure of municipalities in India has remained stable at around 1% of the GDP. § Para-Statal Trap and Institutional Fragmentation: The urban governance system has become slack due to the dominance of numerous parallel institutions (such as SPVs and development authorities), which effectively push the elected urban local bodies (ULBs) to the margins. o State-controlled para-statal bodies take over highly important and revenue-generating functions like land use planning and transportation, while urban local bodies are assigned relatively low-revenue functions (such as waste management and slum services). This creates a situation of "powerless accountability", where accountability becomes unclear due to institutional gaps. o The fatal accident in Noida in January 2026, in which a technician's car filled with water and fell into an unguarded pit, directly exposed the structural failure of this governance system. o Despite warnings being given since 2015 and joint inspections being conducted in October 2023, institutional apathy prevented the hazard from being addressed. o For example, a CAG audit (2024) studying 18 states found that only 4 out of the 18 functions listed in the 12th Schedule are actually autonomous. § Formal Mayors and Bureaucratic Domination: The role of the Mayor in India often remains symbolic, while the actual executive powers are concentrated in the hands of the Municipal Commissioner appointed by the state. o This structural imbalance hampers the chain of democratic accountability because the mayor accountable to the public cannot direct or punish the officer who implements administrative decisions. As a result, the effectiveness of the municipal council is reduced. o According to the Praja's Urban Governance Index (2024), only in Kerala do Mayors have the power to write the Annual Confidential Report (ACR) of Municipal Commissioners. § Planning Narrowness and Climate-Blindness: Urban planning in India is rigid, static, and detached from water-geographical realities, where land is viewed as merely "real estate" rather than an ecosystem. o Master plans are often decades old or do not exist, leading to "concrete-centric" development that blocks natural drains, turning even moderate rainfall into devastating urban floods (pluvial flooding). • For example, a NITI Aayog report states that 65% of India's urban settlements do not have a master plan and only 1 planner is available for every 1,00,000 population in the country. • Bangalore's "Brand Bangalore" struggle highlights how encroachment on rajakaluvas (stormwater drainage canals) causes frequent flooding in the tech corridor. § Staff Paralysis and Lack of Capacity: Urban local bodies lack specialized technical staff such as hydro-geologists or urban designers. o Recruitment powers are centralized at the state level, causing extreme delays in recruitments and forcing municipalities to rely on short-term consultants or unskilled contract labor for critical technical functions. o According to the Praja Index 2024, the rate of vacancies against sanctioned posts in municipal corporations is highly worrying. More than 40% posts are vacant in these 9 cities - Ahmedabad, Gurugram, Shimla, Bhopal, Imphal, Aizawl, Amritsar, Greater Jaipur and Kolkata. Patna has the highest proportion of vacant posts (89%) at the municipal administration level. o The implementation of AMRUT 2.0 is being hampered in many cities because urban local bodies do not have the necessary technical experts to design "circular water economy" projects. § Participatory Democracy Limited to Paper: Although the 74th Constitutional Amendment provides for "Ward Committees" to decentralize power to citizens, in most states they are either non-existent or merely "paper institutions." o Without these committees, governance remains a top-down process where citizens cannot hold local councilors accountable for hyper-local issues like streetlights or drainage, defeating the very purpose of self-governance. o According to Janagraha's Report 2023, only 5 out of 35 States/Union Territories have enacted and notified rules for the Community Participation Law, which mandates functional ward committees. • The Government of India had prepared the Model Municipal Law (2003) to guide the states in reforming municipal laws, but its adoption and effective implementation has largely been inadequate. § Imbalance of "Processing vs. Collection" in Waste Management: The Swachh Bharat Mission has successfully promoted "collection," but cities are completely failing in "processing" and disposal of "legacy waste." o For example, approximately 77% of the waste generated in Indian cities is dumped in open landfill sites without treatment. o In fact, we are moving waste from door to door to massive, fire-prone landfill sites because urban local bodies lack the necessary technology and financial resources for scientific segregation and bio-remediation. This is creating environmental "time bombs." What measures can India adopt to strengthen urban governance? § Institutional Measures: Implementation of "Mayor-in-Council" System: o To address the problem of fragmented accountability between elected representatives and appointed bureaucrats, states should adopt the "Mayor-in-Council" system. o In this arrangement, the Mayor gets the role of executive head similar to the Chief Minister, which restores the democratic feedback mechanism and makes the administration directly accountable to the public's mandate. o This eliminates the tendency of "ceremonial figurehead" and ensures that decision-making is driven by political will and public aspirations instead of bureaucratic inertia. § Financial Measures: Institutionalizing "Value-Creation Financing (VCF): o Moving beyond reliance on state grants, cities should actively adopt instruments such as value-creation financing to partially benefit from the value added by public infrastructure investments. o When the construction of a new metro route or highway increases the value of nearby land, urban local bodies should collect a portion of this unearned increment through "improvement fees" or higher FSI fees. o This creates a self-reliant "virtuous cycle" in which infrastructure raises resources for itself, reduces financial dependence on the center, and ensures that the incidental benefits received by the private sector are used in the public interest. § Planning: Making "GIS-Based Dynamic Master Plans" Mandatory: o Instead of the system of static and long-term (20-year) master plans, dynamic, GIS-based local area plans should be adopted, which can be updated in real time. o Integrating satellite data with information received from the local level, one can continuously and actively monitor changes in city land use, encroachment, and green cover, which will reduce dependence on old and irrelevant maps. o This will promote "Agile Planning," in which zoning regulations can be quickly adapted to economic needs or climate risks, and effective control over the chaotic and illegal urban expansion currently seen in urban peripheral areas will be possible. § Governance: Empowering "Regional Assemblies" for Hyper-Local Democracy: o For the actual implementation of the 74th Constitutional Amendment, states will have to go beyond Ward Committees and transfer legal powers to Regional Assemblies. o With participatory budgeting being legally mandated, these assemblies will be able to vote on specific allocations for services like local parks, streetlights, and drainage, which will make the citizen an active participant instead of a passive voter. o This hyper-local monitoring creates an effective social audit mechanism, ensuring that municipal funds are spent on actual grassroots needs rather than ostentatious projects. § Stability: Adopting a "Water-Absorbing City" Structure: o Urban administrations should assimilate climate adaptation by legally mandating water-absorbing city principles in all building codes and public works. o This includes enforcing "zero-runoff" rules for large premises, revitalizing urban wetlands, and using permeable materials for footpaths to absorb floodwater. o The transition from "concrete-centric" engineering to "nature-based solutions" transforms the city itself into a water-harvesting infrastructure, enabling a concurrent solution to the dual problem of urban flooding and water scarcity. § Regional: Activating “Metropolitan Planning Committees” (MPC): o To control the chaotic urban sprawl that extends beyond city limits, the constitutionally mandated Metropolitan Planning Committees (MPC) need to be activated with binding powers. o Currently, the urban outer area remains a "no man's land" which is run by rural panchayats, who are insufficiently equipped for urbanization. MPC will coordinate these fragmented jurisdictions under a unified regional plan. o This will ensure that the necessary arrangements like water supply, sewage and transport are planned holistically for the entire metropolitan area, preventing the formation of "census towns" without facilities. § Accountability: Creating "Right to Services Act" for Municipalities: o States should enact strict urban public Right to Services Acts, setting clear timelines for municipal services such as birth certificates, water connections, road and pothole repairs. o This law should include automatic penalty provisions under which fines are deducted from the salary of the responsible officer for delays and compensation is provided to the citizen. o This shifts the administrative culture from "arbitrarily granted privilege" to "entitled right", making it mandatory for the bureaucracy to adhere to service level standards, otherwise face direct financial consequences. Conclusion: The success of India's Developed India vision depends on actually authorizing and empowering the urban local bodies under the 74th Constitutional Amendment, and not letting them remain under colonial-era bureaucratic control. Empowering the role of the Mayor, ensuring financial autonomy, and enhancing citizen participation are necessary to transform cities into adaptive and development-oriented engines, to promote Sustainable Development Goal 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), and to strengthen accountability necessary for sustainable urban development.
