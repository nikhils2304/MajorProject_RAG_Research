Introduction 1.1 Spheres of the Earth

(Spheres of the Earth) The Earth is a unique planet in the solar system. The Earth consists of four spheres. They are the lithosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere and biosphere. In this lesson, we will learn about the interior of the Earth. The order of the lessons is based on the importance of the Earth’s lifeless spheres.
Do you know about the structure of the Earth? Or do you know what is inside the Earth?

The total surface area of the Earth's crust is 510 million square kilometers. The four spheres of the Earth are interconnected. The lithosphere, atmosphere and hydrosphere are lifeless spheres. The sphere where living beings live is the biosphere. All these spheres depend on each other.

1.  2 Interior of the Earth

(Interior of the Earth)

The surface and the interior of the Earth differ in nature and structure. The internal structure of the Earth is divided into three parts: crust, mantle and core.

The Earth is shaped like an onion. It is also called the lithosphere. The area surrounded by water is called the hydrosphere, and the area surrounded by air is called the atmosphere. Since living beings live in the place where these three spheres meet, this area is called the biosphere.

Earth's Crust

(Crust)

The Earth's crust can be divided into the continental crust and the oceanic crust. The continental crust is called Sial because it contains more Silica (Si) and Aluminum (Al), and the oceanic crust is called Sima because it contains more Silica (Si) and Magnesium (Ma).

The continental crust is thicker than the oceanic crust.

Mantle (Mantle)

The area below the Earth's crust is called the Mantle. Its thickness is about 2900 kilometers. Rocks are solid in the upper part of the mantle and in molten state in the lower part.

Magma is the molten rock inside the Earth.

Core (Core)

The layer located at the center of the Earth below the Earth's mantle is called the core. It is very hot. This layer is called NIFE because the core contains more Nickel (Ni) and Iron (Fe). The core consists of two layers: the inner core is solid and the outer core is liquid. The high amount of iron in the Earth's core is the reason for the Earth's gravity. As the Earth rotates on its axis, the magnetic field is created because the liquid outer core rotates around the solid inner core. This is the reason why magnetic compass works. Because there is high pressure in the inner core, the objects there are compressed and tightened. Therefore, the inner core is solid.

Historical Information

The ancient city of Petra in Jordan is entirely formed by carving rocks. There are many building arts formed by carving rocks in India. The Ajanta and Ellora caves in Maharashtra, the Aihole and Badami temples in Karnataka, the Konark temple in Odisha, and the Mamallapuram temple in Tamil Nadu are examples of this.

1.  3 Rocks (Rocks)

The Earth's crust is composed of rocks. A rock is a mixture of minerals. Rocks can be solid like granite, soft like clay, or granular like sand.

Types of Rocks

Depending on how rocks are formed, they can be divided as follows:

 Igneous Rocks

 Sedimentary Rock

 Metamorphic Rocks

Igneous Rocks (Igneous Rocks)

The Latin word "Ignis" means fire. Magma is molten rock deep inside the Earth. Magma is released on the Earth's crust and becomes lava. The magma cools as it gets warmer. These cooled rocks are called igneous rocks. The Deccan Plateau is made of igneous rocks. (Example) Granite, Basalt. Igneous rocks are also called 'Primary Rocks' or 'Parent Rocks' because other rocks are directly or indirectly formed from these rocks.

Sedimentary Rocks (Sedimentary Rocks)

The Latin word “sediment” means ‘settling’. Rocks erode and turn into particles, and sediments are deposited layer by layer by rivers, snow rivers, and wind. Sediments thus deposited turn into sedimentary rocks after millions of years.

Plants and animals are deposited in these sediments and turn into fossils. Examples of sedimentary rocks are sandstone, limestone, lime, gypsum, coal and conglomerate.

Until 2011, the deepest part of the world was the Kola Superdeep Borehole (12,262 m deep) in Murmansk, Russia. In 2012, the Z – 44 Chayvo well (Russia) (12,376 m deep) has acquired the title of deepest part. It is 15 times larger than the Burj Khalifa in Dubai. Studies on the Earth's interior are ongoing.

Metamorphic Rocks (Metamorphic Rocks)

The word 'metamorphic' is derived from the word 'metamorphosis'. It means transformation. When igneous and sedimentary rocks are subjected to high heat and pressure, their structure, qualities, and characteristics change. The rocks thus formed are called metamorphic rocks. Granite changes to gneiss, basalt to schist, limestone to marble, and sandstone to quartzite.

Rock Cycle (Rock Cycle)

The rock cycle is a continuous process. In this cycle, igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks change from one structure to another and remain.

Look closely at the rock cycle diagram given and describe its activity in your own words.

(Geomorphic Processes)

Geomorphic processes occur in two ways.

Forces acting from the Earth's interior to the Earth's surface are called 'Endogenic Processes'. These forces create various landforms on the Earth's surface. Natural factors such as rivers, snow rivers, wind, and waves that act on the Earth's surface are called 'Exogenic Processes'. These factors erode the Earth's surface, deposit sediments in lowlands, and raise them and shape the land formed by endogenic processes.

Endogenic Processes

Because of the heat generated inside the Earth, the substances deep inside the Earth are expelled. The Earth's internal radioactivity is the main source for this process. Example: Volcano eruption.

Plate Tectonics

The lithosphere is divided into several plates. These are divided into Major Plates and Minor Plates. These plates float on the mantle. Mountain ranges and irregular landforms are formed on the surface and on the seabed as the plates collide with each other. This event is called Plate Movement. The heat energy in the mantle is the cause for plate movements. Plate movement is a cause for earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.

Types of Plate Boundaries

(Types of plate boundaries)

Convergent boundary - Plates collide with each other and sometimes subduction occurs below. This area is called Subduction Zone. Example: Fold mountains - Himalayas.

Divergent boundary - As plates move away from each other, magma is released from the Earth's mantle. Example - Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

Conservative/Transform boundary - When plates move horizontally past each other, it is called transform movement. Example - San Andreas Fault.

Continental Plate Movements

(Movements of continental plates)

Foldings are formed as plates move up and down. The mountains formed by folding in rocks are called Fold Mountains. The Himalayas and the Alps were formed by this event.

The Earth's plates are constantly moving. They move on average a few centimeters per year. Although this movement appears to be small, we can realize that they have moved a long distance over millions of years. For example, the Indian plate, which was a part of the Gondwana land 250 million years ago, was connected to the continents of Africa, Australia, Antarctica and South America.

Due to the horizontal compression force 140 million years ago, the Indian plate separated from the supercontinent Gondwana and moved northwards to join Asia.

The Indian and Eurasian plates collided at the Indo-Nepal border, forming an orogenic belt.

The Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau, the highest plateau in the world, were formed in this region.

Earthquake

(Earthquake)

An earthquake is a sudden tremor in the Earth's crust. Seismic waves spread in all directions from the epicenter. The point where the earthquake originates inside the Earth is called the focus. These waves create additional elastic waves around them. The point located on the Earth's surface directly above the focus is called the epicenter. The impact of an earthquake is greatest on the Earth's surface at the epicenter.

Seismic waves (Seismic waves)

Earthquakes generate seismic waves. The nature, force, and velocity of these vibrations vary depending on the material they pass through. Based on the nature of seismic waves, they are classified into three types.

Primary or 'P' - waves: Primary waves are faster than other waves. Primary waves reach the Earth's crust first. These waves travel through solid, liquid, and gaseous materials. Its average speed varies from 5.3 kilometers per second to 10.6 kilometers per second.

Historical Information

C.F. Richter discovered the earthquake scale in 1935. This scale helps to know the energy released from the Earth's core and the intensity of the earthquake. This scale has no limit. In 1960, the earthquake that registered 9.5 on the Richter scale at Valdivia, Chile, is considered the highest record.

Secondary or 'S' - waves: Secondary waves can only travel through solid materials. These transverse waves cause vibrations in the Earth perpendicular to the direction they travel. Its average speed is from 1 kilometer per second to 8 kilometers per second.

Surface or 'L' – waves: Surface waves are like primary waves. But they travel a long distance on the Earth's surface. These waves are slower than other waves. They are more likely to cause destruction. Its average speed is from 1 kilometer per second to 5 kilometers per second.

The instrument that records earthquakes is called a Seismograph or Seismometer. The study of earthquakes is called Seismology.

Tsunami

Tsunami is a Japanese word that refers to harbor waves. Earthquakes, volcanic activity (submarine explosion), and major landslides occurring in coastal areas create large waves in the sea. These waves travel at an average speed of 500 kilometers per hour. The length of these waves is more than 600 kilometers. These waves rise up to 15 meters when they reach the coast. These cause great damage to coastal areas.

Volcanoes

(Volcanoes)

The word ‘Volcano’ comes from the Latin word ‘Vulcan’. It is the name of the ‘Roman God of Fire’.

Magma which is in solid, liquid and gaseous states in the interior of the Earth erupts on the Earth's surface through a vent. This is called a volcanic eruption. The magma released on the Earth's surface is called lava. Volcanoes are also formed by the movement of Earth's plates.

Main components found in volcanoes:

i. Magma chamber – This is a large pool of magma under the Earth.

ii. Vents – The ways for gases, smoke, fire and magma to escape during a volcanic eruption are called vents.

iii. Volcanic cone – The magma released through the vents creates a cone-shaped landform.

iv. Crater – The funnel-shaped depression at the top of the volcano is the ‘crater’.

Volcanoes can be divided into three types based on how they act. They are

(i) Active Volcano,

(ii) Dormant Volcano,

(iii) Extinct Volcano

(a) Active Volcano

Active volcanoes are volcanoes that constantly emit magma, particles, and gases. Example: Mount St. Helens – United States of America

(b) Dormant volcano

Dormant volcanoes are volcanoes that have been inactive for a long time. They have the potential to erupt suddenly. There are chances of high loss of life and property when such volcanoes erupt. Example: Fujiyama – Japan.

(c) Extinct volcano

Extinct volcanoes are volcanoes that have no volcanic activity. Example: Kilimanjaro Volcano - Tanzania

Composite volcano

Composite volcanoes are also called strata volcanoes. The sediments of cinder, hardened magma and pumice are deposited layer by layer during volcanic activity. They are cone-shaped. Example: Fujiyama Volcano – Japan.

Dome volcano

When the magma with high silica erupts with high viscosity, it cannot spread to a long distance and settles in a circular shape near the crater and appears like a small hill. This volcano is called a dome volcano. Example: Paricutin Volcano – Mexico.

Shield volcano

Magma with high viscosity flows smoothly in all directions and appears like a dome. This type of volcano is called a shield volcano. Example: Mauna Loa Volcano – Hawaii Island.

Distribution of Volcanoes and Earthquake Areas

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes do not occur everywhere in the world, but only in certain places. Specifically, they occur at the edges of plates. This area is called the Pacific Ring of Fire because volcanic eruptions occur frequently in the Pacific Ocean region where the Pacific plate joins other continental plates.

This area is the most active zone of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions in the world. Next to it are the Mid-oceanic ridges and Mid-continental belts where earthquakes and volcanic eruptions occur frequently. Alps-Himalaya region.

Effects of Volcanoes

Benefits

The materials released from volcanoes enrich the soil. So agriculture is improved. Areas with volcanoes help to harness geothermal energy. Dormant and active volcanoes are the world's best tourist attractions. The materials released from volcanoes are used for construction.

Disadvantages

Volcanic eruptions cause earthquakes, flash floods, landslides and mudslides. Magma traveling for a long distance burns and buries everything in its path. Dust and ash released in large quantities cause eye irritation and breathing difficulties. Volcanic eruptions change the climate of its surrounding areas and cause traffic disruption (Volcanic eruption in Iceland)
