The Sangam Age Tamil Society and Culture

Learning Objectives

*   To learn about the distinguishing features of the literary, archaeological, epigraphic, and non-Tamil language sources for understanding the Tamil society of the Sangam Age.
*   To understand the societal system based on landscape.
*   To understand the Sangam literature, politics, society, economy, and urbanization.

Introduction

Classical Tamil literature like the Tolkappiyam, the Eighteen Major Works, and the Five Great Epics emerged during the Sangam Age. Tamil culture had already taken root before the 3rd century BCE. Tamil merchants and seafarers had trade and cultural links with overseas countries. Foreign traders came to Tamilakam by sea. Cultural contacts and trade activities with foreign lands, along with the growth of indigenous production and lifestyle, led to the first urbanization in Tamil Nadu. Capital cities and port towns emerged. Coinage and currency came into use. The Tamil language was first written in the ‘Tamil Brahmi’ script. Sources were created. Classical Tamil poems were composed in the Tamil language. These are the earliest literary works to have appeared in Tamil. The Eighteen Minor Works were later in time.

3.1 Sources for Learning About the Early Tamil Society

Various types of sources help in scientifically reconstructing the history of the early Tamils. These are:

1.  Classical Tamil literature
2.  Inscriptions
3.  Archaeological excavation finds and cultural artifacts
4.  Literary accounts of non-Tamils and foreigners

Tolkappiyam

The Tolkappiyam, composed by Tolkappiyar, is the earliest extant work on Tamil grammar. The first two sections of this book define the poetics of the Tamil language. The third part defines the poetics for the social life of the people.

The Ten Idylls and the Eight Anthologies are called the Eighteen Major Works. These are the earliest literature in Tamil. The Eighteen Minor Works are later in time.

The Eight Anthologies are:

(1)  Nattrinai (2) Kurunthokai

(3)  Paripadal (4) Pathitrupathu

(5)  Aingurunuru (6) Kaliththokai

The Ten Idylls are:

(1)  Thirumurugatrupadai

(2)  Porunaratrupadai

(3)  Perumpanatrupadai

(4)  Sirupanatrupadai (8) Kurincippattu

(5)  Mullaipattu (9) Pattinappalai

The Eighteen Minor Works:

The eighteen books that expound the code of conduct for life are compiled as the Eighteen Minor Works. The most important of these is the Thirukkural, composed by Thiruvalluvar. Containing 1330 couplets, the Thirukkural is divided into three sections: Aram (virtue), Porul (wealth), and Inbam (love).

The Five Great Epics:

Epics are long literary works in the form of poems. They are:

(1)  Silappathikaram (4) Valayapathi

(2)  Manimekalai (5) Kundalakesi

(3)  Sivakasintamani

Cave Inscriptions

The study of inscriptions is called ‘Epigraphy’. Inscriptions can be found on rocks, tools, coins, and other such objects. The period when the script of a language appeared is considered the early history. Tamil Brahmi inscriptions are found in over thirty sites in Tamil Nadu in caves and on rock beds. Jaina monks mostly used these caves as their residences.

Note: Some of the ancient inscriptions have been damaged by people (locals, tourists) who wrote or carved their names on them. Destroying heritage property or the property of others is called ‘Vandalism’.

The large caves are located on the ancient trade routes. In the caves, smooth beds were carved out of the rocks themselves. Kings and merchants provided naturally formed caves to monks who renounced worldly life as their abodes. In Tamil Nadu, Tamil Brahmi inscriptions are found in Mangulam, Mutupatti, Pugalur, Arachalur, Kongarpuliyankulam, Jambai, Madurai, and other places.

Hero Stones

Hero stones were erected in memory of warriors who died in battles over cattle raids. The pastoral people of Mullai landscape considered cattle as their special wealth. The tribal people who lived nearby fought with other groups to seize their livestock for themselves. It was customary for the chieftain of the Mullai region to fight against the enemy and reclaim the livestock. There were also those who fought against it. They revered those warriors who died during that time as martyrs and erected hero stones in their memory. Sangam literature extensively mentions the scenes of warfare, hero stones, and the rituals performed on them. The Tolkappiyam elaborates on the procedure for erecting hero stones.

Written on burial pottery

Hero Stone, Pulimankombai

Sangam-age hero stones with Tamil Brahmi inscriptions have been discovered in Pulimankombai and Thathapatti in Theni district and Porpanaikkottai in Pudukkottai district. The hero stones belonging to the Sangam Age do not have any images or sculptures. Hero stones erected after the Sangam Age and during the Pallava period are mostly found in the Mullai region. Specifically, such hero stones are found extensively in the areas surrounding the town of Sengam in the Tiruvannamalai district. The names of the warriors in whose memory those hero stones were erected are inscribed and the images of the heroes are also sculpted.

Pottery with inscriptions:

The names of the people are inscribed in Tamil Brahmi on the pottery found dating back to the early historic times. Such pottery inscriptions are found in many places in Tamil Nadu, including Arikamedu, Alagankulam, Kodumanal, and Keeladi. In addition, pieces of pottery with the names of people in Tamil Brahmi script have been found in Berenike and Quseir al Qadim in Egypt and Khor Rori in Oman.

The appearance of a ship on pottery found at Alagankulam.

Thus it is possible to understand that ancient Tamils were involved in trade with the Roman Empire in West Asia and beyond. People inscribed their names on an object to indicate that it belonged to them. The common names found on pottery are in Tamil, and some names are in Prakrit. They also wrote their names to identify their belongings when they were transporting them in ships or carts.

Prakrit was the language spoken by the common people in North India during the Mauryan period.

Archaeological Excavations

Archaeological excavation is the process of systematically digging up a site to uncover and methodically study material evidence of past human life in order to understand the way of life of an ancient society.

Cultural artifacts

Archaeologists discovered brick structures, beads, shell bangles, intaglio, and cameo in places where they carried out excavations dating back to the early historic period. Tamil Brahmi inscribed pottery shards and various types of coins have been found there. These archaeological finds help to understand the lifestyle, arts, skills, and trades of the people who lived in that period.

Cameo are precious stones with carved relief images. Intaglio are ornaments with engraved images.

Coins

Coinage, for business purposes, was first introduced during the Sangam Age. Chera, Chola, Pandya kings' coins, coins with emblems, and Roman coins are another source of information about the Sangam Age. Punch-marked coins have been found in Kodumanal and Bodinayakanur.

Roman coins found in Pudukkottai

Roman coins are found in the Coimbatore region of South India. They have also been found in Alagankulam, Karur, and Madurai. At some point, coins were also preserved as a valuable treasure.

Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) is an Indian government agency that manages archaeological sites, constructions, and monuments. It operates under the authority of the central government. The Tamil Nadu Archaeological Department operates under the government of Tamil Nadu. The Indian Treasure Trove Act (1878), the Antiquities and Art Treasures Act (1972), and the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act (1958) have been enacted to preserve archaeological materials in India.

The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea

The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea is an ancient Greek text. The author of this book is unknown. Periplus means a navigational guide. Sailors used these guides for maritime travel. The sea around the Red Sea is called the Erythraean Sea. This book contains references to the Sangam Age port cities of Musiri, Pondi, Kumari, Korkai, and the Chera and Pandya kings.

Pliny’s ‘Natural History’

Pliny the Elder, a Roman, wrote the book ‘Natural History’. Written in Latin, this book describes the natural resources of the Roman Empire. Pliny, referring to the pepper trade with India, states that if the southwest monsoon blows correctly from the port of Ocelis near northeast Africa, India can be reached in forty days. He mentions that the Pandya kings who ruled Madurai controlled the port of Bacare on the Kerala coast. The southwestern name of the port of Bacare is unknown. Pliny laments that the Roman Empire's wealth was drained by the pepper trade with India. From this, one can understand the value of pepper and that pepper was exported to Tamilakam.

Arthashastra

Kautilya, also known as Chanakya, who lived during the Mauryan period, wrote the book Arthashastra, which discusses economics and governance. The reference to ‘Pandya Kavataka’ in this book may refer to the pearls and marine products found in the Pandya country.

Mahavamsa

The Mahavamsa, a book that narrates the Buddhist history of Sri Lanka, is written in the Pali language. This book mentions the traders and horse traders who were in South India and Tamil Nadu. A chronicle is a record that describes important historical events in chronological order.

Vienna Papyrus

The Vienna Papyrus is a Greek document dating to the second century. This contains a note about the trade that took place in Musiri. This document is currently kept in the Papyrus Collection of the Austrian National Library in Vienna, the capital of Austria. This is a written agreement between merchants. The inventory lists goods such as pepper and ivory that were exported from the ship Hermapollon to the Roman Empire.

Papyrus is a paper made from papyrus reeds in ancient Egypt. At that time, this was used for writing.

3.2 The Sangam Age

The Sangam Age or the Early Historic Period is a remarkable period in the history of South India. The Sangam Age stands out from the pre-historic period because of the availability of written sources in the form of literature, Tamil Brahmi inscriptions, and so on.

Chronologically, the period between the 3rd century BCE and 3rd century CE is considered as the Sangam Age, based on the consensus among the scholars. Information on the Chera, Chola, and Pandya kings found in the Ashoka inscriptions, Tamil Brahmi inscriptions, and Greek and Roman records corroborate this timeframe. It is believed that Sangam poems were composed during the Early Historic Period and later compiled as anthologies.

Asokan Brahmi is named after the Brahmi script used in the inscriptions of Asoka.

Thinai

The concept of Thinai is explained in the Tolkappiyam, the oldest extant grammatical text in Tamil. Thinai refers to a specific ecological landscape and its associated way of life. Sangam poems are organized on the basis of Thinai. They capture human experiences and the close relationship humans had with nature.

Sangam poems can be broadly divided into two categories: Akam poems and Puram poems, based on the theme. Akam refers to love and family life. Puram refers to other aspects of life, especially war and valour.

Ainthinai: Five Thinais or Five Types of Landscapes

Ainthinai refers to five types of landscapes in Tamil Nadu. These five types of landscapes have distinct characteristics. Each Thinai has its own specific deity, occupation, people, and culture. Scholars have explained that this classification reflects the actual living environment of the people who lived in the five types of lands.

The five landscapes are Kurinji, Mullai, Marutham, Neithal, and Palai.

*   Mountains and mountain-related regions – Kurinji
*   Forest and forest-related regions – Mullai
*   Arid regions – Palai
*   Seashore and seashore-related regions – Neithal
*   Agricultural land – Marutham

3.3 Sangam Politics: The Political Setup of Ancient Tamils

The foundation for the Sangam Age was laid in the Iron Age. During the Iron Age, people lived in groups. Chieftains brought those regions under their control. Thus, chieftains emerged in the early historical period from among these chieftains. Velirs were the chieftains of the Sangam Age.

The Mauryan emperor Asoka invaded Kalinga (Odisha) and some parts of Andhra and Karnataka.

The Muventar

Among the rulers of the Sangam Age, the three chieftains, namely the Cheras, the Cholas, and the Pandyas, were at the forefront. The capital cities and port cities of that period were under the control of the Muventar.

Cheras

The Cheras, referred to as Kerala Puthras in Asoka’s inscriptions, ruled the southern part of present-day Kerala and the western part of Tamil Nadu. Their capital was Vanji, and their port cities were Musiri and Thondi. Some people claim that Thiruvanchikulam in present-day Kerala is the ancient Vanji. The Pathitrupathu of Sangam literature speaks about the Chera kings and their territories. The Cheras wore garlands made of Karumbu flowers. Inscriptions found in Pugalur near Karur mention three generations of Chera kings. Coins issued by the kings have been found in Karur.

A Chera coin with an elephant on one side and an Ankush and a bow and arrow on the other side.

Silappathikaram extensively mentions the Chera king Senguttuvan, who built a temple for Kannagi, the patron goddess of Silappathikaram. Ilango Adigal, the author of Silappathikaram, was the brother of Cheran Senguttuvan. The bow and arrow is the emblem of the Cheras.

Cholas

The Cholas who ruled the Cauvery Delta region and the northern parts of Tamil Nadu had Uraiyur as their capital. Poompuhar or Kaveripattinam at the mouth of the river Cauvery served as their port city. The Sangam poet Mudaththamakanniyar has composed the Pattinappalai about Kaveripattinam. Silappathikaram mentions the trade that took place in Kaveripattinam.

Karikala Chola, who is celebrated as the greatest among the Chola kings, effectively harnessed the water resources of the river Cauvery and enriched agriculture. The foundation for efficient water management, which reached its peak during the later Cholas (10th to 13th century), was laid during the reign of Karikala Chola. Karikalan fought against the Pandyas, the Cheras, and several other chieftains. The tiger is the emblem of the Cholas. They issued square-shaped copper coins. These had the image of a tiger on one side and an elephant and sacred symbols on the other side.

Sangam age Chola coins with tiger emblem on one side and elephant and sacred symbol on the other side

Pandiyas

Inscriptions of Asoka mention the Pandyas. They ruled southern Tamilakam with Madurai as their capital. Tamil literature praises the Pandya kings for establishing and supporting the Tamil Sangams. Nediyon, Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi, and Nallagakolai Mudukudumi Peruvaluthi are the notable Pandya kings.

Tamil Brahmi inscriptions found in Mangulam mention the Pandyan Nedunchezhiyan. The emblem of the Pandyas is the fish.

Chieftains/ Minor Rulers

In addition to the ruling Muventar, several minor rulers and chieftains ruled over smaller territories in Tamilakam. These chieftains were called Velirs. Prominent among the Velirs were Pari, Kari, Ori, Nalli, Deegan, Ay, and Adiyaman. Sangam literature praises the generosity of these Velirs. The chieftains who had close contact with the poets are celebrated in the literature for their patronage. Some Velirs stood by the Muventar and fought for them. Certain other Velirs fought against the Muventar as well.

3.4 The Sangam Society

During the Iron Age, several communities were tribal communities. Some lived together as a clan. The Sangam Age society was in transition from a tribal society to a society with a major ruling king.

Social Divisions

Social divisions took root in Tamil society during the Sangam Age. Groups like Panar, Paratavar, Eyinar, Uzhavar, Kanavar, Vettuvar, and Maravar were clan-based communities. Kings and clan leaders were considered the upper class. There were also priests known as Andanars. Artisans, potters, metal workers, and other such sections existed in society. The caste system prevalent in North India did not take root in Tamil Nadu. Instead, social groups were found on the basis of five types of environments and occupations.

Agricultural development and cattle rearing must have affected the natural resources and wildlife to some extent. Some of the sections that lived by hunting may have been forced to live in the forests or may have transformed themselves into industrial workers over time. In fertile agricultural areas, certain social groups provided a lot of manual labour for the development of agriculture.

There are many references in Sangam literature about mothers, deities, midwives, and female poets. Sangam literature mentions female poets of Panar clan, dancing women, goldsmith poets, royal women, and women belonging to five landscapes. Avvaiyar, who belonged to the village of Pavani, is recognized as a goldsmith poet. From this, it can be understood that women were involved in primary production. In some places, Sangam literature mentions women going to the battlefield along with their husbands.

3.5 The Economy

The Sangam Age economy was diverse. Agriculture, cattle rearing, trade, barter, hunting, and fishing were the professions and related economies based on the environment in which the people lived.

Agricultural Production

Agriculture was at the forefront in fulfilling the livelihood needs of the people of the Sangam Age. Paddy, sugarcane, and millets were cultivated. Agriculture was carried out in fertile land and dry land. Paddy was cultivated in river basins and in areas with irrigation facilities such as ponds and lakes. Millets were cultivated in dry land. Literary works mention various types of rice, such as Vennel and Aivana Nel. The excavations carried out in Adichanallur and Porunthal have yielded paddy along with tools. In forest areas, the method of cultivating by shifting from place to place was called Punam.

Cattle Rearing: People improved their livelihood by raising cattle, including cows, buffaloes, and oxen, and sheep.

Sangam Age Crafts and Manufacturing Centers

Handicrafts and delicate artifacts were important indicators of urban life. During the Sangam Age, there were skilled artisans in various places who were experts in manufacturing goods. Manufacturing units are workshops where goods are produced.

Pottery Making

Pottery making was common in all places. Pottery that was essential for the daily life of the people was produced by potters called ‘Potseiitgol’. Various types of pottery, such as black pottery, russet-coated, and black-red ware, were produced.

Iron Smelting

Making tools out of iron was an important industry. Iron smelting workshops were found in various places. There are references to blacksmiths in Sangam literature. Agricultural tools and weapons such as swords, spears, and knives were manufactured.

Jewellery

People of the Sangam Age wore various ornaments and beautified themselves. Ordinary people wore flowers and leaves, and also wore ornaments made of terracotta and metal. The wealthy wore ornaments made of metals like gold and silver, which were decorated with gems.

Gold jewelry was commonly worn by women during the Sangam Age. Jewellery was also made using Roman coins. Evidence of furnaces for melting gold has been found in Kerala. Gold jewelry has been found in prehistoric excavation sites such as Suthukkenni, Adichanallur, Kodumanal, and ancient cities such as Arikamedu, Keeladi, and Pattanam.

Glass Beads

People of that time knew how to make glass beads. Silica and other materials were melted in a furnace and made into long, thin tubes, which were then cut into small beads. Glass beads were made in various colors and shapes. Glass bead manufacturing industries were located in Arikamedu and Kudikadu near Cuddalore. It seems that those who did not have the opportunity to buy precious gemstones may have worn glass beads.

Cloth Weaving

Cloth weaving was another important industry. Spindles and pieces of cloth have been found in Kodumanal. Literary references exist about Kalingam and other types of cloth. The Periplus mentions that the fabrics in Tamilakam were woven. Spindle whorls are used to extract thread from cotton.

Trade, Commerce, Merchants, and Trade Routes

We know about items such as paddy, cattle rearing, and handicrafts production. However, all the items cannot be produced in all the places where people lived. Raw materials, resources, and skilled artisans found in one place are not found in other places. For example, sea fish and salt are not available in mountain areas. Paddy and other grains cannot be cultivated in the interior parts of the seacoast. Those who have the items available in one place will exchange the items with those who have them in other places. This is called Barter.

Merchants

The words Vanigan and Nigama are used in Tamil Brahmi inscriptions. There were merchants who traded only in specific areas such as gold merchants, cloth merchants, and salt merchants. Salt merchants were called Umanars. They travelled on bullock carts with their families to conduct trade.

Barter and Coins

The barter system was the means of exchange among the people. They got fish in exchange for rice. Salt was considered valuable. Therefore, the same amount of rice was exchanged for a certain amount of salt. Coins from the Sangam Age Chera, Chola, Pandya, and Malayaman rulers have been found, from which it is possible to know that they were widely used.

Well designed Yavana ships came to the port of Musiri laden with gold, other metals, and coins and returned with pepper (Agananuru 149).

Another port is Quseir al Qadim. In this place, three pieces of pottery were found with Tamil Brahmi inscriptions. It is written as Kalan Ori, Kanan, and Sattan (Sathan).

From Tamil Nadu, the sea route to Rome

Contacts between Tamilakam and Foreign Countries

The Tamils had trade relations with many foreign countries in the east and west. Roman ships crossed the Arabian Sea during the monsoon season and reached the seacoasts of Tamilakam. Spices like pepper, elephant ivory, and rare items including gems were exported from Tamil Nadu. Metals including gold, silver, and copper were imported.

Westerners, including Greeks, Romans, and West Asians, are called Yavanas. The word Yavana comes from ‘Ionia’, a Greek region.

Pepper from Egypt

In Berenike, Egypt, an Indian pot containing 7.5 kg of pepper, pieces of teak wood, and a pottery shard (from Tamilakam) with Tamil Brahmi inscriptions were found.

One of the rarest stones found in the Quan Luk Pat of Thoi Banh, Vietnam is called ‘Perum Pathan Kal’. This stone may have been used by Perum Pathan. Also, he must have been a goldsmith. That stone is also a touchstone which is helpful in testing the purity of the gold.

3.6 Cities and the Appearance of Port Cities

Cities in Tamil Nadu were first formed during the Sangam Age. Planned cities with streets and residential areas were built. Brick structures were built in the planned cities. Roof tiles were used for houses. There were ring wells and storage tanks. Some cities were port cities and centers of handicraft industries. Arikamedu, Kaveripoompattinam, and Alagankulam, Korkai located on the east coast, and Pattanam in Kerala were prominent as port cities. Kanchipuram, Uraiyur, Karur, Madurai, and Kodumanal were inland trade centers. Various types of goods and artifacts were produced in these centers and exported to different regions. Even though the number was small, these cities were large in size. Numerous villages also had people living. Artefacts like bronze objects, beads, shell bangles, glass beads, and pottery with people’s names inscribed in Tamil Brahmi have been found in these sites.

What is urbanization?

A city is a human settlement with planned designs and brick structures. People who are involved in occupations other than agriculture and cattle rearing will live in large numbers in those places. Various production activities take place in cities.

3.7 Beliefs and Rituals

The way of life of the people and the economic system of the Sangam Age had uniqueness. There were various forms of worship during the Sangam Age: Spirit worship, Ancestor worship, Hero worship, and Small deity worship.

The Tolkappiyam refers to the deities of the five landscapes. Tholkappiyar says that Murugan is the god of Kurinji, Thirumal is the god of Mullai, Indiran is the god of Marutham, Varunan is the god of Neythal, and Kotravai is the god of Palai. However, people mostly worshipped heroes who died in battle and their clan ancestors. Sangam literature mentions the supernatural power possessed by Ayi.

Tamil Brahmi inscriptions found in caves suggest that Jainism was prevalent. Evidence exists for the performance of sacrifices. Buddhism was also practiced in some places. Different groups followed different ways of worship.

3.8 Fine Arts

Various arts flourished in the Sangam Age. There was a type of dance called Veriyattam which was performed during religious ceremonies. Most people knew how to compose poems, play musical instruments, and dance. The Sangam Age diet is mentioned in the literature. Women used thin sticks made of copper to apply kohl to their eyes. These sticks have been excavated in various places. Because of this, it is evident that they cared about their appearance.

Porunai Civilization: The Cradle of Tamil Culture

Porunai (Thamirabarani) is the only perennial river in Tamil Nadu. It flows through the districts of Tirunelveli and Thoothukudi.

Dr. Jagor, a German surveyor, conducted excavations in 1876 at Adichanallur, located on the banks of the Porunai River. Later, Alexander Ray of England conducted excavations in Adichanallur from 1899 to 1905. The excavated artifacts are on display at the Chennai Government Museum. After that, after about a century, the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) conducted an archaeological excavation in Adichanallur in 2004. Its report has been released recently. The Tamil Nadu State Department of Archeology commenced excavation work (2019 – 2021) at the burial sites and residential areas of Adichanallur. Korkai is an ancient port mentioned in Sangam literature and Greek literature. Excavations were conducted here in 1968 by the Department of Archeology of the Government of Tamil Nadu. The current excavation (2020 – 2021) is bringing out many interesting information about the Korkai port and its foreign connections.

In 2019, at Sivagalai, which is few kilometers away from Adichanallur, the paddy grains found in the urn have been subjected to carbon analysis at the Beta Laboratory in Miami, America and its period has been dated to the pre-common era (B.C. 1155). This confirms that the Thamirabarani culture is 3200 years old.

Findings in Adichanallur, Sivagalai and Korkai

Burial Pots: Bones, vessels kept in the belief that they would be useful for the deceased, gold, large amount of leaded bronze, and iron objects have been found in the burial urns buried at a depth of 3 to 12 feet in the soil-rock layers.

Golden forehead bands

Oval-shaped forehead bands made of gold sheets have been found with small triangular shaped carvings and holes at both ends to tie them to the forehead with ropes.

Pottery Types

Black-redware pot types and more than 500 scratched symbols and Tamil (Tamil-Brahmi) inscribed potshards have been found.

A large number of leaded bronze objects

Small and large numbers of leaded bronze vessels of various shapes have been found.

Ornaments

Samples made of copper, iron, gold, glass, ivory, bone, and shell beads and bangles have been found.

Human Figures made of Clay

Female figures similar to what was found in Indus valley have been discovered.

Iron Objects

Various types of iron tools such as throwing spears, javelins, and short swords have been found.

Shell Bangle Work Shop

Thirty-two complete shells, cut shell bangles, and bangle pieces were found which confirms the presence of shell bangle workshop. Many of the Sangam literary songs support the presence of shell bangle makers in Korkai.

Potshards and Coins Revealing Foreign Connections

Foreign potsherds and black polished mudware found here confirm Tamilakam’s trade relations with other parts of the world dating back to the sixth century BCE.

*   Urban life flourished and culture thrived through agricultural production, social relations among people in the landscape, and overseas trade. Literature was created. Literary works were compiled later through the Tamil Sangam.
*   The landscape method explained by the Tolkappiyam is the unique way of life for Tamilakam.
*   During the Sangam Age, society began to shift from tribal people's way of life to kingship.
*   Maritime trade flourished in the Indian Ocean region.
*   Large cities with brick structures emerged in Tamil Nadu
*   The social structure was diversely different.
