Introduction

The 18th century witnessed changes not only in economics but also deep impacts in society and politics.

The big change in the method of production transformed human history itself. This large change is called the period of the Industrial Revolution. Goods produced by hand were replaced by production by machines. As a result, the production of goods increased many fold.

It was marked by the rise of factories and domination by machine production. The Industrial Revolution first began in England, and then spread throughout the world. Though the term ‘Industrial Revolution’ was first used by French writers, it gained wide currency when English economic historians used the term to refer to the growth in English economy from 1760 to 1840.

10.1 Features of the Industrial Revolution

• Use of new basic materials: iron and steel

• Use of new energy sources: coal, electricity, petroleum

• Invention of new machines like Spinning jenny, power loom helped to reduce human efforts and increase the rate of production.

• A new organisation of work known as the factory system, which entailed increased division of labour and specialisation of function

• Development of transportation and communication

• Increasing application of science to industry

• Use of new technology

Beginning

Certain external factors in England made the Industrial Revolution possible there. They were:

• England had plenty of resources and colonies. “India was a shining jewel in its crown”.

• Raw materials such as coal, iron, and cotton were available from the colonies.

• The artisans who had migrated to England from the Netherlands had already created the infrastructure needed for textile production before the Industrial Revolution.

• England had developed its banking system, which, along with a growing industry, included venture capitalists.

• The British royalty encouraged scientific discoveries.

• The stable political nature in England at that time helped to focus entirely on industrial development.

Discovery of Steam Power

Increased seepage of water in the mines in the 18th century was a major hindrance to mine owners in England. They needed to extract water to mine coal. For this, they employed more workers. This cost them huge amounts of money. It was at this time that a British engineer, Thomas Newcomen, invented a new kind of water-lifting machine that used steam and coal to lift water from the mines. However, the machine he created used up more fuel. In this situation, James Watt, an engineer from Scotland, modified the static steam engine into a rotating steam engine. This used less fuel.

Textile Industry Growth

Before the Industrial Revolution, spinning yarn and weaving cloth for manufacturing textile were carried on mainly for domestic and local consumption. These were conducted in small, rented spaces, either at home or elsewhere. The production was a cottage industry. A handloom weaver needed four to eight labourers to operate the spinning wheels manually to make yarn. The ‘flying shuttle’ invented by John Kay in 1733 increased the speed of the spinning. In 1764 James Hargreaves invented the spinning jenny, which spun eight yarns at a time.

Two years later, Richard Arkwright invented the ‘water frame’. This spinning frame used water power instead of human power. It was so large that it could not be operated at home. Thus, the factory was born. In 1779 Samuel Crompton combined the spinning jenny and the water frame to invent the spinning mule. It spun hundreds of yarns at a time, and spun both fine and strong yarns. In 1793 Eli Whitney invented the cotton gin, which separately extracted cotton from the seed.

Iron and Steel

The ‘rolling mill’ emerged as a factory for converting iron into sheets and bars. These mills converted iron into sheets fifteen times faster than the hammering of iron by a blacksmith. Hot blast furnaces improved the efficiency of fuel in iron production. In 1856 Henry Bessemer discovered a fast and economical process for manufacturing steel. Over time, iron and steel were used in all industries, and in the manufacture of all machines.

Mining Industry

Not only did James Watt’s improved steam engine and Arkwright’s factory system help, the need for coal also kept increasing. As a result, coal mines were drilled deeper and more dangerous conditions were created. When the workers used oil lamps inside the mines, the chance of explosions increased and caused loss of life. The invention of the safety lamp in 1815 by Sir Humphrey Davy reduced this danger. In England, coal production increased from 4.7 million tons in 1750 to 250 million tons in 1900.

Transport and Communication

The spread of the Industrial Revolution depended on the efficiency of transport facilities. Because of the increase in production, raw materials had to be brought from far away, and then goods had to be taken to the markets. Therefore, new canals, roads, and railways were created. MacAdamised roads and George Stephenson’s steam engine trains improved road and rail transport facilities in England.

John Loudon McAdam was a Scottish civil engineer. The road construction process designed by him changed the road construction process all over the world.

The first railways were built in Greece around 6th century BCE. These were operated by humans or animals. There has been horse-drawn rail transport. Modern rail transport began with the development of steam engines in England in the early 19th century.

The first railway was built in England in 1825, between the cities of Stockton and Darlington. In the next four decades, 15,000 miles were connected by railways.

In America Robert Fulton invented the Clermont steamship in 1807. This was operated between New York and Albany for a distance of 150 miles. In a few years, steam ships were used in rivers and coasts to carry goods. In 1830, the 40 mile distance between the cities of Manchester and Liverpool could be crossed in one and a half hours.

10.2 Effects of the Industrial Revolution in England

The Industrial Revolution in England led to commercial expansion, increase in food production, the emergence of a new class of factory workers, and urbanisation. The emergence and growth of new cities, an organised labour movement, demands for the right to vote by workers, and demands to improve the working conditions of workers in factories created a new force in politics.

Impact on Environment and Living Conditions

The use of chemical and fossil fuels instead of wind, water, and wood led to increased air and water pollution. The Industrial Revolution was a huge turning point in the relationship between the ecology of the earth, the natural environment, and human beings.

The Industrial Revolution helped all the members of a family to get employment. However, the life of the working class was pathetic. As children were given low wages, they were employed in large numbers in textile mills. The British Parliament published a report on the conditions in coal mining in 1842. That report is called the mining report. In the beginning, safety arrangements for workers in the mines and in factories were very poor. Injuries from machines, minor burns, injuries to hands and legs, amputation of fingers, removal of a hand or leg completely, even death occurred.

The workers in Europe lived in very small, dirty, unhygienic houses. The workers, even if they wanted to, did not have the time to clean or change their surroundings. As a result, diseases like typhoid, cholera, and smallpox spread.

Urbanisation

Following the advent of the Industrial Revolution, England became the industrial workshop of the world. There was a general decline in agricultural production. As a result, people started moving from villages to industrial cities. Population growth, migration, and urbanisation are important social changes of the Industrial Revolution. In the pre-Industrial Revolution society, more than 80 percent of the people lived in rural areas. As migration from the rural areas increased, small towns turned into big cities. The population of London was twenty lakhs in 1840, but in forty years it increased to fifty lakhs.

The cold climate in the city of Manchester was conducive to textile production. Further, it was located near the port of Liverpool and the coal mines of Lancashire. Thus, Manchester became the textile capital of the world. This attracted huge numbers of people to the city. Manchester, which was a dull city with only 22,000 people in 1771, grew to a population of 1,80,000 in the next fifty years.

Socio-Economic Consequences

While the farmers remained poor and the workers suffered, the middle class invested more in industry and trade, and grew into a wealthy class. These groups dominated the governments of that time. All laws protected the interests of that class. Workers did not have permission to form labour unions.

It was in this context that socialism emerged as a new philosophy in Europe. To protect the working class from the exploitative policies of the capitalist class, Karl Marx advanced the theory of scientific socialism. In the later part of the 19th century, strong working class movements arose throughout Western Europe seeking economic and political rights.

Labour Movement

The Reform Act was introduced in 1832. It granted franchise only to the middle class, which mainly owned property. The frustrated working class gathered in lakhs in one place and created a charter containing their demands, and obtained the signatures of lakhs of workers. This charter was submitted to the House of Commons of the English Parliament. As the workers movement emphasised the demands contained in this charter, it was known as the ‘Charter Movement’ and those who participated in it were known as the Charter Movement Activists. This movement operated vibrantly from 1836 to 1848. The movement people insisted demands that franchise to everyone over the age of twenty-one, parliamentary elections through secret ballot, removal of property rights in the qualification for members of parliament, elections to parliament every year, and equal representation.

10.3 Spread of Industrial Revolution

Industrial Revolution in France

France did not have as many natural resources as England. The political instability caused by the French Revolution and Napoleon’s long wars had devastated the country. During the revolution, many of France’s merchants had taken refuge in England. When they returned to France after the end of the Napoleonic wars, they brought and used British technologies. This helped to establish the Industrial Revolution in their country. By using spinning spindles made in Britain, France’s textile production increased two-fold in the 1830-1860 period.

Francaise De Wendel brought British technology to Lorraine. His family introduced the steam engine in coal mining and the puddling kiln in iron extraction in France. By 1860, the Wendel family had employed more than ten thousand workers. Through expansion of trade into various sectors, they began other heavy industries such as railway construction and ship building.

The city of Mulhouse in the Alsace province was famous for the machinery-making moulds. As a result, designers from many parts of the world came to this city. Using this foundation, the Mulhouse company expanded into multiple sectors and excelled as a manufacturer of heavy machinery for industries. Advances in iron production occurred in the paint and dye sector. In 1820, the British technology of refining cast iron began to be used in this city.

In 1832, the first French railway was opened between the cities of Saint-Etienne and Andrezieux. Subsequently, countless railways were built in various areas. By the end of the 19th century, France had grown into a pioneer in the production of vehicles. The fact that the two car companies, which still remain famous automobile companies in France, started in 1891 is noteworthy. Armand Peugeot Company produced the first batch of vehicles. In 1898, Louis Renault created a four-wheel vehicle. Subsequently, Renault Freres (Renault Brothers Company) manufactured a large number of vehicles through their company.

In 1806, the percentage of people engaged in agricultural production in France was 65.1 percent. In 1896, this decreased to 42.5 percent. During the same period, the share of jobs in the industrial sector increased from 20.4 percent to 31.4 percent.

Industrial Revolution in Germany

Germany naturally had the natural resources needed for an industrial revolution to develop. Coal deposits were present in the Saar, Ruhr, West Silesia, and Saxony regions. Iron was available in the Erzgebirge, Harz Mountains, and West Silesia areas.

The serfdom that still existed, the social political system based on land ownership, and the strict licensing process followed to start a business were the major challenges for Germany’s progress. Bremen and Hamburg were the two large ports which were safe harbours in the North Sea. But, the major challenge for the Industrial Revolution to occur in Germany was its political system. Before 1871, Germany was composed of numerous German states. Prussia was the largest of these states.

The Prussian governments finally unified together into one Germany in 1871.

Following this, in Germany by the end of the 19th century became a large industrial nation, and in industrialisation helped both in the formation of Germany into one nation, Railway lines were greatly served. the first railway was set up Between Nuremberg and Furth in December 1835. Private companies took the initiative in setting up railway lines. But, when capital shortages arose, the government intervened and helped. In some areas, the work of building railway lines was nationalised. In Prussia, the task of integrating railway lines was undertaken by the government, which partnered with private companies to carry it out. In 1842, a separate fund was created for the railway project, called the Railway Fund. The city of Berlin emerged as the centre integrating the railway lines in Prussia. The member states of the Zollverein Union were connected by railway lines, intensifying commercial activities.

By using the steam engine, the number of Prussian factories, which had been 419 in 1837, increased to 1,444 in 1849. Coal production, which had been ten lakh tons in 1820, rose to more than sixty lakh tons in the next 30 years. Iron production, which had been 46,000 tons in 1810, increased to 5,29,000 tons around 1850. The length of railway lines, which had been 3,638 miles in 1850, increased to 11,600 miles in 1870.

Germany had become the place for the first Industrial Revolution, replacing England and competing with America. Siemens, for example, was given opportunities in the production of electrical equipment. Germany excelled in the production of chemical substances such as potassium salt, dyes, medicines, and artificial fibres. German companies such as Bayer and BASF led in the chemical sector. Germany also became a leading nation in the automotive industry. Daimler and Benz automobiles were very famous all over the world.

10.4 Second Industrial Revolution in America

Moving from manual to machine-based production was the beginning of the American Industrial Revolution.

Samuel Slater of England had worked in textile mills from the age of ten and had experience managing a mill. When he heard that Americans were interested in new technologies, he migrated illegally to New York in America in 1789. He asked for a job from Moses Brown, a famous industrialist of Rhode Island. Brown had already started a textile mill and had failed. As soon as Brown hired Slater, his factory started running again in 1793. It was the first textile mill in the Americas to operate through water power. Around 1800, many more industrialists began to establish textile mills and prosper after seeing Slater’s textile mill. Slater’s technology became more and more famous.

American President Andrew Jackson called Slater the “Father of the American Industrial Revolution”.

In the 19th century, America led in creating new technological inventions. Robert Fulton started steam boat transport on the Hudson River. Samuel F.B. Morse invented the telegraph, and Elias Howe invented the sewing machine, all these happened before the American Civil War (1860–1865).

After the Civil War, industrialisation began to intensify. In 1869, the first railway connecting the continents was built. This was useful for transporting people, transporting goods, and bringing raw materials. As a result, urbanisation and border expansions occurred in America at an unprecedented rate. From 1860 to 1900 alone, 1 crore 40 lakh people migrated to America from various parts of the world and worked in various kinds of factories. The invention of the electric bulb by Thomas Alva Edison (1879) and the invention of the telephone by Alexander Graham Bell (1876) changed the entire world beyond recognition.

Andrew Carnegie began the first large-scale steel production in America. He bought up mines which supplied raw materials for iron, production plants, hot furnaces which made iron sheets, railways which transported the products, and ship transport. John D. Rockefeller established a trust by integrating several large factories. His Standard Oil Trust monopolised 90 percent of the industry and reduced competition. This monopoly affected small businesses and threatened them. The American government also supported industrial growth by providing land for building railways, and by protecting American companies from foreign competition.

The Industrial Revolution quickened America’s transition from a rural society to an urban society. Youths who had grown up on farms moved to cities in search of job opportunities. Similarly, lakhs of people migrated from European countries. Providing housing for these immigrants who had settled in the cities was difficult. These new workers lived in urban slums. Open sewers ran through the streets; drinking water supply was inadequate; therefore, infectious diseases spread.

10.5 Working Class Struggles

The poor working conditions that prevailed in factories, the long working hours, low wages, and the exploitation of women and children led to the formation of labour unions. After the Civil War in America, workers engaged in strikes. Of these, the massive strike by the railway workers in 1877 was a huge event. This strike occurred after wages were cut because of a prolonged economic slowdown. This strike, which began in West Virginia, spread to three more states within 45 days.

This struggle was crushed by the henchmen of capitalists, the national guard, the army, and the hired forces.

The Haymarket Affair

A workers’ rally took place at the Haymarket Square in Chicago on May 4, 1886. In this rally, which had been organised to insist on an eight-hour work day, the police fired gunshots and killed many people. As a way of paying respect to the martyrs of the Haymarket Affair, May 1st is still celebrated as International Workers’ Day (May Day).

10.6 Impact of the Industrial Revolution in India

Till the middle of the 18th century, England was an agricultural country. At the same time, India was known not only for agriculture but also for its excellent products. Laws were enacted in England in the first quarter of the 18th century banning the import of Indian cotton and silk textiles into England. The spinning and weaving industry had developed greatly in England because of the inventions made in the 30 years following the discovery of the flying shuttle by John Kay. The wealth looted in Bengal and Karnataka by the British and provided the investment required for the Industrial Revolution in England and enabled it to succeed. Weavers of Bengal had to face great difficulties at the hands of officials of the East India Company and their agents.

First, the Indian weavers were forced to pay a transportation tax for moving their products from one place to another. Next, they were compelled to cultivate only the commercial crops needed for English factories. The market for handloom cotton textiles declined in India, and India lost its place in the ancient manufacturing sector and became only a country that exported raw materials.

In the first quarter of the 19th century, the export of Dhaka muslin cloth to England was stopped. Further, due to American competition, the export of raw cotton made in India also gradually declined. As cloths produced in British textile factories were sold at low prices in Indian markets, Indian weavers were rendered jobless and lost their livelihoods and were left without food. In a report given by the Madurai Collector, it has been recorded that around 5,000 handloom weaving families were suffering without even getting one meal. The Thirunelveli district collector recorded that weavers of Thirunelveli district suffered as their “livelihoods were destroyed and the caste system did not allow them to engage in other professions”. Many millions of people died from famine. Not wanting to die from famine, lakhs of Indian farmers and artisans left their motherland and were forced to live as bonded labourers under harsh working and living conditions in English colonies’ plantation.
